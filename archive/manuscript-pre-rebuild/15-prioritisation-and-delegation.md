# Chapter 15 — Prioritisation and Delegation

## Clinical Scenario

A 42-year-old man is brought to the resuscitation room following a high-speed road traffic collision.

He is conscious but agitated. His respiratory rate is 32/min, oxygen saturation is 88% on a non-rebreather mask, heart rate is 138/min, blood pressure is 82/48 mmHg, and his skin is cold and clammy.

There is obvious external haemorrhage from the right thigh. The pelvis is painful and unstable on initial assessment. Breath sounds are markedly reduced on the left.

The trauma team is already working.

One clinician is attempting intravenous access. Another is preparing the airway equipment. A nurse is obtaining blood samples. A junior doctor is performing the secondary survey. The radiographer is waiting for instructions. The anaesthetist is asking whether the patient should be intubated immediately.

The trauma team leader has several competing demands.

The patient may have:

- catastrophic haemorrhage
- tension pneumothorax
- traumatic brain injury
- pelvic injury
- femoral fracture
- impending respiratory failure

The leader cannot personally perform every intervention.

The critical task is therefore not to "do more".

It is to determine:

> What must happen first?
>
> What can happen simultaneously?
>
> What can safely wait?
>
> Who should perform each task?
>
> What information will change the priority?

This is the essence of prioritisation and delegation in major trauma.

---

# 1. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain why prioritisation is central to trauma team leadership.
2. Distinguish immediate life threats from important but non-immediate problems.
3. Establish and communicate a clear hierarchy of priorities.
4. Recognise that trauma priorities are dynamic rather than fixed.
5. Use parallel processing without losing overall situational awareness.
6. Delegate tasks clearly and appropriately.
7. Match tasks to the competence and role of individual team members.
8. Use closed-loop communication when delegating critical actions.
9. Avoid overloading individual team members.
10. Reassess priorities as new information becomes available.
11. Manage competing clinical priorities during complex trauma.
12. Recognise common errors in prioritisation and delegation.
13. Apply structured prioritisation during high-pressure trauma scenarios.

---

# 2. Why Prioritisation Matters in Major Trauma

Major trauma generates more problems than the team can address simultaneously in a sequential fashion.

A patient may have multiple injuries, several physiological abnormalities, incomplete information, and rapidly changing priorities.

The trauma team leader must therefore continuously decide:

- what matters most now
- what matters next
- what can occur concurrently
- what can safely be deferred
- what information is needed to make the next decision

The purpose of prioritisation is not simply to create an ordered list.

It is to allocate limited:

- time
- personnel
- equipment
- attention
- blood products
- imaging capacity
- procedural capability
- cognitive bandwidth

to the problems that have the greatest immediate effect on survival.

A useful principle is:

> Treat the problem that is most likely to kill the patient soonest, while simultaneously preventing other immediately lethal problems from being neglected.

This requires judgement.

The trauma team leader should avoid allowing the sequence of the examination to become more important than the patient's physiology.

---

# 3. The Difference Between Assessment Order and Priority

The primary survey provides a structured framework.

It does not mean that every task must be completed sequentially before another task begins.

The trauma team should use a structured assessment while operating in parallel.

For example:

- airway assessment may occur while haemorrhage control is being initiated
- oxygenation may be supported while circulation is assessed
- blood products may be prepared while a chest decompression is being considered
- monitoring may be attached while the team obtains a focused history
- pelvic stabilisation may occur while vascular access is established

The trauma team leader must therefore distinguish:

> **Sequence of thinking**

from:

> **Sequence of action**

A structured survey protects against omission.

Parallel action reduces avoidable delay.

The leader's role is to combine both.

---

# 4. The Fundamental Priority Hierarchy

A practical hierarchy for the trauma team leader is:

1. Immediate threats to life
2. Problems causing current physiological deterioration
3. Problems likely to cause imminent deterioration
4. Interventions that prevent secondary injury
5. Diagnostic clarification that changes immediate management
6. Definitive treatment planning
7. Non-urgent assessment and documentation

This hierarchy is dynamic.

A problem can move from one category to another within seconds.

For example:

A patient with a seemingly stable chest injury may develop sudden hypotension and severe respiratory distress.

The priority changes immediately.

The team leader should be able to say:

> "Our priority has changed."

This explicit statement helps the entire team reorient.

---

# 5. The Three Questions of Prioritisation

When faced with competing tasks, the trauma team leader can ask three questions.

## Question 1 — What can kill the patient now?

Examples include:

- uncontrolled catastrophic haemorrhage
- airway obstruction
- severe hypoxia
- tension pneumothorax
- profound traumatic shock
- cardiac arrest

These problems require immediate attention.

---

## Question 2 — What will kill the patient if we do not act soon?

Examples include:

- ongoing internal haemorrhage
- evolving airway compromise
- worsening respiratory failure
- severe hypothermia
- coagulopathy
- traumatic brain injury with deterioration
- pelvic haemorrhage

These require active management while immediate threats are being addressed.

---

## Question 3 — What can safely wait?

Examples may include:

- detailed wound description
- complete neurological examination in an unstable patient
- full medication history
- non-essential imaging
- definitive fracture management
- extensive documentation during active resuscitation

The ability to deliberately defer a task is an important leadership skill.

Deferring something is not forgetting it.

It is prioritising.

---

# 6. Recognition, Prioritisation and Action

A useful mental model is:

> Recognition → Prioritisation → Intervention → Reassessment

The cycle then repeats.

## Recognition

Identify the abnormality.

## Prioritisation

Determine its immediate clinical importance.

## Intervention

Assign an appropriate action to an appropriate team member.

## Reassessment

Determine whether the intervention produced the expected response.

This prevents the team from treating an intervention as the endpoint.

A chest drain is not the endpoint.

Improvement in respiratory mechanics, oxygenation and haemodynamics is the clinically relevant outcome.

Similarly:

Blood products being administered is not the endpoint.

The response to haemorrhage control and resuscitation is what matters.

---

# 7. Prioritisation Is Dynamic

Trauma priorities can change rapidly.

A patient may move through several different priority states during the same resuscitation.

For example:

### Phase 1

The initial priority is catastrophic external haemorrhage.

### Phase 2

After haemorrhage control, severe respiratory compromise becomes dominant.

### Phase 3

After chest decompression, persistent shock raises concern for ongoing internal bleeding.

### Phase 4

After haemorrhage control, traumatic brain injury becomes a major concern.

The leader must therefore avoid anchoring on the original problem.

The question is not:

> "What was the priority?"

The question is:

> "What is the priority now?"

---

# 8. The Priority Can Change After Every Intervention

Every major intervention should trigger reassessment.

Examples:

- after airway intervention
- after chest decompression
- after haemorrhage control
- after pelvic stabilisation
- after blood product administration
- after intubation
- after a change in neurological status

The leader should ask:

> "What has changed?"

Then:

> "What is now the greatest threat?"

This creates a continuous feedback loop.

---

# 9. Parallel Processing

One of the most important differences between an effective trauma team and an ineffective one is the ability to process multiple problems in parallel.

A poorly coordinated team may behave like this:

1. assess airway
2. finish airway assessment
3. assess breathing
4. finish breathing assessment
5. assess circulation
6. obtain intravenous access
7. start fluids
8. then consider haemorrhage

A coordinated team may instead operate like this:

- airway clinician assesses and manages the airway
- another clinician assesses breathing
- a nurse applies monitoring
- another clinician controls external haemorrhage
- blood samples are obtained
- blood products are prepared
- the team leader integrates all information

The leader must coordinate these activities without allowing parallel processing to become chaotic processing.

---

# 10. Parallel Processing Requires a Leader

Parallel processing does not mean:

> "Everyone do whatever they think is necessary."

It means:

> "Different team members perform defined tasks simultaneously under a shared plan."

The leader must maintain a mental model of:

- what is happening
- who is doing it
- what has been completed
- what remains outstanding
- what response is expected
- what decision comes next

Without leadership, parallel processing can result in duplication and omission.

---

# 11. The Trauma Team Leader as the Priority Manager

The leader should not attempt to personally perform every clinical intervention.

Instead, the leader should maintain oversight.

A useful question is:

> "What is the most important thing that nobody is currently doing?"

This question can reveal dangerous gaps.

For example:

- everyone is assessing the patient but nobody is controlling haemorrhage
- several people are obtaining access but nobody has activated the massive haemorrhage protocol
- the airway is being prepared but oxygenation is not being monitored
- imaging is being arranged while the patient remains physiologically unstable

The leader must identify these gaps early.

---

# 12. Delegation Is a Clinical Intervention

Delegation is not an administrative activity.

In trauma resuscitation, effective delegation directly affects patient safety.

Poor delegation creates:

- delay
- ambiguity
- duplication
- missed tasks
- loss of situational awareness
- increased cognitive load

Good delegation creates:

- clarity
- ownership
- accountability
- parallel processing
- faster intervention
- better team coordination

The task should have an owner.

If everyone is responsible, nobody may feel specifically responsible.

---

# 13. The Five Elements of Effective Delegation

A critical task should ideally include five elements:

1. **Who**
2. **What**
3. **When**
4. **Expected result**
5. **Confirmation**

For example:

> "Dr Ahmed, insert a second large-bore IV now and tell me when blood can be connected."

This identifies:

- who: Dr Ahmed
- what: second large-bore IV
- when: now
- expected result: access ready for blood
- confirmation: report back

---

# 14. Delegate to a Person, Not to the Room

Avoid vague instructions.

Poor:

> "Someone get another IV."

Better:

> "Dr Ahmed, obtain a second large-bore IV."

Poor:

> "Can we get blood?"

Better:

> "Nurse Ali, activate the massive haemorrhage protocol and confirm when blood products are on the way."

Poor:

> "We need a chest drain."

Better:

> "Dr Sara, prepare for left chest decompression and tell me when you are ready."

Specific delegation creates ownership.

---

# 15. Closed-Loop Delegation

Critical tasks should use closed-loop communication.

The sequence is:

> Instruction → Acknowledgement → Action → Completion confirmation

Example:

**Leader:**

> "Nurse, activate the massive haemorrhage protocol."

**Nurse:**

> "Activating the massive haemorrhage protocol."

**Leader:**

> "Thank you."

Later:

**Nurse:**

> "Massive haemorrhage protocol activated. Blood is being prepared."

This confirms that the instruction was understood and completed.

---

# 16. Delegation Should Match Competence

The leader should know the capability of available team members.

The appropriate task depends on:

- training
- experience
- scope of practice
- procedural competence
- current workload
- clinical context

The most senior person is not automatically the correct person for every task.

A junior clinician may appropriately:

- obtain vascular access
- draw blood
- perform basic procedures
- document interventions
- perform focused assessments

An experienced clinician may be better positioned to:

- manage a difficult airway
- perform thoracostomy
- coordinate haemorrhage control
- interpret complex findings
- make major disposition decisions

The leader must allocate tasks according to competence and urgency.

---

# 17. Do Not Delegate Away Critical Decisions

Delegation does not mean transferring leadership responsibility.

The trauma team leader can delegate:

- procedures
- assessments
- preparation
- communication
- documentation
- equipment acquisition

The leader should retain responsibility for:

- overall priorities
- major treatment decisions
- escalation
- disposition
- reassessment
- coordination

The leader may ask for expert input.

The leader should not disappear into an individual task and lose oversight.

---

# 18. Task Allocation Should Be Visible

Whenever possible, task allocation should be explicit.

For example:

> "Airway is with anaesthesia."

> "Breathing assessment is with Dr Omar."

> "Nurse Fatima is managing access and blood products."

> "Dr Khalid is performing the secondary survey."

> "I am coordinating the resuscitation and making the destination decision."

This creates a shared map of responsibility.

---

# 19. Avoid Duplication

Duplication is common when tasks are not explicitly allocated.

Two clinicians may:

- obtain the same history
- prepare the same equipment
- attempt the same procedure
- call the same service
- repeat the same assessment

Meanwhile another critical task remains undone.

The leader should periodically ask:

> "Who is doing what?"

and:

> "What remains outstanding?"

---

# 20. Avoid Task Overload

A single highly capable team member can become a bottleneck.

For example, asking one clinician to:

- obtain access
- draw blood
- administer medication
- document
- communicate with radiology
- prepare equipment

may appear efficient but can become unsafe.

Delegation should distribute workload according to capacity.

A useful principle is:

> Do not delegate according to who is available alone; delegate according to who is available and capable.

---

# 21. Prioritisation During Catastrophic Haemorrhage

Haemorrhage often creates simultaneous priorities.

The leader may need to coordinate:

- direct pressure
- tourniquet application where appropriate
- pelvic stabilisation when indicated
- vascular access
- blood product activation
- warming
- calcium monitoring and replacement when clinically indicated
- antifibrinolytic therapy where appropriate
- definitive haemorrhage control
- surgical or interventional radiology involvement
- transfer planning

The leader should avoid becoming distracted by secondary injuries.

The central question is:

> "What is controlling the bleeding?"

If the answer is unclear, the priority remains haemorrhage control.

---

# 22. Avoid the "Access First" Trap

Obtaining vascular access is important.

However:

> Access is a means to resuscitation, not the endpoint.

If a patient has uncontrolled external haemorrhage, the team should not spend excessive time obtaining multiple peripheral lines while the bleeding continues.

If peripheral access is difficult, the team should escalate appropriately according to the patient's condition and local protocols.

The leader should continuously ask:

> "Is our current activity actually improving the patient's chance of survival?"

---

# 23. Prioritisation During Airway Crisis

A patient with traumatic airway compromise may require immediate intervention.

At the same time, the patient may have:

- cervical spine injury
- haemorrhage
- hypoxia
- facial trauma
- aspiration risk
- traumatic brain injury

The leader must coordinate airway management while ensuring that other immediately lethal threats are not neglected.

Delegation may include:

- airway operator
- medication preparation
- suction
- oxygenation
- cervical spine support
- monitoring
- backup airway equipment
- surgical airway preparation
- haemorrhage control

The airway team should not be left to manage the entire room alone.

---

# 24. Prioritisation During Chest Trauma

Chest trauma may create several competing problems.

Examples include:

- tension pneumothorax
- massive haemothorax
- open chest wound
- pulmonary contusion
- cardiac tamponade
- airway-related hypoxia

The leader must determine whether the patient's physiology supports immediate intervention.

The presence of an abnormality on imaging does not automatically make it the highest priority.

The clinical state matters.

---

# 25. Prioritisation During Traumatic Brain Injury

Traumatic brain injury introduces an additional principle:

> Prevent secondary brain injury while identifying and treating immediately reversible causes of deterioration.

Important physiological threats include:

- hypoxia
- hypotension
- airway compromise
- seizures
- hypothermia
- abnormal ventilation
- raised intracranial pressure in appropriate contexts

A patient with severe head injury and haemorrhagic shock may require simultaneous management of both.

The leader should not allow the head injury to distract from uncontrolled haemorrhage.

Nor should haemorrhage management result in prolonged neglect of airway and oxygenation.

---

# 26. Prioritisation During Pelvic Trauma

A patient with suspected pelvic injury and shock may require:

- haemorrhage control
- pelvic stabilisation when indicated
- blood product resuscitation
- vascular access
- imaging when appropriate
- early involvement of definitive treatment teams

The leader should avoid repeated unnecessary pelvic manipulation.

The priority is recognition and appropriate haemorrhage control rather than repeated examination.

---

# 27. Prioritisation During Paediatric Trauma

Children can compensate physiologically before sudden deterioration.

This makes trend recognition particularly important.

Priorities may include:

- airway and oxygenation
- ventilation
- haemorrhage control
- age-appropriate vascular access
- weight-based medication and resuscitation planning
- prevention of hypothermia
- early recognition of deterioration

Delegation must be especially clear because paediatric trauma often generates additional cognitive demands.

The leader should ensure that:

- weight or estimated weight is available
- equipment is appropriately sized
- drug doses are checked
- blood products and resuscitation strategy are coordinated
- safeguarding considerations are not forgotten when clinically relevant

---

# 28. Prioritisation During Pregnancy and Trauma

Pregnancy creates two overlapping considerations:

1. maternal resuscitation
2. fetal considerations

The fundamental principle is:

> Optimal maternal resuscitation is central to fetal survival.

The leader must coordinate appropriate maternal trauma care while ensuring that obstetric expertise is involved when indicated.

Additional considerations may include:

- gestational age
- uterine displacement where appropriate
- fetal assessment when appropriate
- obstetric consultation
- maternal haemorrhage
- placental injury
- preparation for emergency delivery in appropriate circumstances

The leader should not allow fetal assessment to delay life-saving maternal interventions.

---

# 29. Prioritisation and Diagnostic Testing

Diagnostic tests should answer clinically relevant questions.

The leader should ask:

> "Will the result change what we do now?"

If the answer is no, the test may not be an immediate priority.

If the answer is yes, the test may be highly valuable.

Examples include:

- bedside ultrasound in selected unstable patients
- chest imaging when it changes immediate management
- CT in a sufficiently stable patient when it will define management

The important distinction is:

> Diagnostic information should support decision-making, not replace it.

---

# 30. CT Is Not a Priority in Isolation

A CT scanner may be available.

That does not mean the patient is ready for CT.

Before leaving the resuscitation environment, the leader should consider:

- airway security
- ventilation
- haemodynamic stability
- haemorrhage control
- monitoring
- vascular access
- blood availability
- escort personnel
- ability to manage deterioration during transfer

The decision is not:

> "Can we get a CT?"

It is:

> "Is CT the safest and most useful next step for this patient?"

---

# 31. Prioritisation and Definitive Treatment

A trauma patient may need:

- operating theatre
- interventional radiology
- endoscopy
- orthopaedic intervention
- neurosurgical intervention
- transfer to a higher-level trauma centre

The trauma team leader should begin thinking about definitive destination early.

However, planning definitive care should not distract from immediate resuscitation.

A useful principle is:

> Resuscitation and disposition planning should occur in parallel.

---

# 32. Delegation and Time-Critical Communication

When a decision is time-sensitive, the leader should make the instruction explicit.

Examples:

> "We need blood now."

> "Prepare for immediate chest decompression."

> "Call the operating theatre now."

> "Activate the massive haemorrhage protocol."

> "Prepare the patient for transfer."

Avoid vague language such as:

> "Maybe we should think about..."

In an emergency, ambiguity creates delay.

---

# 33. The Leader Should Announce Priority Changes

When priorities change, say so.

For example:

> "The airway is currently adequate. Our priority is haemorrhage control."

Later:

> "The bleeding is controlled. Persistent hypotension is now the main concern."

Later:

> "The patient is responding. We can now proceed to definitive imaging."

This creates a shared mental model.

---

# 34. The One-Minute Reorientation

During a prolonged or complicated resuscitation, the leader can briefly pause the team.

For example:

> "Team, quick update."

Then state:

1. current physiological status
2. major injuries identified
3. current priority
4. active interventions
5. next decision

This should be brief.

The purpose is not to stop resuscitation.

It is to restore shared situational awareness.

---

# 35. The "What Are We Waiting For?" Question

A powerful leadership question is:

> "What are we waiting for?"

Possible answers:

- blood
- airway equipment
- imaging
- consultant
- theatre
- interventional radiology
- transport
- laboratory result

Once identified, the leader can ask:

> "Can anything useful happen while we wait?"

This converts passive waiting into active parallel processing.

---

# 36. The "What Could We Be Missing?" Question

After immediate threats are addressed, ask:

> "What could still kill this patient?"

This helps prevent premature closure.

Possible answers may include:

- occult haemorrhage
- evolving airway compromise
- missed chest injury
- traumatic brain injury
- spinal injury
- hypothermia
- coagulopathy

The leader should keep the differential diagnosis alive until the clinical picture becomes clearer.

---

# 37. Delegation During Reassessment

Reassessment itself can be delegated.

For example:

> "Dr Ahmed, repeat the airway and breathing assessment after decompression and tell me the oxygen saturation and respiratory findings."

This is more useful than:

> "Keep an eye on him."

Specific reassessment creates measurable feedback.

---

# 38. Delegation of Monitoring

Monitoring should have clear ownership.

Tasks may include:

- continuous ECG monitoring
- blood pressure measurement
- oxygen saturation
- temperature
- urine output
- blood gas analysis
- repeated haemoglobin assessment where appropriate
- response to blood products
- neurological reassessment

The leader should know who is responsible for communicating significant changes.

---

# 39. Delegation of Documentation

Documentation is important but should not compete with immediate life-saving interventions.

During active resuscitation, documentation should capture:

- times
- major interventions
- medications
- blood products
- physiological changes
- procedures
- major decisions
- communication with receiving teams

A designated team member should document whenever possible.

The trauma team leader should not become the primary scribe unless circumstances require it.

---

# 40. Delegation of External Communication

Communication outside the resuscitation room may include:

- blood bank
- operating theatre
- radiology
- interventional radiology
- intensive care
- specialist services
- retrieval teams
- receiving trauma centre

These tasks can often be delegated.

For example:

> "Dr Sara, contact the operating theatre and tell them we have an unstable patient with suspected ongoing intra-abdominal haemorrhage. Confirm the expected theatre readiness."

This allows the leader to maintain clinical oversight.

---

# 41. Escalation Should Be Delegated Early

Do not wait until the patient has deteriorated completely before involving the appropriate senior or specialist team.

If the likely destination is:

- operating theatre
- interventional radiology
- neurosurgery
- vascular surgery
- paediatric surgery
- obstetrics

early communication may reduce delay.

Delegation should include the urgency.

For example:

> "Please contact neurosurgery now and tell them this is an unstable patient with severe traumatic brain injury."

Not:

> "Can you call neurosurgery?"

---

# 42. Prioritisation Under Uncertainty

Trauma decisions are often made with incomplete information.

The leader may not know:

- the exact injury
- the source of bleeding
- the full medical history
- the final diagnosis
- the response to treatment

Waiting for certainty can be dangerous.

A practical approach is:

> Act on the most dangerous plausible diagnosis when delay carries significant risk.

At the same time, avoid unnecessary intervention based solely on speculation.

The leader must balance:

- probability
- severity
- reversibility
- time sensitivity
- risk of intervention

---

# 43. The Cost of Delay

A useful way to prioritise is to consider the cost of waiting.

A problem deserves immediate attention when:

- deterioration is rapid
- the intervention is time-sensitive
- delay causes irreversible harm
- treatment is relatively straightforward
- the diagnosis is sufficiently likely

This is why catastrophic haemorrhage, airway obstruction and severe hypoxia receive immediate attention.

---

# 44. The Cost of Distraction

Every task consumes attention.

The leader should ask:

> "If I focus on this problem, what am I no longer watching?"

This is particularly important when the leader becomes involved in a procedure.

A procedure may be necessary.

But if performing it causes the leader to lose oversight of the entire patient, another team member may need to take over the leadership role temporarily.

---

# 45. When the Leader Should Step Into a Procedure

The leader may need to perform a procedure when:

- no appropriately skilled clinician is available
- immediate intervention is required
- the procedure is within the leader's competence
- delay would be dangerous

If the leader must perform the procedure, leadership functions should be deliberately transferred or shared.

For example:

> "I am going to perform the chest decompression. Dr Ahmed, maintain oversight of the room and tell me immediately if the blood pressure or oxygenation changes."

This prevents leadership blindness.

---

# 46. The Leader Should Avoid Becoming the Bottleneck

A common failure mode is:

> "I will do it myself because it is faster."

Sometimes this is true.

Repeatedly doing this can create a bottleneck.

The leader may become responsible for:

- airway
- vascular access
- procedures
- medication decisions
- communication
- imaging
- documentation

The rest of the team then waits for the leader.

This reverses the intended structure.

The leader should make the team faster, not become the fastest individual clinician in the room.

---

# 47. Delegation Does Not Mean Abdication

Effective delegation requires supervision.

The leader should remain aware of:

- task progress
- complications
- completion
- patient response

Delegating a task does not mean forgetting it.

For example:

> "Insert the arterial line and let me know when it is functioning."

The leader does not need to perform the arterial line.

But the leader should know whether it was successful.

---

# 48. Prioritisation of Multiple Injuries

A patient may have:

- head injury
- chest trauma
- abdominal injury
- pelvic injury
- limb injury

The presence of many injuries should not result in equal attention to all injuries.

Prioritise according to:

1. physiological threat
2. time sensitivity
3. reversibility
4. expected benefit of intervention
5. resources required

A fractured limb may be significant.

Uncontrolled haemorrhage is usually more immediately important.

---

# 49. The Principle of "Life Before Anatomy"

The trauma team leader should avoid allowing anatomical complexity to distract from physiology.

A dramatic injury may not be the most dangerous injury.

A relatively subtle finding may represent catastrophic internal pathology.

The leader should therefore continuously integrate:

> Mechanism + anatomy + physiology + response to treatment

rather than relying on visual appearance alone.

---

# 50. Response to Treatment Is Diagnostic Information

Treatment response can help refine priorities.

For example:

Persistent shock despite appropriate initial resuscitation should trigger reconsideration of:

- ongoing haemorrhage
- inadequate source control
- obstructive causes
- cardiac causes
- other physiological contributors

Similarly:

Persistent hypoxia despite oxygen therapy should prompt reassessment of:

- airway
- ventilation
- chest pathology
- equipment
- circulation

The leader should not simply repeat the same intervention.

---

# 51. The "No Improvement" Trigger

A useful rule is:

> If the patient does not improve as expected, reassess the diagnosis and priority.

Do not assume:

> "We just need more of the same."

Instead ask:

- Was the diagnosis correct?
- Was the intervention performed correctly?
- Was the intervention sufficient?
- Is there another problem?
- Is the original problem still active?

---

# 52. Prioritisation During Deterioration

When a patient suddenly deteriorates:

1. Announce the deterioration.
2. Reassess immediate life threats.
3. Identify the most likely reversible cause.
4. Allocate tasks.
5. Treat immediately.
6. Reassess response.
7. Re-establish the overall plan.

The leader should avoid allowing the team to fragment into multiple unrelated investigations.

---

# 53. Avoiding the "Everything Is Urgent" Problem

If everything is labelled urgent, nothing has a meaningful priority.

The leader should differentiate:

### Immediate

Must happen now.

### Urgent

Must happen very soon.

### Important

Should happen, but can wait while immediate threats are addressed.

### Deferred

Can safely wait until later.

This simple classification can reduce cognitive overload.

---

# 54. The Priority Ladder

A useful practical ladder is:

**1 — Do now**

Life-saving intervention.

**2 — Do concurrently**

Important action that can occur while another intervention is underway.

**3 — Do next**

Necessary but not immediately time-critical.

**4 — Do later**

Can safely be deferred.

**5 — Do not do now**

Low-value activity that distracts from resuscitation.

The last category is often overlooked.

Knowing what not to do is part of leadership.

---

# 55. Common Prioritisation Errors

## Error 1 — Following the checklist mechanically

A structured approach is helpful.

Mechanical behaviour is dangerous.

---

## Error 2 — Treating every injury equally

Not every injury has equal immediate significance.

---

## Error 3 — Focusing on the most obvious injury

The most visible injury may not be the most lethal.

---

## Error 4 — Waiting for diagnostic certainty

Time-critical treatment may need to occur before definitive diagnosis.

---

## Error 5 — Failing to reassess

The patient's priority may change.

---

## Error 6 — Doing everything personally

This creates bottlenecks.

---

## Error 7 — Vague delegation

Nobody knows who owns the task.

---

## Error 8 — Delegating without confirmation

The leader assumes the task happened.

---

## Error 9 — Overloading one team member

Workload becomes unsafe.

---

## Error 10 — Forgetting the next destination

The team may resuscitate indefinitely without progressing toward definitive care.

---

# 56. Common Delegation Errors

### "Someone do this."

No ownership.

### "Can we get blood?"

No named person.

### "Prepare the airway."

No indication of urgency or role.

### "Call surgery."

No information about the problem or urgency.

### "Keep an eye on the patient."

No measurable task.

Better delegation specifies:

- person
- task
- urgency
- expected result
- feedback

# 57. Case-Based Discussion 1 — Unstable Chest Trauma

A patient arrives after blunt trauma.

He is hypotensive, tachycardic and severely hypoxic.

Breath sounds are markedly reduced on one side.

At the same time, there is active external bleeding from the leg.

### Poor leadership

The leader spends several minutes completing a detailed secondary survey.

### Better leadership

The leader identifies two simultaneous priorities:

- immediate chest threat
- active haemorrhage

Tasks are delegated in parallel.

One clinician manages the chest emergency.

Another controls the leg haemorrhage.

Another prepares blood products.

The leader maintains overall coordination and reassesses response.

### Lesson

Parallel processing allows more than one immediately lethal problem to be addressed without sacrificing leadership oversight.

---

# 58. Case-Based Discussion 2 — Difficult IV Access

A hypotensive trauma patient has one small peripheral IV.

A junior doctor has spent several minutes attempting another peripheral line.

The patient remains unstable.

### Leadership response

The leader should ask:

> "What are our access options?"

Then allocate the most appropriate available method according to the patient's condition, local protocols and team competence.

The leader should not allow repeated unsuccessful attempts to consume excessive time.

### Lesson

The goal is resuscitation, not perfect peripheral access.

---

# 59. Case-Based Discussion 3 — CT Request

A patient has multiple injuries but remains haemodynamically unstable.

The CT scanner is immediately available.

The radiologist calls asking for the patient.

### Leadership response

The leader assesses whether the patient is sufficiently stable and whether CT will change immediate management.

If immediate haemorrhage control is required, definitive treatment may take priority over CT.

### Lesson

Availability does not determine priority.

Clinical need does.

---

# 60. Case-Based Discussion 4 — The Overloaded Senior Nurse

During a major trauma activation, the senior nurse is simultaneously:

- managing access
- preparing blood
- documenting
- administering medication
- answering telephone calls

Several tasks are delayed.

### Leadership response

The leader redistributes workload.

One person owns blood products.

Another owns documentation.

Another manages medications.

External calls are delegated.

### Lesson

High-performing individuals can become unsafe bottlenecks when overloaded.

---

# 61. Case-Based Discussion 5 — Sudden Deterioration

A patient initially responds to resuscitation but suddenly becomes hypotensive again.

The team continues giving fluid.

### Leadership response

The leader interrupts the pattern:

> "Stop. We have new deterioration."

Then reassesses:

- haemorrhage
- airway
- breathing
- circulation
- intervention effectiveness

The team looks for a new or persistent cause.

### Lesson

Failure to respond should trigger reassessment, not automatic repetition.

---

# 62. Case-Based Discussion 6 — Multiple Specialists

A trauma patient has suspected:

- abdominal bleeding
- pelvic injury
- head injury
- limb ischaemia

Several specialists arrive simultaneously.

Each wants immediate attention.

### Leadership response

The trauma team leader retains overall prioritisation.

Specialist opinions are valuable, but the leader must integrate them into the patient's immediate physiological priorities.

### Lesson

Multiple experts do not remove the need for a single coordinating leader.

---

# 63. The Trauma Team Leader's Practical Delegation Script

A useful structure is:

> "Name — task — urgency — expected result — report back."

Example:

> "Dr Ahmed — obtain large-bore access — now — suitable for rapid blood administration — tell me when it is functioning."

Another example:

> "Nurse Fatima — activate the massive haemorrhage protocol — now — blood products requested — confirm when activated."

Another:

> "Dr Sara — repeat the neurological assessment after resuscitation — in five minutes — report GCS and pupil findings."

---

# 64. The Trauma Team Leader's Priority Script

When the room becomes chaotic, the leader can say:

> "Team, our immediate priority is..."

Then:

> "At the same time..."

Then:

> "After that..."

Then:

> "I want reassessment when..."

For example:

> "Our immediate priority is haemorrhage control. At the same time, anaesthesia will manage oxygenation and airway preparation. Blood products are being activated. Once haemorrhage control is achieved, we will reassess physiology and decide whether the patient goes directly to theatre or requires further imaging."

This creates a shared plan.

---

# 65. Delegation During a Major Haemorrhage

A structured allocation may look like:

### Team leader

- overall priorities
- clinical integration
- escalation
- destination

### Airway clinician

- airway
- ventilation
- oxygenation

### Procedure clinician

- haemorrhage-control procedures
- access
- invasive procedures

### Nurse team

- monitoring
- medications
- blood products
- equipment

### Scribe

- timeline
- interventions
- blood products
- key decisions

### Runner / coordinator

- blood bank
- theatre
- imaging
- external communication

Roles vary according to local staffing.

The principle remains:

> Clear ownership with central coordination.

---

# 66. Prioritisation and Human Factors

Prioritisation is strongly influenced by human factors.

Under pressure, clinicians may:

- anchor on the first diagnosis
- focus on familiar tasks
- continue an ineffective intervention
- follow the most vocal team member
- become fixated on one procedure
- lose awareness of the whole room

The leader should actively counter these tendencies.

---

# 67. The Leader's Cognitive Guardrails

Useful internal questions include:

> What can kill this patient now?

> What are we treating?

> Is the treatment working?

> What are we waiting for?

> What is nobody doing?

> What has changed?

> What could we be missing?

> Where is this patient going?

These questions act as cognitive guardrails.

---

# 68. The "Stop and Reframe" Technique

If the resuscitation becomes chaotic, the leader can deliberately reframe the situation.

For example:

> "Let's pause for a ten-second update."

Then:

- state current physiology
- identify current priority
- allocate tasks
- identify next decision

This does not mean stopping treatment.

It means stopping uncoordinated activity.

---

# 69. Prioritisation During Prolonged Resuscitation

A prolonged trauma resuscitation can create fatigue and loss of structure.

The leader should periodically reassess:

- physiology
- bleeding control
- ventilation
- temperature
- metabolic status
- blood product requirements
- medication needs
- imaging
- definitive treatment
- transfer needs

The priority should not remain frozen because the team has been working on the same problem for thirty minutes.

---

# 70. When Priorities Conflict

Sometimes two important interventions compete for the same resource.

For example:

- one clinician is required for an airway procedure
- the same clinician is needed for another critical intervention

The leader should ask:

1. Which problem is more immediately lethal?
2. Can another person perform one task?
3. Can the tasks be performed sequentially?
4. Can additional help be obtained?
5. What is the consequence of delay?

This is a resource-allocation problem.

---

# 71. Delegation and Escalation

If the team lacks sufficient capability, the leader should escalate.

Examples include:

- difficult airway
- uncontrolled haemorrhage
- complex vascular injury
- severe traumatic brain injury
- paediatric major trauma
- pregnancy-related trauma
- need for operative or interventional control

Escalation is not failure.

Failure is recognising the need for help but delaying the call.

---

# 72. The Leader's Relationship With Experts

The trauma team leader should welcome specialist input.

The leader should ask:

> "What do you think is the immediate threat?"

and:

> "What do you need from the team now?"

This converts specialist expertise into actionable information.

The leader remains responsible for integrating the recommendations with the overall resuscitation.

---

# 73. The "One Team, One Plan" Principle

Complex trauma may involve:

- emergency medicine
- surgery
- anaesthesia
- radiology
- orthopaedics
- neurosurgery
- nursing
- blood bank
- intensive care

Each discipline may have different priorities.

The trauma team leader should establish:

> One patient.
>
> One shared clinical picture.
>
> One immediate priority.
>
> One coordinated plan.

---

# 74. Practical Prioritisation Checklist

During resuscitation, ask:

### Immediate threat

- What can kill the patient now?

### Physiology

- Is the patient improving or deteriorating?

### Haemorrhage

- Is bleeding controlled?

### Airway

- Is the airway secure?

### Breathing

- Is oxygenation and ventilation adequate?

### Circulation

- Is perfusion adequate?

### Neurology

- Is neurological status changing?

### Environment

- Is hypothermia being prevented?

### Parallel tasks

- What can happen simultaneously?

### Delegation

- Does every critical task have an owner?

### Reassessment

- When will the response be checked?

### Destination

- Where does the patient need to go next?

---

# 75. The 30-Second Leadership Reset

When overwhelmed, use:

## 1. LOOK

What is happening to the patient?

## 2. IDENTIFY

What is the greatest immediate threat?

## 3. ASSIGN

Who is doing what?

## 4. ACT

What intervention is required now?

## 5. REASSESS

Did the patient improve?

## 6. PLAN

What happens next?

This can restore structure rapidly.

---

# 76. The Principle of "Right Task, Right Person, Right Time"

Effective trauma leadership can be summarised as:

> Right task.
>
> Right person.
>
> Right time.
>
> Right priority.
>
> Right feedback.

A task performed by the wrong person can be unsafe.

A correct task performed too late can be ineffective.

A correct intervention without reassessment may be incomplete.

---

# 77. Key Takeaways

1. Prioritisation is one of the central functions of the trauma team leader.
2. The leader must distinguish immediate threats from problems that can safely wait.
3. Trauma priorities are dynamic.
4. The sequence of assessment does not require sequential treatment.
5. Parallel processing is essential in complex trauma.
6. Parallel processing requires clear leadership.
7. Every critical task should have an owner.
8. Delegation should be specific.
9. Critical tasks should use closed-loop communication.
10. Delegation should match competence and workload.
11. The leader should not become the procedural bottleneck.
12. Delegation does not remove the leader's responsibility for oversight.
13. Lack of expected improvement should trigger reassessment.
14. Diagnostic tests should support clinical decisions rather than delay necessary treatment.
15. Definitive treatment and disposition planning should begin early.
16. The leader should explicitly announce changes in priority.
17. The question "What is nobody doing?" can identify dangerous gaps.
18. The question "What are we waiting for?" can identify avoidable delays.
19. When the room becomes chaotic, a brief leadership reset can restore coordination.
20. The goal is not for the leader to do everything.
21. The goal is for the team to do the right things, in the right order, at the right time.

---

# 78. Trauma Team Leader Cognitive Model

A practical cognitive model is:

> **Recognise**
>
> ↓
>
> **Prioritise**
>
> ↓
>
> **Delegate**
>
> ↓
>
> **Intervene**
>
> ↓
>
> **Reassess**
>
> ↓
>
> **Re-prioritise**
>
> ↓
>
> **Definitive treatment**

This cycle continues throughout the resuscitation.

---

# 79. Final Perspective

The trauma team leader's greatest contribution is rarely a single technical procedure.

It is the ability to maintain a coherent clinical picture while multiple problems evolve simultaneously.

An effective leader knows:

- what must happen now
- what can happen in parallel
- what can wait
- who should perform each task
- when to escalate
- when to change direction
- when the current strategy is failing
- when the patient is ready to move beyond the resuscitation room

The leader creates order without slowing the team.

The leader creates urgency without creating panic.

The leader delegates without abandoning responsibility.

The leader reassesses without losing momentum.

Ultimately:

> **Prioritisation determines what matters most.**
>
> **Delegation determines who makes it happen.**
>
> **Reassessment determines whether it worked.**
>
> **Leadership connects all three.**

# Summary

The trauma team leader manages two simultaneous processes:

**Clinical prioritisation**

and

**Team coordination.**

The leader must continuously ask:

> What is the greatest threat?

> What must happen now?

> What can happen simultaneously?

> Who should do it?

> Has it worked?

> What is the next priority?

The quality of trauma leadership is therefore measured not by how many tasks the leader personally performs, but by how effectively the entire team identifies, prioritises, delegates, executes and reassesses the interventions that matter most.
