# Chapter 12 — The Leader's Mental Model

## The Leader's Mental Model

> The trauma team leader does not need to know everything at every moment.
> The leader needs to know what matters now, what may matter next,
> and what must not be missed.

---

## Clinical Scenario

A 32-year-old man is brought to the resuscitation room after a high-speed road traffic collision.

The pre-alert reports:

- High-speed collision
- Significant vehicle intrusion
- Brief loss of consciousness
- Suspected chest injury
- Suspected pelvic injury
- Hypotension at the scene
- Two large-bore intravenous cannulas inserted
- Ongoing tachycardia

The trauma team is assembled before arrival.

As the patient enters the room, multiple things happen simultaneously.

The airway clinician reports:

> "Airway currently patent."

The breathing clinician reports:

> "Reduced air entry on the left."

The circulation clinician reports:

> "Blood pressure 82 systolic."

The nurse reports:

> "I have no blood available yet."

The pelvis feels unstable.

The monitor alarms.

Someone asks whether the patient should go directly to CT.

Another team member asks whether a chest drain should be inserted.

The radiographer asks whether a portable chest and pelvis radiograph are required.

The trauma team leader is now receiving information from several directions at once.

The problem is no longer simply clinical knowledge.

The problem is **mental organisation**.

The leader must construct a coherent picture from incomplete, rapidly changing information.

The leader must distinguish:

- What is immediately life-threatening
- What requires intervention now
- What can safely wait
- What information is still missing
- What decision is approaching
- Where the team is heading
- Whether the current plan is still appropriate

This is the essence of the trauma team leader's mental model.

---

# Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Define a mental model in the context of trauma leadership.
2. Explain why mental models are important during major trauma resuscitation.
3. Construct a shared clinical picture from incomplete information.
4. Separate immediate threats from important but non-immediate findings.
5. Maintain a forward-looking view of the resuscitation.
6. Anticipate the next decision before it becomes urgent.
7. Recognise when the team's mental model is becoming inaccurate.
8. Use structured reassessment to update the working diagnosis.
9. Avoid fixation on the initial diagnosis.
10. Communicate the team's current understanding clearly.
11. Recognise when the patient is no longer following the expected trajectory.
12. Use mental modelling to support prioritisation, delegation and decision-making.

---

# 1. What Is a Mental Model?

A mental model is an organised internal representation of:

- The patient
- The mechanism of injury
- The current physiological state
- The injuries that may be present
- The interventions already performed
- The response to those interventions
- The uncertainties that remain
- The likely next events
- The decisions that may soon be required

In trauma, the mental model is continuously evolving.

It is not a fixed diagnosis.

It is a **working representation of the patient and the resuscitation**.

A useful mental model allows the leader to answer:

> "What is happening to this patient right now?"

and:

> "What is most likely to happen next?"

and:

> "What do we need to do about it?"

---

# 2. The Leader's Mental Model Is Not the Same as a Diagnosis

A diagnosis describes a disease or injury.

A mental model is broader.

For example:

> "Pelvic fracture"

is a diagnosis.

A more useful trauma leadership mental model might be:

> "Blunt polytrauma with suspected pelvic haemorrhage, hypotension despite initial resuscitation, possible left thoracic injury, and ongoing occult haemorrhage. The immediate priority is haemorrhage control while excluding immediately reversible thoracic compromise."

The second statement contains:

- Mechanism
- Current physiology
- Probable injury
- Response to treatment
- Uncertainty
- Priority
- Immediate direction

That is much more useful to a team leader.

---

# 3. Build the Model From Multiple Layers

A practical trauma mental model can be constructed using several layers.

## Layer 1 — Mechanism

Ask:

- What happened?
- How much energy was involved?
- What direction did the force travel?
- Was there intrusion?
- Was there ejection?
- Was there a fall?
- Was there a crush mechanism?
- Was there a blast?
- Was there penetrating trauma?
- Was there a delay before treatment?

Mechanism provides the initial map of possible injury.

It does not provide the diagnosis.

---

## Layer 2 — Physiology

Ask:

- Is the patient maintaining the airway?
- Is oxygenation adequate?
- Is ventilation adequate?
- Is there shock?
- Is the patient conscious?
- Is the neurological state deteriorating?
- Is the temperature falling?
- Is the patient responding to treatment?

Physiology tells the leader how threatened the patient is.

---

## Layer 3 — Anatomy

Ask:

- What injuries are already identified?
- What injuries are strongly suspected?
- What injuries have not yet been excluded?
- Which anatomical injuries could explain the physiology?

The leader should constantly connect anatomy with physiology.

For example:

> Hypotension + pelvic fracture + ongoing blood loss

is more meaningful than simply:

> Pelvic fracture.

---

## Layer 4 — Interventions

Ask:

- What has already been done?
- What was the indication?
- Did it work?
- Is it still working?
- What complications may have been introduced?

Every intervention changes the state of the patient.

---

## Layer 5 — Trajectory

Ask:

> Is the patient getting better, worse, or staying the same?

This is one of the most important questions in trauma leadership.

A single blood pressure reading is less informative than the trajectory:

> 82 → 76 → 68 systolic despite ongoing resuscitation.

That trajectory should change the leader's mental model.

---

# 4. The Patient Is a Moving Target

A trauma patient is not a static problem.

The clinical picture changes continuously.

The patient may:

- Bleed more
- Develop airway obstruction
- Develop tension pneumothorax
- Become hypothermic
- Deteriorate neurologically
- Respond to treatment
- Fail to respond to treatment
- Develop a new complication
- Reveal an injury that was initially occult

Therefore:

> The leader's mental model must be updated continuously.

A mental model that was correct five minutes ago may now be wrong.

---

# 5. The Initial Mental Model

Before patient arrival, the leader should construct an initial model from the pre-alert.

A useful pre-arrival summary might be:

> "High-energy blunt trauma, adult male, likely multisystem injury, currently hypotensive with suspected chest and pelvic injuries. We should be prepared for major haemorrhage and immediate thoracic intervention."

This model should be:

- Brief
- Flexible
- Explicit
- Shared with the team

It should not become an assumption that cannot be challenged.

---

# 6. Avoid Premature Closure

One of the greatest dangers is deciding too early what is happening.

For example:

> "The hypotension is due to the pelvic fracture."

This may be true.

But it may also be:

- Tension pneumothorax
- Massive haemothorax
- Cardiac tamponade
- Abdominal haemorrhage
- External haemorrhage
- Neurogenic shock in selected circumstances
- A combination of injuries

The leader should ask:

> "What else could explain this physiology?"

This question protects against premature closure.

---

# 7. Think in Terms of Threats, Not Just Diagnoses

The trauma leader should initially think in terms of **threats to life**.

Examples include:

- Uncontrolled external haemorrhage
- Airway obstruction
- Severe hypoxaemia
- Tension pneumothorax
- Massive haemothorax
- Cardiac tamponade
- Major internal haemorrhage
- Severe traumatic brain injury
- Hypothermia
- Impending cardiovascular collapse

This approach is particularly useful when the diagnosis is not yet certain.

The question becomes:

> "What can kill this patient in the next few minutes, and what can we do about it?"

---

# 8. The Mental Model Should Contain Priorities

At any point during the resuscitation, the leader should be able to identify:

### Priority 1 — Immediate threat

What must happen now?

### Priority 2 — Threat requiring preparation

What is likely to require intervention shortly?

### Priority 3 — Important information

What information is needed to make the next decision?

### Priority 4 — Definitive planning

Where is the patient going next?

This prevents the team from treating every problem as equally urgent.

---

# 9. The "Now, Next, Later" Model

A simple practical framework is:

## NOW

What must be addressed immediately?

Examples:

- Airway obstruction
- Massive external haemorrhage
- Tension pneumothorax
- Profound shock

## NEXT

What is likely to require action within the next few minutes?

Examples:

- Blood product administration
- Pelvic haemorrhage control
- Chest tube placement
- Intubation
- Transfer to operating theatre
- Additional imaging required for decision-making

## LATER

What can safely wait until immediate threats are controlled?

Examples:

- Detailed secondary survey
- Minor wounds
- Definitive fracture management
- Complete documentation
- Non-urgent imaging

This framework is particularly useful when the room becomes cognitively overloaded.

---

# 10. Maintain a Forward View

A strong trauma leader does not only ask:

> "What is happening now?"

The leader also asks:

> "What will we need in five minutes?"

For example:

If the patient is:

- Hypotensive
- Receiving blood
- Suspected of pelvic haemorrhage
- Not responding adequately

the leader should anticipate:

- Activation or continuation of the major haemorrhage pathway
- Additional blood products
- Calcium monitoring and replacement according to local protocol
- Warming measures
- Definitive haemorrhage control
- Surgical or interventional radiology involvement
- Transfer destination

The leader should avoid waiting until each problem becomes critical before preparing for it.

---

# 11. Anticipation Is a Leadership Skill

Anticipation means recognising what is likely to happen before it happens.

Examples:

### Example 1

A patient with major haemorrhage is becoming increasingly cold.

The leader anticipates worsening coagulopathy and ensures active warming is addressed.

### Example 2

A patient with severe facial trauma has progressive airway swelling.

The leader anticipates difficulty with airway management rather than waiting for complete obstruction.

### Example 3

A patient with pelvic injury remains unstable despite initial resuscitation.

The leader anticipates the need for definitive haemorrhage control.

### Example 4

A patient with severe traumatic brain injury is deteriorating neurologically.

The leader anticipates airway, ventilation and neuroprotective priorities while arranging definitive management.

Anticipation converts reactive leadership into proactive leadership.

---

# 12. Reassessment Is Model Updating

Reassessment should not simply repeat observations.

It should update the mental model.

For example:

Initial model:

> "Hypotension is probably due to pelvic bleeding."

After examination:

> "There is reduced left-sided air entry."

After intervention:

> "Chest decompression produced no improvement."

The model must now change.

Possible interpretations include:

- Another source of shock
- Incorrect initial assumption
- Additional injury
- Multiple simultaneous causes

The key principle is:

> If the patient does not respond as predicted, reconsider the model.

---

# 13. Response to Treatment Is Diagnostic Information

Treatment response is itself information.

If a suspected intervention is performed and the patient improves:

> The hypothesis becomes more plausible.

If the patient does not improve:

> The hypothesis must be questioned.

For example:

A patient with severe hypotension and suspected tension pneumothorax undergoes appropriate decompression.

If there is no physiological improvement, the leader should not simply continue repeating the same assumption.

Ask:

> "What have we failed to identify?"

---

# 14. The Difference Between Facts and Assumptions

The leader should distinguish:

## Facts

- Blood pressure 70 mmHg systolic
- GCS 8
- Open femoral wound
- Absent left breath sounds
- Pelvic binder applied
- Two units of blood administered

## Assumptions

- The pelvis is the source of bleeding
- The chest injury is causing the hypotension
- The patient will improve after transfusion
- CT will be safe
- The patient can tolerate transfer

Assumptions may be necessary.

But they must remain identifiable as assumptions.

---

# 15. Use Explicit Uncertainty

Strong leaders do not need to appear certain when the evidence is uncertain.

Useful statements include:

> "Our leading concern is haemorrhagic shock, but the source is not yet established."

> "The pelvic injury may be contributing significantly, but we have not excluded another major bleeding source."

> "The patient is not responding as expected, so we need to reassess our working diagnosis."

> "I am concerned about deterioration during transfer."

This allows the team to think rather than simply follow an unchallenged conclusion.

---

# 16. Shared Mental Model

The leader's mental model should become a **shared mental model**.

The team should understand:

- What is happening
- What the immediate priorities are
- Who is doing what
- What information is still required
- What decision is approaching
- What the destination plan is

A team may have highly skilled individuals but still perform poorly if each member has a different understanding of the situation.

---

# 17. How to Create a Shared Mental Model

The leader can periodically summarise:

> "Let's pause for a brief update."

Then:

> "This is high-energy blunt trauma with ongoing haemorrhagic shock. We have treated the immediate chest concern, but the patient remains unstable. Our leading concern is major internal haemorrhage. Blood is being administered, and we are preparing for definitive haemorrhage control. We are not sending this patient to CT while unstable."

This takes seconds.

It can dramatically improve team alignment.

---

# 18. The One-Line Patient Summary

A useful technique is to construct a one-line summary.

For example:

> "Unstable adult with high-energy blunt polytrauma, suspected pelvic and thoracic injuries, ongoing haemorrhagic shock despite initial resuscitation."

The summary should contain:

- Patient type
- Mechanism
- Major injury pattern
- Physiological state
- Major unresolved threat

The one-line summary helps the leader maintain orientation.

---

# 19. The Problem List Should Be Dynamic

The problem list changes during resuscitation.

### Initial list

1. Hypotension
2. Suspected chest injury
3. Suspected pelvic injury
4. Altered consciousness

### After intervention

1. Persistent haemorrhagic shock
2. Pelvic injury
3. Chest injury treated but not fully explained
4. Traumatic brain injury
5. Hypothermia developing

The list should reflect the current patient, not merely the initial pre-alert.

---

# 20. Track What Has Been Done

A common leadership failure is losing track of interventions.

The leader should know:

- What procedures have been performed
- What medications have been administered
- What blood products have been given
- What imaging has been completed
- What devices are in place
- What interventions worked
- What interventions failed

A useful mental sequence is:

> "Problem → intervention → response."

For example:

> "Hypotension → blood products and haemorrhage control measures → partial response only."

That information should influence the next decision.

---

# 21. Track What Has Not Been Done

The leader should also maintain awareness of unresolved tasks.

Examples:

- No definitive haemorrhage control yet
- No temperature measured
- No repeat neurological assessment
- No definitive airway
- No blood gas
- No cross-sectional imaging when indicated and appropriate
- No receiving team contacted
- No transfer plan

The leader's mental model must include both completed and incomplete actions.

---

# 22. Avoid Cognitive Saturation

Trauma resuscitation generates a large amount of information.

The leader may receive:

- Monitor data
- Laboratory results
- Ultrasound findings
- Radiographs
- Clinical examination findings
- Medication information
- Blood product information
- Procedural updates
- Team concerns
- Requests from other departments

Not all information has equal importance.

The leader must filter.

Ask:

> "Does this change what we need to do now?"

If not, it may not belong in the immediate decision-making space.

---

# 23. Signal Versus Noise

### High-value information

- Persistent hypotension
- Deteriorating GCS
- Increasing oxygen requirement
- Ongoing haemorrhage
- Failed intervention
- New major examination finding
- Major imaging finding
- Failure to respond to treatment

### Lower-priority information

- Minor wounds
- Non-urgent documentation issues
- Definitive management of stable injuries
- Details that do not alter immediate management

The leader's job is not to ignore information.

It is to **prioritise information**.

---

# 24. Information Should Be Linked to Action

Whenever information is presented, the leader should consider:

> "What does this mean for the patient?"

and:

> "Does it change our plan?"

For example:

> "The haemoglobin is low."

The more useful interpretation may be:

> "The patient has evidence supporting significant blood loss; ongoing haemorrhage remains a major concern."

The laboratory value becomes clinically meaningful when connected to the overall model.

---

# 25. Beware of Anchoring

Anchoring occurs when the team gives excessive weight to an initial interpretation.

Examples:

> "The patient is hypotensive because of the pelvis."

> "The altered consciousness is due to alcohol."

> "The chest X-ray explains everything."

> "The mechanism sounds minor."

Anchoring becomes dangerous when contradictory information is ignored.

The corrective question is:

> "What finding would make our current diagnosis wrong?"

---

# 26. Beware of Confirmation Bias

Once a diagnosis is suspected, people may preferentially notice evidence supporting it.

The leader should actively look for contradictory evidence.

Ask:

> "What doesn't fit?"

For example:

If pelvic bleeding is believed to be the sole cause of shock but the patient develops severe hypoxaemia and unilateral absent breath sounds, the model must expand.

---

# 27. Beware of Search Satisfaction

Finding one important injury does not mean the assessment is complete.

A patient may have:

- A pelvic fracture
- A pneumothorax
- An abdominal injury
- A head injury
- Long-bone fractures

The discovery of one major injury should not terminate the search for others.

---

# 28. Beware of Diagnosis Momentum

A diagnosis may become increasingly accepted simply because it has been repeated.

For example:

> "Pelvic haemorrhage."

becomes:

> "Pelvic haemorrhage."

and eventually:

> "The pelvic haemorrhage is the reason for the shock."

without additional evidence.

The leader should periodically return to the actual data.

---

# 29. Use the Mechanism as a Check, Not a Conclusion

Mechanism is useful for predicting injury patterns.

But mechanism should not override clinical findings.

For example:

> "Low-speed collision"

does not guarantee minor injury.

Likewise:

> "High-speed collision"

does not guarantee severe injury.

The patient must be assessed according to current physiology and anatomy.

---

# 30. Use the "Could This Patient Be Worse?" Question

When the patient appears stable, ask:

> "Could there be a serious injury that we have not yet recognised?"

When the patient is unstable, ask:

> "What immediately reversible life threat have we not yet addressed?"

When the patient fails to improve:

> "What assumption is wrong?"

These questions help keep the mental model dynamic.

---

# 31. The Leader Should Know the Destination Early

The mental model should eventually include the next destination.

Potential destinations include:

- Operating theatre
- Interventional radiology
- CT
- Intensive care
- Emergency department observation or ward in selected patients
- Transfer to a major trauma centre

The destination should be determined by:

- Physiology
- Injury pattern
- Response to resuscitation
- Available resources
- Need for definitive intervention
- Local trauma pathways

A patient who requires immediate haemorrhage control should not be delayed simply because a more complete diagnostic picture is desirable.

---

# 32. CT Is a Decision, Not a Default

One of the most important leadership decisions is whether the patient is safe for imaging.

The mental model should ask:

- Is the patient physiologically stable enough?
- Will CT change immediate management?
- Is there a more urgent intervention required?
- Can the patient be continuously monitored during transfer?
- Is the necessary team and equipment available?
- What happens if the patient deteriorates in the scanner?

The question is not:

> "Can we get a CT?"

It is:

> "Is CT the correct next step for this patient?"

---

# 33. The Mental Model During Transfer

The mental model must travel with the patient.

Before transfer, the leader should be able to summarise:

> "This patient has unstable blunt polytrauma with suspected major haemorrhage. Blood products are ongoing. The pelvis is stabilised. The airway is secured. We are transferring for definitive haemorrhage control. If hypotension worsens, the immediate concern is ongoing haemorrhage."

This gives the receiving team a working model before the patient arrives.

---

# 34. Mental Models and Handover

A poor handover gives isolated facts.

A strong handover gives a coherent picture.

Poor:

> "He's 35. BP 90. Pelvis fracture. Chest injury. Two units given."

Better:

> "This is a 35-year-old with high-energy blunt polytrauma. He remains haemodynamically unstable despite initial blood product resuscitation. Imaging and examination suggest pelvic injury with possible thoracic trauma. Immediate chest threats have been addressed, but ongoing haemorrhage remains the major concern. He requires definitive haemorrhage control."

The second handover communicates the mental model.

---

# 35. Mental Model and Team Briefing

The pre-arrival briefing should create an initial shared model.

For example:

> "We are expecting an unstable blunt trauma patient with suspected major haemorrhage. Let's assume we may need blood immediately. Airway and chest intervention equipment should be ready. We need early access to ultrasound and blood products. If the patient remains unstable, our pathway is haemorrhage control rather than routine CT."

This creates anticipation.

---

# 36. Mental Model and Closed-Loop Communication

Closed-loop communication helps maintain the model.

Example:

> Leader: "Prepare the massive haemorrhage protocol."

> Nurse: "Massive haemorrhage protocol activated."

> Leader: "Good. Tell me when blood arrives."

> Nurse: "Understood."

The leader knows:

- The task was assigned
- The task was understood
- The task was completed or remains pending

This reduces uncertainty.

---

# 37. The Leader's Internal Questions

Throughout the resuscitation, the leader should repeatedly ask:

### Situation

> What is happening?

### Threat

> What can kill this patient now?

### Evidence

> What do we actually know?

### Uncertainty

> What do we not know?

### Response

> Is the patient responding?

### Priority

> What needs to happen next?

### Anticipation

> What will we need in the next few minutes?

### Destination

> Where is this patient going?

### Reassessment

> Does the current model still fit?

These questions provide a cognitive scaffold.

---

# 38. A Practical Mental Model Template

A trauma team leader can mentally organise the case as:

### Patient

Who is the patient?

### Mechanism

What happened?

### Physiology

How sick is the patient?

### Anatomy

What injuries are known or suspected?

### Threats

What can kill the patient now?

### Interventions

What has been done?

### Response

Did it work?

### Uncertainty

What remains unexplained?

### Next action

What must happen now?

### Next decision

What decision is approaching?

### Destination

Where does the patient need to go?

This template can be used repeatedly.

---

# 39. The Five-Line Trauma Model

When cognitive load is high, reduce the situation to five lines:

1. **Mechanism**
2. **Physiology**
3. **Major injuries**
4. **Immediate threats**
5. **Next destination**

For example:

> **Mechanism:** High-energy blunt trauma.

> **Physiology:** Persistent haemorrhagic shock.

> **Major injuries:** Pelvic fracture and suspected thoracic injury.

> **Immediate threats:** Ongoing haemorrhage and potential respiratory deterioration.

> **Destination:** Definitive haemorrhage control.

This can restore orientation when the room becomes chaotic.

---

# 40. When the Model Breaks Down

Warning signs include:

- The leader cannot summarise the case
- Multiple team members give conflicting priorities
- Repeated interventions occur without reassessment
- The patient is deteriorating without an explanation
- Tasks are being duplicated
- Important tasks are missed
- The destination remains unclear
- Team members ask the same questions repeatedly
- The leader becomes focused on one problem
- The team is treating observations rather than threats

When these occur:

> Stop briefly, reassess, summarise, and rebuild the model.

---

# 41. The Reset

A cognitive reset can be as simple as:

> "Everyone pause for a moment."

Then:

> "This patient is currently unstable."

> "Our main concern is ongoing haemorrhage."

> "The chest issue has been addressed but does not fully explain the physiology."

> "We need blood resuscitation and definitive haemorrhage control."

> "What information do we need before the next decision?"

This restores shared orientation.

---

# 42. Mental Model Under Pressure

Pressure tends to narrow attention.

The leader may become focused on:

- The airway
- The blood pressure
- The ultrasound
- A procedure
- A difficult team member
- A technical problem

The danger is **tunnel vision**.

The leader must periodically zoom out.

Ask:

> "What else is happening?"

This is one of the most valuable questions in high-acuity medicine.

---

# 43. Zoom In and Zoom Out

Effective trauma leadership requires movement between two levels.

## Zoom in

Focus on the immediate technical problem.

Example:

> "The airway is becoming difficult."

## Zoom out

Return to the whole patient.

Example:

> "While the airway is being managed, is haemorrhage also progressing?"

Both are necessary.

A leader who only zooms in may lose situational awareness.

A leader who only zooms out may fail to recognise a rapidly evolving technical emergency.

---

# 44. The Leader Does Not Need to Personally Solve Every Problem

The mental model should include the whole team.

The leader can ask:

> "What are you seeing?"

> "What concerns you?"

> "What do you think is causing the deterioration?"

> "What would you do next?"

This uses the expertise distributed throughout the team.

The leader's role is to integrate these observations into a coherent plan.

---

# 45. Invite Challenge

A robust mental model should be challengeable.

Useful phrases include:

> "Does anyone see a different explanation?"

> "What are we missing?"

> "Is there anything that does not fit?"

> "Any concerns about the current plan?"

This is not loss of authority.

It is a patient-safety strategy.

---

# 46. The Leader's Mental Model Must Be Shared but Not Rigid

A shared mental model does not mean everyone must agree with every assumption.

It means everyone understands:

- The current working diagnosis
- The immediate priorities
- The major uncertainties
- The planned next steps

Team members should be able to challenge the model when new evidence appears.

---

# 47. Case-Based Discussion

## Case

A 45-year-old patient is brought after a high-speed motorcycle collision.

On arrival:

- GCS 13
- HR 132
- BP 78/48
- RR 30
- SpO₂ 91%
- Cool peripheries
- Pelvic tenderness
- Abdominal tenderness
- Reduced left-sided breath sounds

A pelvic binder is applied.

Oxygen is administered.

A focused ultrasound examination does not show an obvious large pericardial effusion.

The patient remains hypotensive.

### Question 1

What is the immediate mental model?

### Suggested answer

High-energy blunt polytrauma with severe physiological instability and multiple possible causes of shock, including thoracic and intra-abdominal or pelvic haemorrhage.

The cause of shock should not be prematurely attributed to the pelvis alone.

---

## Question 2

What should the leader ask?

### Suggested answer

- What immediate life threats are present?
- Is ventilation adequate?
- Is there a treatable thoracic emergency?
- Is there ongoing major haemorrhage?
- What has already been done?
- Has the patient responded?
- What should happen next?
- Is the patient stable enough for further imaging?

---

## Question 3

The patient remains hypotensive after initial interventions.

What does this mean?

### Suggested answer

The mental model must be updated.

The leader should consider:

- Ongoing haemorrhage
- Multiple simultaneous bleeding sources
- Inadequately treated thoracic injury
- Another cause of shock
- Insufficient resuscitation
- Incorrect initial assumptions

Failure to improve is information.

---

## Question 4

The team proposes CT.

What should the leader ask?

### Suggested answer

> "Will CT change immediate management, and is the patient sufficiently stable to undergo it?"

If the patient requires immediate definitive haemorrhage control, delaying that intervention for imaging may be inappropriate.

---

# 48. Advanced Scenario: The Model Changes

A patient arrives after a penetrating abdominal injury.

Initial assessment suggests major intra-abdominal haemorrhage.

The patient becomes increasingly hypotensive.

The team focuses on abdominal bleeding.

Suddenly:

- Oxygen saturation falls
- Airway pressures increase
- Neck veins appear distended
- Breath sounds become markedly reduced on one side

The mental model must change.

The leader should recognise that a new immediately reversible thoracic threat may have developed or become apparent.

The lesson is:

> The initial diagnosis does not own the patient.

New evidence can and should change the plan.

---

# 49. Advanced Scenario: The Patient Does Not Behave as Expected

A patient with presumed isolated chest trauma receives appropriate initial treatment.

The patient remains profoundly hypotensive.

The team continues discussing the chest injury.

The leader asks:

> "If the chest injury were the main cause, what response would we expect?"

The patient has not produced that response.

Therefore:

> The model requires reconsideration.

Possible additional causes include:

- Abdominal haemorrhage
- Pelvic haemorrhage
- External blood loss
- Cardiac injury
- Other causes of shock

The leader must resist the urge to simply repeat the existing plan.

---

# 50. A Mental Model Is a Hypothesis

The most useful way to think about the trauma mental model is:

> It is a working hypothesis that must continuously be tested against reality.

The cycle is:

**Observe → Interpret → Act → Reassess → Update**

Not:

**Observe → Diagnose → Stop thinking**

---

# 51. The Trauma Leadership Loop

A practical leadership loop is:

### 1. Observe

What is happening?

### 2. Orient

What does it mean?

### 3. Prioritise

What matters most?

### 4. Act

What should we do?

### 5. Reassess

Did it work?

### 6. Update

Does the mental model still fit?

### 7. Anticipate

What happens next?

This cycle should continue throughout the resuscitation.

# 52. Practical Cognitive Aid

## TRAUMA MENTAL MODEL

### MECHANISM
What happened?

### PHYSIOLOGY
How sick is the patient?

### ANATOMY
What injuries are known or suspected?

### THREATS
What can kill the patient now?

### ACTIONS
What has been done?

### RESPONSE
Did it work?

### GAPS
What remains unexplained?

### NEXT
What needs to happen now?

### DESTINATION
Where is the patient going?

### RESET
Does our model still fit?

---

# 53. Common Pitfalls

## Pitfall 1 — Treating the initial diagnosis as fact

### Problem

The team becomes anchored to the pre-alert.

### Correction

Treat the pre-alert as an initial hypothesis.

---

## Pitfall 2 — Focusing on one injury

### Problem

The team discovers a major injury and stops searching.

### Correction

Continue to ask whether the injury explains the entire physiological picture.

---

## Pitfall 3 — Ignoring treatment response

### Problem

The same intervention is repeated despite failure.

### Correction

Use response to treatment as diagnostic information.

---

## Pitfall 4 — Losing the big picture

### Problem

The leader becomes absorbed in a technical problem.

### Correction

Periodically zoom out and reassess the whole patient.

---

## Pitfall 5 — Failure to anticipate

### Problem

Resources are requested only after deterioration.

### Correction

Think one step ahead.

---

## Pitfall 6 — Poor shared mental model

### Problem

Different team members believe different things are happening.

### Correction

Give short, explicit team summaries.

---

## Pitfall 7 — Information overload

### Problem

The leader attempts to process every detail equally.

### Correction

Prioritise information according to its effect on immediate decisions.

---

## Pitfall 8 — Confusing activity with progress

### Problem

The room is busy but the patient is not improving.

### Correction

Ask:

> "What has actually changed?"

---

# 54. Team Communication Prompts

Useful phrases include:

### Establishing the model

> "Give me the one-line picture."

### Testing the model

> "What doesn't fit?"

### Challenging assumptions

> "What else could explain this?"

### Reassessment

> "Has the patient responded?"

### Anticipation

> "What are we going to need next?"

### Prioritisation

> "What is the immediate threat?"

### Reset

> "Let's pause and reassess the whole patient."

### Shared understanding

> "This is where we are, and this is where we are going."

---

# 55. When to Deliberately Rebuild the Mental Model

A deliberate reset is particularly useful when:

- The patient's physiology changes
- An intervention fails
- A new major injury is identified
- The diagnosis becomes uncertain
- Multiple specialists become involved
- The team changes
- The patient is moved to another location
- The destination changes
- There is prolonged resuscitation
- The situation becomes chaotic

The leader should not wait for complete loss of control.

---

# 56. The Mental Model During Prolonged Resuscitation

Long resuscitations create additional cognitive risks.

The team may forget:

- What has already been given
- Which interventions were effective
- What injuries remain untreated
- What the original physiology was
- Whether the patient has improved
- Why a decision was made

Periodic summaries become increasingly important.

For example:

> "We are now 30 minutes into resuscitation. The patient remains unstable despite blood product administration. Thoracic intervention has not corrected the shock. The dominant concern remains uncontrolled haemorrhage. We need definitive haemorrhage control now."

---

# 57. Mental Model and Leadership Authority

Authority should come from:

- Clarity
- Consistency
- Situational awareness
- Appropriate delegation
- Evidence-based decision-making
- Willingness to reconsider
- Calm communication

Not from pretending to have complete certainty.

A strong leader can say:

> "I don't know yet, but this is what we know, this is what worries me, and this is what we are doing next."

That is effective leadership.

---

# 58. The Difference Between Confidence and Certainty

Confidence means:

> "I can make a decision despite uncertainty."

Certainty means:

> "I believe there is no meaningful uncertainty."

Trauma rarely provides complete certainty.

Therefore, the leader should aim for:

> **Decisive action with appropriate uncertainty.**

---

# 59. The Mental Model and Decision-Making

A good mental model improves decision-making because it clarifies:

- The current problem
- The available options
- The risks
- The time pressure
- The information required
- The consequences of delay

The leader does not need perfect information.

The leader needs enough information to make the safest appropriate decision at that moment.

---

# 60. The Mental Model and Time-Critical Decisions

Some decisions cannot wait for diagnostic completeness.

Examples include:

- Immediate airway intervention
- Control of life-threatening external haemorrhage
- Treatment of tension pneumothorax
- Initiation of major haemorrhage resuscitation
- Rapid transfer for definitive haemorrhage control

The mental model should therefore distinguish:

> Information that is useful

from:

> Information that is necessary before acting.

This prevents diagnostic delay.

---

# 61. The "Minimum Safe Information" Principle

Before a time-critical action, ask:

> "What do we absolutely need to know before acting?"

Do not automatically seek every available piece of information.

For example:

If the patient has clear clinical evidence of an immediately life-threatening reversible problem, treatment should not necessarily wait for a complete diagnostic work-up.

---

# 62. The Leader's Mental Model and Cognitive Load

Cognitive load increases with:

- Multiple simultaneous problems
- Incomplete information
- Time pressure
- Noise
- Team conflict
- Equipment problems
- Unfamiliar environments
- Fatigue
- Prolonged resuscitation

Structured thinking reduces cognitive load.

The leader should externalise information where possible:

- Whiteboard
- Trauma chart
- Team summary
- Written task list
- Visible blood product record
- Clear role allocation

The goal is not to remember everything.

The goal is to make important information difficult to lose.

---

# 63. Externalising the Mental Model

A visible trauma board can include:

| Domain | Current status |
|---|---|
| Airway | Patent / secured / threatened |
| Breathing | Current concern |
| Circulation | Shock status |
| Haemorrhage | Suspected source |
| Disability | Neurological status |
| Exposure | Temperature / injuries |
| Procedures | Completed |
| Blood products | Given / ongoing |
| Investigations | Completed / pending |
| Major concerns | Current threats |
| Destination | Planned location |

This converts an internal mental model into a shared team model.

---

# 64. The Mental Model and Documentation

Documentation should support, not replace, the mental model.

The record should make it possible to understand:

- What was happening
- What the team believed
- What interventions were performed
- How the patient responded
- Why major decisions were made
- Where the patient was transferred

Good documentation preserves the reasoning pathway.

---

# 65. A Final Mental Check Before Major Decisions

Before committing to a major pathway, ask:

1. What is the current physiological state?
2. What is the most likely major threat?
3. What immediately reversible threats remain?
4. What evidence supports our current model?
5. What does not fit?
6. Has the patient responded as expected?
7. What information is still essential?
8. What is the consequence of waiting?
9. What is the next safest action?
10. Where does the patient need to go?

If the leader can answer these questions, the team is usually better positioned to proceed.

---

# 66. Key Takeaways

1. The trauma leader's mental model is a dynamic representation of the patient, injuries, physiology, interventions and trajectory.
2. A mental model is broader than a diagnosis.
3. The model should begin with mechanism but must be driven by current physiology and clinical findings.
4. Think in terms of life-threatening threats, not only anatomical diagnoses.
5. Separate what must happen now from what can happen later.
6. Maintain a forward view of the resuscitation.
7. Anticipate deterioration and resource requirements.
8. Treatment response is diagnostic information.
9. Failure to improve should trigger reconsideration of the working model.
10. Distinguish facts from assumptions.
11. Avoid anchoring, premature closure, confirmation bias and diagnosis momentum.
12. The leader should periodically zoom out from technical problems to reassess the whole patient.
13. A shared mental model keeps the team aligned.
14. Explicit summaries reduce confusion.
15. Invite challenge and actively look for information that does not fit.
16. The patient's destination should be considered early.
17. CT is a decision, not an automatic step.
18. The mental model should travel with the patient during transfer and handover.
19. Structured cognitive aids can reduce cognitive load.
20. The mental model should continuously cycle through observation, interpretation, action, reassessment and updating.

> **The best trauma leaders do not simply recognise injuries. They maintain an accurate, shared and continuously updated picture of the patient — and use that picture to decide what matters next.**
