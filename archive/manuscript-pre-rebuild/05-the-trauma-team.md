# Chapter 5 — The Trauma Team

## Clinical Scenario

A 34-year-old man is brought to the emergency department after a high-speed road traffic collision.

The pre-hospital team reports:

- high-energy mechanism
- suspected chest and abdominal injuries
- GCS 12
- heart rate 128/min
- systolic blood pressure 88 mmHg
- respiratory rate 30/min
- oxygen saturation 91% despite supplemental oxygen
- pelvic pain and deformity of the right lower limb
- estimated time of arrival: 5 minutes

The trauma team is activated.

Within seconds, multiple clinicians enter the resuscitation room.

An emergency physician prepares to lead the resuscitation.

An anaesthetist prepares for airway management.

A trauma surgeon arrives and asks for the mechanism and vital signs.

Several nurses begin preparing monitoring equipment, intravenous access, blood products and airway equipment.

A radiographer asks whether portable imaging is required.

A junior doctor begins asking the paramedics questions while another clinician starts examining the patient.

The room becomes busy.

Everyone is working.

But nobody has clearly stated:

> "I am leading."

No one has explicitly assigned:

- airway responsibility
- circulation responsibility
- procedure responsibility
- medication responsibility
- documentation responsibility
- communication with relatives
- liaison with radiology
- liaison with theatre or interventional radiology

The team is clinically capable.

But the team is not yet organised.

This distinction is fundamental.

A trauma team is not simply a collection of clinicians standing around an injured patient.

It is a **structured clinical system**.

Its effectiveness depends on:

- appropriate composition
- clear leadership
- defined roles
- shared situational awareness
- effective communication
- anticipation
- coordination
- task allocation
- escalation pathways
- disciplined reassessment

The Trauma Team Leader does not need to perform every task.

The leader needs to make sure that the right tasks are performed, in the right order, by the right people, at the right time.

5. Allocate responsibilities according to patient needs and team capability.

# 1. What Is a Trauma Team?

A trauma team is a multidisciplinary group of healthcare professionals assembled to assess and treat patients with major or potentially life-threatening trauma.

The team exists because major trauma frequently involves:

- multiple injured organ systems
- rapidly changing physiology
- simultaneous diagnostic and therapeutic requirements
- competing priorities
- time-critical interventions
- multiple specialist disciplines
- substantial information uncertainty

No single clinician can reliably perform all required functions simultaneously.

The trauma team therefore distributes work.

A high-performing team creates **parallel processing**.

While one clinician manages the airway:

- another assesses circulation
- another establishes vascular access
- another obtains blood samples
- another prepares blood products
- another performs the initial neurological assessment
- another documents events
- the leader integrates the information

This allows several clinically important processes to occur simultaneously.

The purpose of the team is not simply to increase the number of people in the room.

The purpose is to increase the team's ability to:

> **recognise threats, intervene rapidly, reassess continuously and coordinate definitive care.**
