# Chapter 23 — Clinical Governance and Accountability

## Why Clinical Governance Matters to the Trauma Team Leader

Clinical governance is not a separate administrative activity that occurs after the trauma call.

It is part of clinical leadership.

Every trauma resuscitation is an opportunity to demonstrate whether the organisation can provide safe, effective, timely and accountable care under pressure.

The trauma team leader is therefore not responsible only for what happens during the resuscitation.

The leader is also responsible for recognising when care is becoming unsafe, escalating concerns, ensuring important decisions are communicated, maintaining an appropriate standard of documentation, and helping the team learn from significant events.

A technically successful resuscitation can still contain important governance failures.

Conversely, a difficult case with an adverse outcome may represent high-quality care if the team recognised deterioration, acted appropriately, communicated clearly, escalated early and learned from the event.

Clinical governance is therefore not synonymous with avoiding bad outcomes.

It is the system through which teams continuously improve the quality and safety of care.

---

## 1. Governance Is a Clinical Responsibility

The trauma team leader should think about governance during the resuscitation rather than after it.

The leader should continuously ask:

- Is the patient receiving appropriate care?
- Are important decisions being made by the appropriate clinician?
- Are concerns being escalated early enough?
- Are we working within our competence?
- Are we following agreed pathways where appropriate?
- Are deviations from normal practice justified?
- Is the team communicating critical information effectively?
- Is the patient's response being reassessed?
- Is there a clear plan for definitive care?
- Will another clinician understand what happened when they review the record later?

These questions transform governance from an administrative concept into a practical component of clinical leadership.

---

## 2. The Three Dimensions of Accountability

During major trauma, accountability operates at several levels.

### Individual accountability

Each clinician remains responsible for the actions they personally undertake.

This includes:

- recognising deterioration;
- performing tasks competently;
- identifying limitations;
- seeking assistance;
- communicating important findings;
- documenting significant decisions;
- escalating concerns.

Being part of a trauma team does not remove individual professional responsibility.

---

### Team accountability

The team leader has responsibility for coordinating the overall process.

This includes:

- establishing priorities;
- assigning roles;
- coordinating interventions;
- monitoring progress;
- ensuring reassessment;
- escalating when required;
- ensuring appropriate transfer or definitive management.

The leader should create a team environment in which clinicians can raise concerns.

---

### System accountability

Some failures arise from the environment rather than from an individual clinician.

Examples include:

- inadequate staffing;
- unavailable equipment;
- delayed blood products;
- unclear referral pathways;
- communication failures between departments;
- insufficient training;
- inadequate access to specialist support;
- delays in theatre, interventional radiology or critical care.

A mature governance system distinguishes individual performance problems from system weaknesses.

The trauma leader should recognise both.

---

## 3. Accountability Does Not Mean Blame

One of the most important principles of clinical governance is the distinction between accountability and blame.

A clinician may be accountable for an action without being blameworthy.

Similarly, an adverse outcome does not automatically indicate negligence or poor performance.

Trauma care is characterised by:

- incomplete information;
- time pressure;
- rapidly changing physiology;
- competing priorities;
- uncertain diagnoses;
- limited resources;
- complex team interactions.

The appropriate question after a difficult case is not simply:

> "Who made the mistake?"

A better sequence is:

1. What happened?
2. What was known at the time?
3. What decisions were made?
4. Why did those decisions appear reasonable at the time?
5. What went well?
6. What did not go well?
7. What contributed to the problem?
8. Could the problem reasonably have been prevented?
9. What should change as a result?

This approach supports learning rather than concealment.

---

# 4. The Trauma Leader as a Governance Role Model

The behaviour of the team leader strongly influences team culture.

A leader who ignores concerns teaches the team to remain silent.

A leader who welcomes challenge teaches the team to speak up.

A leader who hides uncertainty teaches the team to conceal uncertainty.

A leader who openly acknowledges uncertainty teaches the team to manage it safely.

A leader who becomes defensive after an error may create fear.

A leader who responds constructively can turn the same event into a learning opportunity.

The leader should therefore model:

- professionalism;
- transparency;
- respectful communication;
- appropriate escalation;
- situational awareness;
- willingness to reconsider decisions;
- acknowledgement of uncertainty;
- ownership of mistakes;
- commitment to improvement.

---

# 5. Speaking Up and Psychological Safety

A safe trauma team must allow clinicians to raise concerns.

Any member of the team may recognise something that the leader has missed.

This is particularly important because the leader may be focused on:

- overall priorities;
- haemorrhage control;
- airway decisions;
- operative planning;
- coordination with specialists;
- transfer arrangements.

Other team members may have better access to specific information.

For example:

The anaesthetist may recognise deteriorating ventilation.

The nurse may notice an unexpected change in vital signs.

The radiographer may identify a concerning imaging finding.

The junior doctor may notice that a medication has not been administered.

The blood bank may identify a problem with product availability.

The team leader should therefore actively invite concerns.

Useful phrases include:

> "If anyone sees a problem, call it out."

> "Tell me if you think our priority needs to change."

> "What am I missing?"

> "Does anyone have a concern about this plan?"

These statements create permission for challenge.

---

# 6. Constructive Challenge

Challenge should be directed at the clinical problem rather than at the person.

Poor:

> "You are doing this wrong."

Better:

> "I'm concerned that the patient's blood pressure is continuing to fall despite the current strategy."

Better still:

> "The patient remains unstable. I think we need to reconsider the current plan."

The goal is not to establish who is right.

The goal is to identify the safest course of action.

The team leader should respond to challenge with curiosity rather than defensiveness.

---

# 7. When the Leader Is Wrong

Every leader will eventually make an incorrect assessment.

The critical issue is how quickly the error is recognised and corrected.

A dangerous leadership pattern is:

1. make an early decision;
2. become psychologically committed to it;
3. reinterpret new information to support it;
4. ignore contradictory evidence;
5. continue despite deterioration.

A safer pattern is:

1. make the best decision available with current information;
2. state the assumptions behind it;
3. reassess the patient;
4. look for evidence that supports or contradicts the plan;
5. modify the plan when necessary.

A leader should be able to say:

> "The patient is not responding as expected. We need to change direction."

Changing a plan is not weakness.

Persisting with an unsafe plan because it was previously announced is weakness.

---

# 8. Clinical Documentation as a Governance Tool

Documentation is not merely a legal record.

It is also a communication and patient-safety tool.

A good trauma record should allow another clinician to reconstruct:

- what happened;
- the patient's condition;
- the important findings;
- major interventions;
- response to treatment;
- significant decisions;
- who was involved;
- why major decisions were made;
- what the next plan was.

Documentation becomes particularly important when:

- the patient is transferred;
- the team changes;
- the patient deteriorates later;
- a procedure is performed;
- a major decision is challenged;
- a complication occurs;
- the case is reviewed.

---

# 9. What Should Be Documented?

Documentation requirements vary by institution, but major trauma records should generally capture clinically significant events.

Important elements include:

### Initial condition

Record relevant:

- mechanism;
- time of injury when known;
- initial physiological state;
- major injuries suspected;
- important examination findings.

### Major interventions

Document significant interventions such as:

- airway management;
- ventilation;
- chest decompression;
- haemorrhage control;
- blood product administration;
- major procedures;
- analgesia;
- imaging;
- transfer.

### Response

Record important physiological or clinical responses.

Examples:

- blood pressure improved;
- oxygenation improved;
- persistent shock;
- ongoing external bleeding;
- worsening respiratory distress;
- neurological deterioration.

### Major decisions

Important decisions should be identifiable.

Examples:

- decision to intubate;
- decision to activate massive transfusion;
- decision for emergency surgery;
- decision to transfer;
- decision to stop a particular intervention;
- decision to change the treatment strategy.

---

# 10. Documenting Clinical Reasoning

A record that states only:

> "Intubated."

may be insufficient to communicate the clinical reasoning.

Where appropriate, documentation should make clear:

- what prompted the intervention;
- who made the decision;
- what was done;
- what happened afterwards.

Similarly:

> "CT requested."

provides less information than:

> "Given haemodynamic stability after initial resuscitation and concern for intra-abdominal injury, CT trauma imaging requested."

The objective is not to create unnecessary documentation.

The objective is to record information that matters for patient care and subsequent review.

---

# 11. The Importance of Time

Trauma care is time-sensitive.

Important events should be documented with accurate times where feasible.

Examples include:

- arrival;
- recognition of deterioration;
- administration of blood products;
- procedures;
- imaging;
- consultant review;
- activation of major protocols;
- transfer.

Accurate chronology can be extremely valuable when reviewing a complex resuscitation.

It can reveal:

- delays;
- parallel processes;
- repeated reassessments;
- treatment-response relationships;
- communication gaps.

---

# 12. Verbal Decisions Must Become Shared Decisions

A trauma leader may make numerous decisions verbally.

However, important decisions should not remain isolated inside the leader's mind.

The team should know:

- what the decision is;
- why it has been made;
- what is expected next;
- what would trigger a change.

For example:

> "The patient remains unstable despite blood products. We are treating this as uncontrolled haemorrhage and moving toward definitive haemorrhage control."

This creates a shared mental model.

---

# 13. Escalation Is a Governance Skill

Failure to escalate is a common source of risk.

Escalation should occur when:

- the patient's condition exceeds the team's capability;
- specialist expertise is required;
- the diagnosis remains uncertain and the consequences are significant;
- the patient is deteriorating;
- resources are insufficient;
- transfer is required;
- a major intervention may be needed.

Escalation is not an admission of failure.

It is recognition of the limits of the current system.

---

# 14. Escalation Should Be Early

A useful principle is:

> Escalate before the situation becomes impossible.

Early escalation creates options.

Late escalation creates emergencies.

For example, contacting:

- the trauma surgeon;
- anaesthesia;
- orthopaedics;
- neurosurgery;
- interventional radiology;
- intensive care;
- blood bank;
- retrieval services;

may be appropriate before the patient reaches a point of physiological collapse.

The exact escalation pathway depends on the local system.

---

# 15. Knowing the Limits of the System

Clinical governance requires awareness of organisational capability.

The trauma leader should know:

- what specialist services are immediately available;
- what services require referral;
- whether emergency surgery is available;
- whether interventional radiology is available;
- critical care capacity;
- blood product availability;
- transfer pathways;
- retrieval arrangements;
- imaging capability;
- out-of-hours limitations.

A treatment plan that depends on an unavailable resource is not a safe plan.

---

# 16. Deviating From Protocol

Protocols are designed to support safe care.

They are not substitutes for clinical judgement.

Trauma frequently produces circumstances in which the patient's condition does not fit the standard pathway.

A deviation may therefore be appropriate when:

- the patient has an unusual presentation;
- the standard pathway is unavailable;
- the patient is deteriorating;
- multiple injuries create competing priorities;
- specialist advice changes the plan;
- local circumstances require adaptation.

When deviating from a protocol, the leader should be able to explain:

1. What the usual pathway would be.
2. Why it was not appropriate in this situation.
3. What alternative strategy was chosen.
4. Why the alternative was considered safer.

---

# 17. Protocols Must Not Replace Reassessment

A protocol may recommend a sequence of actions.

But the patient's physiology determines whether the strategy is working.

The team must continue to reassess.

If the patient deteriorates despite following the protocol, the leader should not simply continue because:

> "That is what the protocol says."

Instead:

> "The patient is not responding. What does this tell us?"

The protocol provides structure.

Clinical reassessment determines direction.

---

# 18. Incident Recognition

A significant incident may involve:

- medication error;
- equipment failure;
- delayed treatment;
- communication failure;
- incorrect patient identification;
- procedural complication;
- delayed escalation;
- blood product error;
- failure of monitoring;
- inappropriate transfer;
- unexpected deterioration associated with a system problem.

The leader should recognise when an event may require formal reporting.

Local reporting procedures should be followed.

---

# 19. Immediate Response to an Incident

The first priority is always the patient.

When an incident occurs:

### Step 1 — Stabilise the patient

Correct the immediate clinical problem.

### Step 2 — Protect others

Prevent the same error from affecting another patient.

### Step 3 — Inform appropriate senior staff

Escalate according to local policy.

### Step 4 — Preserve relevant information

Ensure important documentation and records are maintained.

### Step 5 — Report through the appropriate system

Follow local incident-reporting procedures.

### Step 6 — Support the team

Recognise that clinicians involved in adverse events may also require support.

---

# 20. Do Not Conceal Errors

An error that is concealed cannot be reliably learned from.

Concealment may also allow the same system weakness to harm another patient.

A professional response is:

- recognise;
- stabilise;
- disclose appropriately;
- document accurately;
- escalate;
- report;
- learn.

The exact requirements for disclosure and reporting depend on local policy and professional regulation.

---

# 21. Human Factors in Error

Errors rarely occur because of one isolated factor.

They may result from interactions between:

- workload;
- fatigue;
- interruptions;
- unclear communication;
- equipment;
- environment;
- staffing;
- hierarchy;
- cognitive overload;
- poor system design;
- inadequate training.

The team leader should therefore resist simplistic explanations.

Instead of:

> "The clinician made a mistake."

ask:

> "What conditions made this mistake more likely?"

This question is more useful for prevention.

---

# 22. Cognitive Error

Trauma leadership is vulnerable to several cognitive traps.

Examples include:

### Anchoring

Becoming fixed on the initial diagnosis.

### Premature closure

Stopping the diagnostic process too early.

### Confirmation bias

Looking primarily for evidence that supports the existing diagnosis.

### Availability bias

Overestimating a diagnosis because it was recently encountered.

### Framing effect

Allowing the way information is presented to influence interpretation.

### Action bias

Feeling compelled to intervene even when the intervention may not help.

### Omission bias

Avoiding an intervention because the clinician fears causing harm.

The leader should recognise these patterns in themselves and the team.

---

# 23. Countermeasures Against Cognitive Error

Useful countermeasures include:

- deliberate reassessment;
- structured primary and secondary surveys;
- verbalising uncertainty;
- asking for alternative diagnoses;
- inviting challenge;
- reviewing response to treatment;
- using checklists where appropriate;
- involving senior expertise early;
- pausing before irreversible decisions when time permits.

A useful question is:

> "What else could explain this?"

Another is:

> "What would I expect to see if our current diagnosis were wrong?"

---

# 24. Safety Pauses

A safety pause can be useful before a major transition or irreversible intervention.

Examples:

- before emergency transfer;
- before leaving the resuscitation area;
- before major surgery;
- before induction of anaesthesia;
- before transferring a critically ill patient;
- when the diagnosis or plan has changed significantly.

A safety pause does not need to be lengthy.

It should establish:

- current problem;
- current physiology;
- immediate priorities;
- destination;
- responsible clinicians;
- outstanding risks.

---

# 25. Handover as a Governance Event

Handover is a high-risk transition.

Information can be lost when responsibility moves between teams.

The trauma leader should ensure that the receiving team understands:

- mechanism;
- injuries;
- physiological trajectory;
- major interventions;
- blood products;
- procedures;
- imaging;
- response;
- unresolved problems;
- current priorities;
- anticipated deterioration;
- outstanding investigations;
- definitive plan.

The handover should be structured and interactive.

The receiving clinician should have an opportunity to ask questions.

---

# 26. Closed-Loop Handover

A useful handover structure is:

> "This is what happened."

> "This is what we found."

> "This is what we did."

> "This is how the patient responded."

> "These are the remaining concerns."

> "This is what needs to happen next."

The receiving clinician should confirm understanding of critical issues.

This reduces the risk that responsibility is transferred without shared understanding.

---

# 27. Transfer of Responsibility

The leader should explicitly identify when responsibility changes.

For example:

> "Dr Ahmed is now leading the resuscitation."

or:

> "The intensive care team is now responsible for ongoing management."

The transfer should be clear rather than assumed.

Ambiguity about leadership can create dangerous gaps.

---

# 28. Continuity of Care

Trauma care often crosses multiple clinical environments:

- ambulance;
- emergency department;
- operating theatre;
- interventional radiology;
- intensive care;
- ward;
- specialist centre;
- rehabilitation.

Each transition introduces risk.

The leader should think beyond the current location.

The question is not:

> "Have we finished our part?"

The question is:

> "What does the patient need next, and have we transferred that information safely?"

---

# 29. Clinical Governance and Mortality Review

Major trauma deaths may undergo formal review.

The purpose should be to understand:

- what happened;
- whether care met expected standards;
- what contributed to the outcome;
- whether avoidable factors existed;
- whether system changes are required.

A mortality review should not be reduced to:

> "The patient died."

The clinically useful question is:

> "What can this case teach us about future care?"

---

# 30. Avoidability Is Not Binary

A death or complication may contain:

- clearly unavoidable elements;
- potentially modifiable elements;
- system weaknesses;
- areas of uncertainty.

The purpose of review is not necessarily to label the entire case as:

> "avoidable"

or

> "unavoidable."

Instead, identify specific opportunities for improvement.

For example:

- earlier activation of the trauma team;
- faster blood availability;
- clearer escalation;
- improved equipment;
- better handover;
- earlier transfer.

Specific learning points are more actionable than broad conclusions.

---

# 31. Morbidity and Mortality Learning

A strong case review should identify:

### What went well?

Recognise effective practice.

### What did not go well?

Identify problems without personal blame.

### Why did it happen?

Explore contributing factors.

### What should change?

Define a practical intervention.

### How will we know it changed?

Identify an outcome or audit measure.

Without the final question, learning may remain theoretical.

---

# 32. From Learning to Change

A governance system fails when incidents are repeatedly discussed but nothing changes.

For each significant learning point, consider:

**Problem → Intervention → Owner → Deadline → Measurement**

For example:

**Problem:** Delay obtaining emergency blood.

**Intervention:** Review blood-release process.

**Owner:** Emergency department and blood bank leads.

**Deadline:** Agreed locally.

**Measurement:** Time from activation to first unit.

This converts discussion into quality improvement.

---

# 33. Audit and Quality Improvement

Audit asks whether actual practice meets an agreed standard.

Examples of trauma audit measures may include:

- time to trauma team activation;
- time to CT;
- time to blood product administration;
- documentation quality;
- time to definitive haemorrhage control;
- time to specialist review;
- compliance with agreed pathways.

The exact metrics should reflect local priorities.

Not everything needs to be measured.

Measurement should support improvement rather than create unnecessary administrative burden.

---

# 34. The Danger of Meaningless Metrics

A metric is useful only if it helps answer a meaningful question.

For example:

> "How many trauma calls occurred?"

may describe activity.

It does not necessarily describe quality.

A more useful question might be:

> "How often was the major haemorrhage pathway activated within the expected timeframe when predefined criteria were present?"

Good governance measures what matters.

---

# 35. Training and Competence

Clinical governance includes maintaining competence.

Trauma team members require:

- knowledge;
- technical skills;
- communication skills;
- teamwork;
- decision-making;
- situational awareness;
- familiarity with local systems.

Training should not focus only on procedures.

The team should also rehearse:

- leadership;
- escalation;
- communication;
- role allocation;
- equipment problems;
- unexpected deterioration;
- transfer;
- major haemorrhage;
- difficult airway;
- cardiac arrest.

---

# 36. Simulation as a Governance Tool

Simulation can reveal weaknesses that routine clinical care may hide.

A simulation may expose:

- unclear leadership;
- poor role allocation;
- inaccessible equipment;
- communication problems;
- delays in escalation;
- unfamiliarity with protocols;
- poor handover.

The purpose of simulation is not simply to test individuals.

It can test the system.

After simulation, ask:

> "What did the environment make difficult?"

and:

> "What should we change before the next real patient?"

---

# 37. Equipment Governance

A trauma team leader should not assume that equipment will always be available and functional.

Important equipment should be:

- accessible;
- checked;
- maintained;
- familiar to staff;
- appropriately stocked.

Examples include:

- airway equipment;
- suction;
- oxygen;
- chest decompression equipment;
- haemorrhage control equipment;
- blood warming systems;
- ultrasound;
- monitoring equipment;
- transfer equipment.

Equipment failure during a resuscitation should trigger both immediate problem-solving and, where appropriate, governance review.

---

# 38. The Environment Is Part of the System

The physical environment affects performance.

Potential hazards include:

- poor lighting;
- excessive noise;
- crowding;
- blocked access;
- unclear equipment storage;
- inadequate space;
- interruptions;
- poor visibility of monitors.

A leader who repeatedly encounters the same environmental problem should consider whether it requires system-level action.

---

# 39. Staffing and Workload

Understaffing increases risk.

So does inappropriate allocation of experienced staff.

The leader should recognise when the workload exceeds available capacity.

Possible responses include:

- calling additional staff;
- escalating to senior clinicians;
- reallocating tasks;
- reducing non-essential activity;
- requesting specialist assistance;
- arranging transfer.

The correct response to excessive workload is not simply:

> "Work faster."

It is:

> "Increase capacity or reduce competing demands."

---

# 40. Fatigue and Performance

Fatigue can impair:

- attention;
- working memory;
- reaction time;
- decision-making;
- communication;
- emotional regulation.

A governance-focused team recognises fatigue as a system risk rather than simply a personal weakness.

During prolonged resuscitations, appropriate delegation and rotation may reduce performance deterioration.

---

# 41. The Second Victim Concept

A serious adverse event can affect clinicians emotionally and professionally.

Clinicians may experience:

- guilt;
- anxiety;
- loss of confidence;
- anger;
- shame;
- fear of disciplinary consequences;
- intrusive recollection of the event.

Supporting staff after significant incidents is part of a healthy safety culture.

Support does not mean avoiding accountability.

Both can exist together.

---

# 42. Professionalism After an Error

After an error, the leader should avoid:

- humiliation;
- public blame;
- speculation;
- premature conclusions;
- defensive behaviour;
- hiding information.

Instead:

- protect the patient;
- establish facts;
- support staff;
- escalate appropriately;
- document accurately;
- investigate systematically;
- identify learning;
- implement improvement.

---

# 43. Just Culture

A just culture attempts to distinguish between:

- human error;
- at-risk behaviour;
- reckless behaviour;
- system failure.

Human error generally requires:

- support;
- redesign;
- education;
- system improvement.

At-risk behaviour may require:

- coaching;
- clarification of expectations;
- removal of incentives for unsafe practice.

Reckless behaviour may require:

- formal investigation;
- appropriate professional action.

The purpose is to respond proportionately.

---

# 44. Governance During High-Pressure Resuscitation

Governance principles still apply during extreme emergencies.

The leader should maintain:

- clear leadership;
- role clarity;
- communication;
- reassessment;
- escalation;
- documentation;
- appropriate prioritisation.

However, governance should never become an excuse to delay life-saving treatment.

In a time-critical emergency:

> Treat the patient first.

Then document, communicate and review appropriately.

---

# 45. Balancing Speed and Safety

Trauma care frequently involves a tension between:

**speed**

and

**safety.**

The solution is not to choose one universally.

The leader must identify where speed is essential and where a brief safety pause prevents a larger error.

Examples:

- delaying decompression of a tension pneumothorax may be dangerous;
- spending several minutes completing unnecessary documentation during active exsanguination may be inappropriate;
- confirming patient identity before a non-immediate procedure may be essential;
- confirming blood product compatibility according to emergency protocols remains important.

Good leadership recognises the difference.

---

# 46. The Governance Mindset

A useful mental model is:

> **Safe care → Reliable systems → Learning → Improvement**

The trauma leader contributes to all four.

### Safe care

Do the right thing for the patient now.

### Reliable systems

Ensure the process supports safe performance.

### Learning

Understand what happened.

### Improvement

Change the system where necessary.

---

# 47. Governance Questions for the Team Leader

At the end of a major trauma case, the leader should be able to answer:

### Clinical

- Was the patient appropriately assessed?
- Were immediate threats identified?
- Were priorities appropriate?
- Was deterioration recognised?

### Leadership

- Was there clear leadership?
- Were roles appropriate?
- Was workload distributed effectively?

### Communication

- Were critical instructions understood?
- Were concerns encouraged?
- Was handover effective?

### Escalation

- Was senior help requested appropriately?
- Were specialist services involved early enough?

### Safety

- Were there equipment problems?
- Were there medication or identification issues?
- Were there communication failures?

### Governance

- Does the case require incident reporting?
- Does it require formal review?
- Is there a learning point?
- Does a system need to change?

---

# 48. The Five-Minute Governance Review

When time permits after a major case, the leader can conduct a brief review.

### 1. What happened?

One-sentence summary.

### 2. What went well?

Identify effective practice.

### 3. What worried us?

Identify safety concerns.

### 4. What needs follow-up?

Identify unresolved clinical or organisational issues.

### 5. What should change?

Identify one or two actionable improvements.

This can be followed by a more formal review when required.

---

# 49. The Governance Trigger List

Consider formal escalation or review when there is:

- unexpected death;
- unexpected deterioration;
- major complication;
- significant delay;
- medication error;
- blood product incident;
- equipment failure;
- communication failure;
- unexpected transfer;
- serious diagnostic error;
- failure of escalation;
- repeated deviation from an agreed pathway;
- significant near miss.

Local policy determines the exact reporting requirements.

When uncertain, seek senior advice.

---

# 50. Near Misses Matter

A near miss is an event that could have caused harm but did not.

Near misses are valuable because they reveal system weaknesses without requiring patient harm to occur.

Examples:

- wrong medication identified before administration;
- incorrect blood product identified before transfusion;
- incorrect patient identified before procedure;
- missed injury recognised before transfer;
- equipment failure identified before use.

A team should not wait for harm before improving the system.

---

# 51. The Leader Should Ask "Why Did We Get Lucky?"

Sometimes a case ends safely despite a significant vulnerability.

For example:

A wrong medication is prepared but noticed at the last moment.

The patient remains stable despite delayed escalation.

A critical piece of equipment fails but another device is immediately available.

The correct response is not:

> "No harm occurred, so nothing happened."

Instead:

> "What prevented harm this time?"

That question may reveal an important safety barrier.

---

# 52. Safety Barriers

Patient safety depends on multiple barriers.

Examples include:

- protocols;
- checklists;
- identification systems;
- trained staff;
- supervision;
- monitoring;
- equipment checks;
- communication;
- pharmacy checks;
- blood bank checks.

When several barriers fail simultaneously, harm becomes more likely.

The leader should therefore consider the system rather than a single point of failure.

---

# 53. The Swiss Cheese Concept

Major adverse events may occur when several weaknesses align.

For example:

- unclear handover;
- staffing shortage;
- equipment problem;
- communication failure;
- delayed escalation.

Each weakness may be tolerable individually.

Together they can produce serious harm.

The governance response is to strengthen multiple barriers rather than rely on one person to prevent every error.

---

# 54. Governance and Team Culture

A high-performing trauma team develops a culture in which:

- concerns are welcomed;
- uncertainty is acceptable;
- escalation is encouraged;
- errors are reported;
- learning is shared;
- standards are clear;
- feedback is constructive;
- responsibility is understood.

Culture is created by repeated behaviour.

The team leader contributes to this culture every time they lead a resuscitation.

---

# 55. The Leader's Language Shapes Culture

Compare:

> "Why did you do that?"

with:

> "Talk me through what you saw."

The second question seeks understanding before judgement.

Compare:

> "You should have known better."

with:

> "What made this difficult to recognise?"

The second question may reveal a system problem.

This does not mean avoiding accountability.

It means obtaining accurate information before reaching conclusions.

---

# 56. Governance and Leadership Under Uncertainty

Trauma leadership rarely provides complete information.

A responsible leader should be able to say:

> "We do not know yet."

But uncertainty should be followed by action.

For example:

> "We do not yet know the exact source of bleeding. The patient is unstable, so we will continue haemorrhage control and activate the appropriate pathway while we clarify the source."

This is better than pretending certainty.

---

# 57. Transparency

Transparency means communicating important information honestly and appropriately.

This includes:

- acknowledging uncertainty;
- acknowledging complications;
- communicating significant changes;
- reporting safety events;
- documenting important decisions.

Transparency supports trust between:

- clinicians;
- patients;
- families;
- departments;
- organisations.

---

# 58. The Patient and Family

Governance ultimately exists to improve patient care.

When appropriate, patients and families should receive clear and honest communication about:

- the clinical situation;
- significant changes;
- important complications;
- ongoing management.

The exact requirements for disclosure following adverse events depend on local policy, professional guidance and applicable law.

The trauma leader should ensure that appropriate senior clinicians and institutional processes are involved when significant adverse events occur.

---

# 59. Governance Is Not Paperwork

The greatest mistake is to reduce clinical governance to:

- forms;
- reports;
- meetings;
- policies;
- audits.

These are tools.

The objective is safer care.

A useful test is:

> "Did this governance activity make patient care safer?"

If not, its value should be questioned.

---

# 60. The Trauma Team Leader's Governance Checklist

Before closing a major trauma episode, consider:

### Patient

- Is the patient physiologically stable enough for the next transition?
- Are major unresolved problems identified?
- Is there a clear definitive plan?

### Team

- Is responsibility clear?
- Has an appropriate handover occurred?
- Are staff aware of outstanding risks?

### Safety

- Were any incidents or near misses identified?
- Were equipment or system failures encountered?
- Were there medication, blood or identification concerns?

### Documentation

- Are major interventions documented?
- Are significant decisions documented?
- Are important times recorded where feasible?
- Is the current plan clear?

### Escalation

- Has the appropriate senior team been informed?
- Are further specialist services required?

### Governance

- Does the case require reporting?
- Does it require formal review?
- Is there an immediate system problem that should be escalated?

---

# 61. A Governance Conversation After the Case

A concise post-case discussion might sound like:

> "The patient is now transferred. Before everyone leaves, let's identify anything that could affect safety."

Then:

> "What went well?"

Pause.

> "What caused difficulty?"

Pause.

> "Did we have any near misses or system problems?"

Pause.

> "Is there anything that needs to be reported or escalated?"

Finally:

> "What is the one thing we should change before the next major trauma?"

This takes little time but can generate significant learning.

---

# 62. What Good Governance Looks Like

Good governance is visible in behaviour.

The team:

- recognises deterioration;
- escalates early;
- communicates clearly;
- documents important decisions;
- reports significant incidents;
- reviews difficult cases;
- learns from near misses;
- improves systems;
- supports staff;
- maintains professional accountability.

It is not perfection.

It is a commitment to continuously safer care.

---

# 63. The Leader's Responsibility After the Resuscitation

The end of the resuscitation is not necessarily the end of leadership.

The leader should consider:

- Does the patient need further escalation?
- Does the receiving team have all critical information?
- Are there unresolved clinical issues?
- Does the case require documentation or reporting?
- Does the team need a debrief?
- Does equipment need replacement?
- Did anything expose a system weakness?
- Is there a learning point that should be shared?

The leader should close the loop.

---

# 64. Closing the Governance Loop

A complete governance cycle is:

**Recognise → Stabilise → Communicate → Document → Report → Review → Learn → Change → Reassess**

The cycle should not stop at:

> "We discussed it."

It should reach:

> "We changed something."

And ideally:

> "We measured whether the change worked."

---

# 65. Practical Leadership Principle

> **If the system repeatedly relies on individual heroics to remain safe, the system is not yet safe.**

A strong trauma leader should therefore look beyond individual performance.

If a problem occurs repeatedly, ask:

- Is the process flawed?
- Is the equipment inadequate?
- Is staffing insufficient?
- Is training incomplete?
- Is communication unclear?
- Is the escalation pathway unreliable?

Fixing the system is often more powerful than telling individuals to try harder.

---

# 66. Final Mental Model

The trauma team leader should hold five governance principles in mind:

### 1. Protect the patient

Immediate clinical safety comes first.

### 2. Make responsibility clear

Everyone should know who is doing what and who is leading.

### 3. Speak up early

Concerns should be raised before they become crises.

### 4. Learn from what happens

Errors, near misses and difficult cases contain information.

### 5. Change the system

Learning has value only when it improves future care.

---

# 67. Final Summary

Clinical governance is not something that happens after the trauma team leaves the resuscitation room.

It is embedded in leadership.

The trauma team leader demonstrates governance by:

- recognising unsafe situations;
- maintaining clear accountability;
- encouraging challenge;
- escalating appropriately;
- documenting significant decisions;
- supporting safe handover;
- recognising incidents and near misses;
- participating in review;
- identifying system weaknesses;
- supporting staff;
- translating learning into improvement.

The strongest leader is not the person who appears never to make an error.

It is the person who creates a system in which errors are detected early, concerns can be voiced, deterioration is recognised, responsibility is clear, and the team learns continuously.

> **Good trauma leadership protects the patient today.
> Good clinical governance makes the next patient safer tomorrow.**

---

# Chapter Take-Home Points

- Clinical governance is part of clinical leadership.
- Accountability is not the same as blame.
- Every clinician retains individual professional responsibility.
- The team leader coordinates overall team accountability.
- System failures should be recognised as system problems.
- Psychological safety allows team members to challenge unsafe decisions.
- Leaders should actively invite concerns.
- Changing a plan when new information appears is a strength.
- Important clinical decisions should be documented.
- Handover is a patient-safety and governance event.
- Escalation should occur before available resources are exhausted.
- Protocols support care but do not replace clinical judgement.
- Errors and near misses should be recognised and managed appropriately.
- Human factors contribute significantly to clinical error.
- Incident review should focus on learning and system improvement.
- A just culture distinguishes human error, at-risk behaviour and reckless behaviour.
- Audit is useful when it measures something meaningful.
- Simulation can identify weaknesses in both individuals and systems.
- Equipment, staffing and environment are governance issues.
- Staff involved in serious incidents may require support.
- A near miss is an opportunity to improve before harm occurs.
- Governance is incomplete unless learning leads to change.
- The ultimate purpose of governance is safer patient care.

---

# Final Principle

> **The trauma team leader is accountable not only for what the team does, but for how safely the system learns from what the team experiences.**
