# Chapter 2 — The First Minutes of Trauma Resuscitation

## Clinical Scenario

A 27-year-old man is brought to the resuscitation room following a high-speed motor vehicle collision.

The pre-hospital team reports a short period of reduced consciousness at the scene, increasing respiratory effort, persistent tachycardia, and concern for significant torso trauma.

The patient arrives speaking, but in short sentences. He appears distressed and pale.

The Trauma Team Leader is informed that the patient's blood pressure is currently preserved but trending downward.

The team is assembled and the patient is transferred to the resuscitation trolley.

At this point, the leader has limited information, incomplete diagnostic certainty, and a patient whose condition may deteriorate rapidly.

The first minutes therefore require more than simply beginning the primary survey.

They require the team leader to establish priorities, allocate tasks, identify immediately reversible threats, anticipate deterioration, and begin planning definitive management.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain why the first minutes of major trauma are time-critical.
2. Establish immediate priorities when diagnostic information is incomplete.
3. Coordinate parallel assessment and intervention without losing overall situational awareness.
4. Recognise when physiological change requires reprioritisation.
5. Delegate time-critical tasks clearly and assign responsibility for their completion.
6. Anticipate deterioration and prepare the resources required to manage it.
7. Recognise when resuscitation must transition towards definitive treatment.
8. Avoid common errors that create preventable delay during the first minutes of trauma resuscitation.

## Key Concepts

### The First Minutes Are an Active Resuscitation Phase

The first minutes of major trauma should not be viewed as a period of information gathering before treatment begins.

Assessment and treatment occur together.

The team should identify immediately life-threatening problems and intervene as they are recognised while continuing the structured assessment.

The Trauma Team Leader must therefore maintain two simultaneous perspectives:

- What is threatening the patient now?
- What is the patient likely to need next?

### Time Is a Clinical Variable

In major trauma, delay can allow physiological deterioration to progress beyond the point at which simple intervention is sufficient.

The importance of time does not mean that every action should be rushed.

Speed should not come at the expense of accuracy, coordination, or appropriate clinical judgement.

It means that the team should distinguish between actions that are immediately necessary, actions that can occur in parallel, and actions that can safely wait.

The leader should continually ask:

> **"What needs to happen now, what can happen simultaneously, and what can wait?"**

### The First Assessment Is Not a Fixed Plan

The initial assessment represents the patient's condition at one point in time.

Trauma physiology can change rapidly.

A patient who initially appears relatively stable may develop worsening haemorrhage, airway compromise, respiratory failure, or neurological deterioration.

The leader must therefore treat the initial assessment as a starting point rather than a final diagnosis or management plan.

Changes in physiology, examination findings, or response to treatment should trigger reassessment and reprioritisation.

### Parallel Processing

Major trauma frequently requires several actions to occur simultaneously.

While one clinician assesses the airway, another may assess breathing, another may establish vascular access, and another may prepare blood products or obtain additional information.

Parallel processing should remain coordinated.

It should not become uncontrolled activity.

The Trauma Team Leader must maintain a clear hierarchy of priorities while ensuring that important tasks have identifiable owners.

Parallel processing should therefore increase the speed of appropriate care without obscuring who is responsible for each critical action.

### Anticipation

The leader should anticipate the next likely clinical problem rather than waiting for deterioration to occur.

This may include preparing equipment, activating specialist support, arranging blood products, or identifying the likely destination for definitive treatment.

Anticipation is particularly important when the patient's current physiology suggests a trajectory towards deterioration.


## Practical Approach

### Before the Patient Arrives

The first minutes of trauma resuscitation begin before the patient enters the resuscitation room.

The Trauma Team Leader should use the available pre-hospital information to establish an initial working picture of:

- mechanism of injury;
- suspected injury pattern;
- physiological status;
- treatment already provided;
- response to pre-hospital interventions;
- and the likely need for definitive haemorrhage control, surgery, critical care, or transfer.

The leader should ensure that the appropriate team members, equipment, monitoring, blood products, airway equipment, and other resources are available according to the anticipated clinical needs.

The pre-arrival period should also be used to establish roles and communicate the initial priorities to the team.

The purpose is not to predict the final diagnosis.

It is to ensure that the team is prepared for the most dangerous and most time-critical possibilities.

### The First 30–60 Seconds

Once the patient arrives, the leader should establish an immediate overview while the team begins the structured assessment.

The leader should rapidly determine:

1. Is there an immediately life-threatening problem?
2. Is there major external haemorrhage?
3. Is the airway currently adequate?
4. Is ventilation and oxygenation adequate?
5. Is there evidence of significant circulatory compromise?
6. Is neurological status changing?
7. Does the patient appear to be deteriorating?

The leader should avoid becoming absorbed in a single examination finding or procedure.

The objective is to establish the immediate priorities while ensuring that the team continues parallel assessment and treatment.

### Establish Immediate Priorities

The initial priorities should be based on the combination of:

- immediate threats to life;
- physiological abnormality;
- mechanism of injury;
- suspected injury pattern;
- response to treatment;
- and trajectory.

The leader should distinguish between problems that require immediate intervention and those that can safely be assessed or managed in parallel.

A useful question is:

> **"What can kill this patient in the next few minutes, and what can we do about it now?"**

This question should help the team focus on reversible, time-critical pathology without losing sight of the broader assessment.

### Start Parallel Processing

Once immediate priorities have been established, the team should work on several critical tasks simultaneously.

For example:

- one clinician may assess and manage the airway;
- another may assess breathing and immediately treat a life-threatening chest problem;
- another may establish vascular or intraosseous access;
- another may obtain blood samples and prepare blood products;
- and another may obtain relevant background information or assist with monitoring.

The Trauma Team Leader should coordinate these activities rather than personally performing every task.

Parallel processing should remain structured.

Each important task should have an identifiable owner, and the leader should know when an update or reassessment is expected.

### Treat Life-Threatening Problems Immediately

A life-threatening problem that is sufficiently recognised should be treated without unnecessary delay.

The team should not wait for completion of the entire primary survey before intervening.

At the same time, treatment should remain integrated with continued assessment.

After a major intervention, the leader should ask:

> **"Did the patient respond as expected?"**

If the expected response does not occur, the leader should reconsider the working diagnosis, the effectiveness of the intervention, and whether another life-threatening problem is present.

### Monitor Response to Intervention

Treatment should produce an expected physiological or clinical effect when the underlying problem has been correctly identified and adequately addressed.

The leader should therefore actively monitor:

- blood pressure and heart rate;
- respiratory effort and oxygenation;
- mental status;
- peripheral perfusion;
- temperature;
- and other relevant physiological trends.

The response to treatment is itself diagnostic information.

Failure to improve should not simply lead to repetition of the same intervention.

It should prompt reassessment of the underlying problem and the overall strategy.

### Reassess and Reprioritise

Trauma resuscitation is dynamic.

The priority order established at the beginning may change as new information becomes available.

A patient who initially appears to have a predominantly respiratory problem may subsequently demonstrate evidence of major haemorrhage.

A patient who initially appears stable may develop rapidly worsening physiology.

The leader should therefore repeatedly ask:

- What has changed?
- Why has it changed?
- Is the current intervention working?
- Has a new life-threatening problem become apparent?
- Does the priority order need to change?

Reprioritisation is not a failure of the original plan.

It is an essential part of managing a changing clinical situation.

### Anticipate the Next Problem

The leader should continuously consider what is likely to happen next.

If the patient has suspected major haemorrhage, blood products and definitive haemorrhage-control resources may need to be prepared before cardiovascular collapse occurs.

If the airway is becoming progressively compromised, advanced airway equipment and appropriate expertise may need to be brought to the bedside before deterioration becomes critical.

If the patient is likely to require operative or interventional management, the relevant team should be activated early enough to avoid preventable delay.

Anticipation converts a reactive resuscitation into a proactive one.

### Plan Definitive Care

Resuscitation should proceed towards definitive management rather than becoming an endpoint in itself.

The leader should establish early whether the patient is likely to require:

- operating theatre;
- interventional radiology;
- critical care;
- specialist surgery;
- or transfer to another trauma facility.

The decision should take into account the patient's physiology, injury pattern, response to treatment, available resources, and local trauma pathways.

The key question is:

> **"What does this patient need next, and what needs to happen now to get them there safely?"**

Definitive care should be planned while resuscitation continues.

### Maintain a Shared Mental Model

As the resuscitation progresses, the leader should periodically summarise the situation to the team.

A concise summary may include:

- the major suspected problems;
- the patient's current physiological state;
- interventions already performed;
- response to those interventions;
- unresolved threats;
- and the next intended step.

For example:

> **"We have ongoing circulatory compromise with suspected torso haemorrhage. The airway is currently maintained. Blood is being given, we are reassessing the response, and we are preparing for definitive haemorrhage control."**

A shared mental model helps the team understand not only what is happening, but also what is expected to happen next.

### Practical Mental Model

The first minutes can be summarised as:

> **Recognise → Prioritise → Delegate → Intervene → Reassess → Reprioritise → Anticipate → Definitive Care**

This is not a rigid sequence.

Several steps occur simultaneously, and the cycle may need to restart whenever the patient's condition changes.

## Clinical Pearl

> **The first minutes of trauma resuscitation are not about completing tasks as quickly as possible. They are about making the right decisions early enough to change the patient's trajectory.**


## Practical Approach

### The First 30–60 Seconds

The first 30–60 seconds should establish the direction of the resuscitation.

The exact sequence will vary according to the mechanism, physiology, injuries identified, and resources available.

The Trauma Team Leader should nevertheless ensure that several critical functions begin immediately.

The leader should establish:

1. Who is leading the resuscitation.
2. Which clinicians are responsible for key tasks.
3. What the immediate life-threatening concerns are.
4. Which interventions need to begin now.
5. Which actions can occur in parallel.
6. What deterioration the team should anticipate.
7. What definitive treatment or destination may ultimately be required.

The objective is not to complete a fixed list within 60 seconds.

The objective is to prevent avoidable delay in recognising and treating threats to life.

### Before the Patient Arrives

The first minutes begin before the patient enters the resuscitation room.

When a pre-alert is received, the Trauma Team Leader should rapidly establish a working picture of:

- mechanism of injury;
- estimated time of arrival;
- physiological status;
- suspected injury pattern;
- interventions already performed;
- response to pre-hospital treatment;
- and any specific concerns identified by the pre-hospital team.

The leader should then determine whether the available team, equipment, blood products, monitoring, and specialist support are appropriate for the anticipated clinical needs.

Roles should be assigned before arrival whenever possible.

For example:

> **"I am leading. Sarah will manage the airway. Ahmed will assess breathing. Fatima will establish access and circulation. Please prepare blood products and the haemorrhage equipment."**

The precise allocation will depend on local staffing and the patient's anticipated needs.

The important principle is that responsibility should be explicit before critical tasks become urgent.

### The First Look

When the patient arrives, the leader should obtain an immediate overall impression while the team begins the structured assessment.

The first look should consider:

- appearance and level of consciousness;
- airway patency and ability to communicate;
- work of breathing;
- obvious major haemorrhage;
- skin colour and peripheral perfusion;
- and the overall trajectory suggested by the available history and physiology.

A patient who is speaking may have a currently patent airway, but this does not guarantee that the airway will remain adequate.

Similarly, a patient with a currently acceptable blood pressure may still have significant haemorrhage or evolving circulatory compromise.

The first impression should therefore generate hypotheses rather than premature diagnostic closure.

### Identify the Immediate Threats

The team should rapidly identify conditions that can cause death or irreversible deterioration if treatment is delayed.

These may include:

- catastrophic external haemorrhage;
- airway obstruction;
- severe respiratory compromise;
- tension pneumothorax;
- massive haemothorax;
- severe traumatic brain injury;
- and major internal haemorrhage.

The exact threats will depend on the patient's presentation.

The leader should not wait for completion of the entire primary survey before treating an immediately life-threatening problem that has already been recognised.

### Start Assessment and Treatment Together

Once the patient is in the resuscitation room, assessment and treatment should proceed together.

One clinician may assess the airway while another evaluates breathing and another establishes vascular access.

At the same time, the team may obtain monitoring, prepare blood products, control external haemorrhage, administer appropriate interventions, and obtain additional information.

The leader should coordinate these activities around the highest current priorities.

The question is not:

> **"Which step of the checklist are we on?"**

The more useful questions are:

> **"What can kill this patient now?"**

and

> **"What needs to happen next to prevent that outcome?"**

### Establish a Working Priority List

The leader should maintain a short, dynamic list of the patient's most important active problems.

For example:

1. Airway currently patent but potentially at risk.
2. Increasing respiratory distress with suspected chest injury.
3. Suspected haemorrhagic shock.
4. Possible pelvic injury.
5. Need for early definitive haemorrhage control.

This list is not a substitute for the primary survey.

It is a leadership tool that helps the team maintain a shared understanding of what matters most at that moment.

The leader should distinguish between problems that require immediate intervention, problems that require active monitoring, and problems that can safely be deferred.

The list should change as new information becomes available.

### Assign Ownership of Critical Tasks

A critical task should have a clearly identifiable owner.

Delegation should specify:

- who is responsible;
- what needs to be done;
- and, when appropriate, when the leader expects an update or reassessment.

For example:

> **"Sarah, obtain two large-bore IVs, send the trauma bloods, and tell me when access is established."**

This is more reliable than giving a general instruction to the room.

For time-critical tasks, the leader should also make clear what constitutes completion and when the result should be communicated back.

The leader should also avoid unnecessary micromanagement.

Once a competent team member has been assigned a task, the leader should maintain oversight while allowing that clinician to perform the task.

### Reassess as the Situation Changes

The initial plan should remain provisional.

A change in blood pressure, respiratory effort, oxygenation, mental state, perfusion, or response to treatment may represent a change in the underlying pathology.

When this occurs, the leader should pause long enough to establish what has changed and whether the priority order needs to change with it.

The team should not continue mechanically with the original plan when the patient's physiology is moving in a different direction.

A useful mental model is:

> **Recognise → Prioritise → Delegate → Intervene → Reassess → Reprioritise**

This cycle should continue throughout the early resuscitation.

Reassessment should occur after significant interventions and whenever the patient's physiology, examination, or response differs from what was expected.

### Begin Planning Definitive Care Early

The first minutes should also establish the likely direction of definitive management.

Depending on the patient's injuries and physiology, this may involve:

- operating theatre;
- interventional radiology;
- critical care;
- specialist surgical management;
- or transfer to another trauma facility.

The leader should avoid allowing diagnostic processes to become an endpoint.

The important question is:

> **"What does this patient need next, and what needs to happen now to get them there safely?"**

Definitive management should be anticipated while resuscitation is ongoing rather than considered only after the initial assessment has been completed.

Early planning does not require diagnostic certainty; it requires recognition of the most likely definitive pathway and timely preparation for it.
