# Chapter 5 — Circulation and Haemorrhage

## Clinical Scenario

A 29-year-old man is brought to the resuscitation room following a high-speed road traffic collision.

The pre-hospital team reports significant vehicle intrusion, abdominal and pelvic pain, and progressive tachycardia during transport.

On arrival, the patient is pale, anxious, and cool to touch.

Initial observations are:

- heart rate 138/min;
- systolic blood pressure 88 mmHg;
- respiratory rate 28/min;
- oxygen saturation 96% with supplemental oxygen;
- Glasgow Coma Scale 14/15;
- cool peripheries;
- delayed capillary refill.

There is no major external haemorrhage.

The Trauma Team Leader immediately considers the possibility of significant internal haemorrhage.

The team establishes vascular access and begins resuscitation while simultaneously searching for the source of bleeding.

The patient's blood pressure improves briefly but then falls again.

At this point, the leader must decide whether the patient is responding adequately to resuscitation, whether the major haemorrhage pathway should be activated, where the bleeding is most likely to be coming from, and how quickly definitive haemorrhage control must be achieved.

This is the central challenge of trauma circulation:

**Resuscitation must occur at the same time as recognition of the source and progression towards definitive haemorrhage control.**

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Recognise haemorrhagic shock early despite apparently preserved blood pressure.
2. Identify potential sources of occult traumatic haemorrhage.
3. Assess circulation using trends rather than isolated physiological measurements.
4. Coordinate vascular access, blood sampling, monitoring, and haemostatic resuscitation.
5. Recognise when major haemorrhage activation is appropriate.
6. Understand the principles of damage-control resuscitation.
7. Recognise the importance of temperature, coagulation, calcium, and metabolic abnormalities during major haemorrhage.
8. Assess response to resuscitation and recognise failure to respond.
9. Anticipate the need for definitive haemorrhage control.
10. Coordinate progression towards surgery, interventional radiology, critical care, or transfer.

## Key Concepts

### Circulation Is About Perfusion

Circulatory assessment should not be reduced to a single blood pressure measurement.

The leader should integrate:

- heart rate;
- blood pressure;
- pulse quality;
- peripheral perfusion;
- mental status;
- skin temperature;
- capillary refill;
- urine output where available;
- laboratory findings;
- mechanism and injury pattern;
- and response to resuscitation.

A patient may have significant blood loss before profound hypotension develops.

The leader should therefore recognise the overall physiological pattern rather than waiting for a specific blood pressure threshold.

### A Normal Blood Pressure Does Not Exclude Major Haemorrhage

Compensatory mechanisms can maintain blood pressure during the early stages of blood loss.

Tachycardia, peripheral vasoconstriction, anxiety, cool extremities, and altered mental status may provide important clues before severe hypotension develops.

The leader should therefore ask:

> **"Does this physiology fit a patient who is bleeding?"**

rather than:

> **"Is the blood pressure low enough to prove shock?"**

The trajectory is often more informative than a single value.

### Search for the Source

When major haemorrhage is suspected, the team should rapidly consider the principal potential sources.

These include:

- external bleeding;
- chest;
- abdomen;
- pelvis;
- retroperitoneum;
- long bones;
- and other soft-tissue injuries.

The mechanism and examination should guide the search.

A patient with blunt torso trauma, pelvic injury, tachycardia, and worsening perfusion should be assumed to have a significant internal source until the situation has been adequately explained.

### Resuscitation Does Not Control the Bleeding

Blood products and other resuscitative measures can support circulation, but they do not constitute definitive haemorrhage control.

The leader must therefore avoid a common cognitive error:

**treating temporary physiological improvement as resolution of the underlying problem.**

A transient improvement followed by deterioration should increase concern that active bleeding continues.

The key questions are:

- Is bleeding continuing?
- Is the intervention effective?
- Is the patient responding as expected?
- Where is the blood being lost?
- What is the fastest appropriate route to definitive haemorrhage control?

### The Response to Resuscitation Is Diagnostic Information

Response to treatment should be continuously assessed.

A patient who improves and remains stable may have a different management pathway from a patient who remains unstable despite appropriate initial resuscitation.

The leader should therefore repeatedly compare:

**Expected response → Observed response → Revised assessment**

Failure to respond should trigger reconsideration of:

- the diagnosis;
- the source of haemorrhage;
- the adequacy of haemorrhage control;
- the adequacy of resuscitation;
- and the need for immediate definitive intervention.

### Major Haemorrhage Requires a Team Response

Severe traumatic bleeding is rarely managed effectively by one clinician working in isolation.

The response may require simultaneous involvement of:

- trauma clinicians;
- emergency nursing staff;
- transfusion laboratory staff;
- anaesthesia;
- surgery;
- interventional radiology;
- critical care;
- operating theatre personnel;
- and transfer or retrieval services.

The Trauma Team Leader should activate the appropriate resources early rather than waiting until physiological collapse makes coordination more difficult.

NICE recommends using physiological criteria that include haemodynamic status and response to initial volume resuscitation when deciding whether to activate a major haemorrhage protocol, rather than relying on a single risk score at one time point. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

### Haemorrhage Control Is the Priority

The overall objective is not simply to restore a normal blood pressure.

The objective is to restore effective circulation while stopping the source of bleeding.

This may require:

- direct pressure;
- haemostatic measures;
- tourniquet use when indicated;
- pelvic stabilisation when appropriate;
- operative control;
- interventional radiology;
- or other definitive haemorrhage-control techniques.

NICE recommends rapid progression towards haemorrhage control and a restrictive approach to volume resuscitation until definitive control has been achieved in patients with active bleeding. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

## Practical Approach

### Recognise the Pattern Early

The leader should recognise the combination of:

- mechanism;
- injury pattern;
- physiological abnormalities;
- examination findings;
- and trajectory.

Possible warning signs include:

- persistent tachycardia;
- narrowing pulse pressure;
- hypotension;
- cool peripheries;
- delayed capillary refill;
- altered mental status;
- increasing respiratory rate;
- worsening metabolic or laboratory abnormalities;
- and transient or absent response to resuscitation.

No single finding should be interpreted in isolation.

The important question is whether the overall picture is consistent with significant blood loss.

### Control External Haemorrhage Immediately

Visible life-threatening external haemorrhage should be controlled without unnecessary delay.

Appropriate measures may include:

- direct pressure;
- wound packing or haemostatic dressings where appropriate;
- tourniquet application for life-threatening limb haemorrhage when direct pressure is inadequate;
- and escalation to definitive surgical control when required.

NICE recommends direct pressure for external haemorrhage and tourniquet use when direct pressure fails to control life-threatening bleeding from major limb trauma. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

The leader should ensure that the intervention has a clearly identified owner and that its effectiveness is reassessed.

### Establish Reliable Vascular Access

The team should establish appropriate vascular access rapidly.

This may include:

- large-bore peripheral intravenous access;
- additional intravenous access when required;
- or intraosseous access when peripheral access cannot be established promptly.

NICE recommends peripheral intravenous access for major trauma and consideration of intraosseous access when peripheral access fails. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

The leader should avoid allowing repeated unsuccessful attempts to consume valuable time.

A useful leadership instruction is:

> **"If peripheral access is not obtained promptly, move to the agreed alternative."**

### Send Blood Samples Early

Blood should be sent early according to the local major haemorrhage pathway.

Depending on the local protocol and clinical circumstances, this may include:

- full blood count;
- coagulation studies;
- fibrinogen;
- blood group and crossmatch;
- biochemical testing;
- blood gas analysis;
- lactate;
- ionised calcium;
- and other tests required to guide ongoing resuscitation.

The purpose is not simply to collect laboratory data.

The results should help the team understand:

- severity of physiological disturbance;
- evolving coagulopathy;
- adequacy of resuscitation;
- and requirements for ongoing haemostatic support.

### Activate the Major Haemorrhage Pathway

The decision to activate the major haemorrhage pathway should be based on the overall clinical picture.

The leader should consider:

- haemodynamic status;
- suspected ongoing bleeding;
- response to initial resuscitation;
- mechanism and injury pattern;
- anticipated transfusion requirement;
- and the likely need for urgent definitive haemorrhage control.

The team should know:

- who has activated the pathway;
- who is responsible for obtaining blood products;
- who communicates with the laboratory;
- who monitors the patient;
- and who is responsible for reassessment.

A major haemorrhage protocol should be locally defined and rehearsed.

The Trauma Team Leader should understand the local protocol rather than attempting to improvise a transfusion strategy during a crisis.

### Use Blood Components Appropriately

In major traumatic haemorrhage, resuscitation should focus on restoring circulating volume while supporting haemostasis.

Blood component strategies vary between institutions and should follow the local major haemorrhage protocol.

NICE recommends an initial fixed-ratio approach for blood components in active bleeding, followed by transition to laboratory-guided therapy at the earliest opportunity. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

The European guideline similarly emphasises structured, protocolised management of major traumatic bleeding and trauma-induced coagulopathy. :contentReference[oaicite:6]{index=6}

The leader should therefore know:

- what blood components are immediately available;
- how emergency blood is accessed;
- how the local protocol is activated;
- when laboratory-guided adjustment occurs;
- and who communicates with transfusion services.

### Avoid Excessive Crystalloid Resuscitation

Large volumes of crystalloid can dilute clotting factors and contribute to tissue oedema and other complications.

In patients with active bleeding, NICE recommends a restrictive approach to volume resuscitation until definitive haemorrhage control has been achieved and recommends blood components rather than crystalloids in hospital patients with active bleeding. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}

The practical leadership principle is:

> **Support circulation while moving rapidly towards haemorrhage control.**

The exact resuscitation target must take into account the clinical situation, including the presence and severity of traumatic brain injury.

### Consider Tranexamic Acid Early

Tranexamic acid should be considered early in patients with major trauma and active or suspected active bleeding according to current guidance and local protocol.

NICE recommends intravenous tranexamic acid as soon as possible in major trauma with active or suspected active bleeding and advises against routine intravenous administration more than three hours after injury unless there is evidence of hyperfibrinolysis. :contentReference[oaicite:8]{index=8}

The important leadership principle is timing.

The team should not allow avoidable delay in a treatment whose potential benefit is time-dependent.

### Prevent the Physiological Spiral

Major haemorrhage can produce a progressively worsening physiological state.

The leader should actively consider:

- hypothermia;
- acidosis;
- coagulopathy;
- hypocalcaemia;
- and ongoing tissue hypoperfusion.

These abnormalities can reinforce one another and make haemorrhage increasingly difficult to control.

The team should therefore:

- minimise heat loss;
- use appropriate warming measures;
- monitor relevant laboratory and physiological parameters;
- replace blood components according to the local protocol;
- and correct important metabolic abnormalities according to local guidance.

The objective is not simply to normalise laboratory numbers.

The objective is to prevent physiological deterioration from becoming self-sustaining.

### Recognise Pelvic Haemorrhage

Pelvic injury should remain an important consideration in blunt high-energy trauma.

Potential clues include:

- high-energy mechanism;
- pelvic pain or deformity;
- unexplained shock;
- lower abdominal or perineal findings;
- and persistent haemodynamic instability without an obvious external source.

When pelvic haemorrhage is suspected, the team should use appropriate pelvic stabilisation according to local trauma protocols.

NICE recommends a purpose-made pelvic binder when active bleeding is suspected from a pelvic fracture following blunt high-energy trauma. :contentReference[oaicite:9]{index=9}

The leader should simultaneously consider the need for definitive haemorrhage control rather than treating the binder as definitive treatment.

### Do Not Forget the Chest

Thoracic haemorrhage can produce both respiratory and circulatory compromise.

The leader should consider:

- massive haemothorax;
- tension pneumothorax;
- major vascular injury;
- and other thoracic sources.

The clinical picture should determine the urgency of intervention.

In a haemodynamically unstable patient with suspected life-threatening thoracic pathology, treatment should not be unnecessarily delayed for imaging.

### Consider Abdominal and Retroperitoneal Sources

Major abdominal or retroperitoneal bleeding may have few obvious external signs.

The leader should integrate:

- mechanism;
- abdominal examination;
- pelvic findings;
- haemodynamic response;
- ultrasound where appropriate;
- and other available information.

A negative FAST examination does not exclude intra-abdominal or retroperitoneal haemorrhage. NICE specifically warns against using a negative FAST to exclude these sources. :contentReference[oaicite:10]{index=10}

The absence of a positive imaging finding should therefore not override a convincing clinical picture of ongoing haemorrhage.

### Reassess After Every Major Intervention

After a major intervention, the leader should ask:

> **"What changed?"**

and:

> **"Was the response what we expected?"**

Reassessment should include:

- heart rate;
- blood pressure;
- pulse quality;
- peripheral perfusion;
- mental status;
- respiratory status;
- temperature;
- blood gas and laboratory trends where available;
- and evidence of ongoing blood loss.

The response should be interpreted as a trend.

A single improved measurement does not necessarily indicate successful resuscitation.

### Recognise Failure to Respond

Failure to respond to initial resuscitation should increase concern for ongoing uncontrolled haemorrhage or another major life-threatening problem.

Possible explanations include:

- continued bleeding;
- incorrect identification of the bleeding source;
- inadequate haemorrhage control;
- insufficient resuscitation;
- cardiac or obstructive causes of shock;
- or multiple simultaneous injuries.

The leader should avoid simply repeating the same intervention.

The appropriate response is:

**Reassess → Reidentify the problem → Reprioritise → Escalate.**

### Decide When Imaging Is Appropriate

Imaging should answer a clinical question and should not delay definitive treatment in an unstable patient who requires immediate intervention.

In suspected haemorrhage with haemodynamic instability and inadequate response to resuscitation, NICE recommends limiting diagnostic imaging to the minimum required to direct intervention. Patients who are responding to resuscitation or who have normal haemodynamic status may proceed to CT when appropriate. :contentReference[oaicite:11]{index=11}

The leader should therefore distinguish between:

- **imaging that will change immediate management;**
- **imaging that can safely wait;**
- and **imaging that adds information without changing the immediate pathway.**

### Progress Towards Definitive Haemorrhage Control

The resuscitation room should not become the final destination for a patient who requires haemorrhage control.

Depending on the source and physiological state, definitive management may include:

- operating theatre;
- interventional radiology;
- endovascular treatment;
- specialist surgical intervention;
- critical care;
- or transfer to a higher-level trauma facility.

NICE recommends damage-control surgery for haemodynamically unstable patients who do not respond to volume resuscitation, while definitive surgery may be considered in selected patients who are responding and is recommended when haemodynamic status is normal. :contentReference[oaicite:12]{index=12}

The leader should identify the likely pathway early.

### Damage-Control Resuscitation

Damage-control resuscitation is a strategy designed for patients with severe traumatic haemorrhage and evolving physiological derangement.

Its principles include:

- rapid recognition of haemorrhage;
- early haemorrhage control;
- appropriate blood-component resuscitation;
- avoidance of unnecessary crystalloid loading;
- prevention and correction of hypothermia;
- attention to coagulation;
- correction of important metabolic abnormalities;
- and rapid transition to definitive haemorrhage control.

The exact implementation should follow the local trauma and major haemorrhage protocol.

The leader's responsibility is to ensure that the strategy is coherent and that individual interventions are not performed in isolation.

## Leadership During Major Haemorrhage

### Give the Team a Clear Situation Report

During major haemorrhage, the leader should periodically summarise the situation.

For example:

> **"This patient has ongoing haemodynamic instability with suspected pelvic and abdominal haemorrhage. Major haemorrhage protocol is active, blood is being administered, and we are preparing for definitive haemorrhage control."**

This allows the team to maintain a shared mental model.

### Allocate Specific Responsibilities

Important tasks should have identifiable owners.

For example:

- one clinician manages vascular access;
- one clinician coordinates blood products;
- one clinician monitors and reports physiology;
- one clinician performs the focused assessment;
- one clinician communicates with surgery or interventional radiology;
- and one clinician documents the resuscitation.

The leader should know the status of each critical task without personally performing all of them.

### Escalate Early

A patient who is deteriorating despite resuscitation may require resources that take time to mobilise.

The leader should therefore activate:

- surgical support;
- anaesthesia;
- interventional radiology;
- operating theatre;
- critical care;
- blood bank;
- and transfer pathways

before physiological collapse makes mobilisation more difficult.

Anticipation is a core component of haemorrhage leadership.

### Know When to Stop Diagnosing

A common failure mode is continuing diagnostic assessment while the patient is becoming progressively unstable.

The leader should recognise when the clinical question has effectively become:

> **"How do we control the bleeding now?"**

rather than:

> **"What is the exact diagnosis?"**

Diagnostic precision remains important, but it must be balanced against the time sensitivity of uncontrolled haemorrhage.

## Reassessment Framework

The following mental model can be used throughout haemorrhage resuscitation:

> **Recognise → Control → Resuscitate → Reassess → Identify Source → Escalate → Definitive Haemorrhage Control**

This is not a rigid sequence.

Several elements occur simultaneously.

For example:

- haemorrhage control may begin while vascular access is established;
- blood products may be administered while the bleeding source is investigated;
- surgery may be mobilised while resuscitation continues;
- and reassessment should occur continuously.

The leader should repeatedly ask:

1. Is the patient still bleeding?
2. Is the patient responding?
3. Are we controlling the source?
4. Are our resuscitative measures supporting haemostasis?
5. What physiological complication is developing?
6. What resource will be needed next?
7. What is the definitive destination?

## Common Leadership Errors

### Waiting for Profound Hypotension

A patient can lose a substantial volume of blood before severe hypotension develops.

**Better approach:**

Interpret the complete physiological picture and trajectory.

### Treating Blood Pressure Rather Than Haemorrhage

Temporary restoration of blood pressure does not prove that bleeding has stopped.

**Better approach:**

Treat circulation while pursuing definitive haemorrhage control.

### Repeating the Same Intervention Without Reassessment

Failure to improve should trigger reconsideration.

**Better approach:**

Ask why the expected response has not occurred.

### Delaying Major Haemorrhage Activation

Waiting until catastrophic deterioration may make coordination more difficult.

**Better approach:**

Use the patient's physiology and response to resuscitation to activate the local pathway early when indicated.

### Excessive Crystalloid Administration

Large volumes of crystalloid can contribute to dilution and physiological deterioration.

**Better approach:**

Follow the local haemorrhage protocol and move rapidly towards blood-component resuscitation and definitive haemorrhage control.

### Treating Pelvic Stabilisation as Definitive Treatment

A pelvic binder can support early management but does not provide definitive control of all pelvic haemorrhage.

**Better approach:**

Use stabilisation while simultaneously planning definitive haemorrhage control.

### Sending an Unstable Patient for Unnecessary Imaging

Imaging can become a source of preventable delay.

**Better approach:**

Ask whether the investigation will change immediate management.

### Failing to Anticipate the Next Step

A deteriorating patient may require theatre, interventional radiology, critical care, or transfer.

**Better approach:**

Mobilise the next level of care before the patient reaches physiological collapse.

## Clinical Pearl

> **In traumatic haemorrhage, resuscitation buys time; definitive haemorrhage control saves the patient.**

The Trauma Team Leader should therefore continuously connect three questions:

> **Where is the patient bleeding?**

> **Is the patient responding to resuscitation?**

> **How quickly can definitive haemorrhage control be achieved?**

The strongest haemorrhage response is not the one that gives the most interventions.

It is the one that recognises ongoing bleeding early, coordinates resuscitation effectively, prevents physiological deterioration, and moves the patient to definitive haemorrhage control without avoidable delay.
