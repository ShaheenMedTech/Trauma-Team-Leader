# Chapter 11 — Responsibilities of the Trauma Team Leader

## Clinical Scenario

A 34-year-old man is brought to the resuscitation room following a high-speed road traffic collision.

The pre-hospital team reports:

- Significant vehicle intrusion
- Prolonged extrication
- Suspected pelvic injury
- Ongoing external haemorrhage from the lower limb
- Initial systolic blood pressure of 78 mmHg
- Heart rate 132/min
- Reduced level of consciousness
- Two large-bore intravenous cannulas inserted
- Pelvic binder applied
- One unit of blood administered pre-hospital

As the patient arrives, the room becomes active immediately.

One clinician is preparing the airway.
Another is attaching monitoring.
A nurse is attempting additional intravenous access.
A surgeon is examining the abdomen.
The blood bank is being contacted.
A radiographer is preparing the portable X-ray machine.
Someone asks whether the patient should go directly to CT.
Another clinician begins discussing the possibility of pelvic embolisation.

The trauma team leader must prevent the resuscitation from becoming a collection of simultaneous but disconnected actions.

The leader's responsibility is not to perform every procedure.

The leader must ensure that the team:

1. Recognises immediate threats.
2. Establishes priorities.
3. Allocates appropriate tasks.
4. Maintains situational awareness.
5. Reassesses the patient's response.
6. Escalates when current treatment is failing.
7. Makes timely disposition decisions.
8. Communicates the evolving plan.
9. Maintains team coordination.
10. Ensures safe transfer of care.

The central principle of this chapter is:

> The trauma team leader is responsible for the overall performance of the resuscitation, not for personally performing every intervention.

---

# Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

- Define the core responsibilities of the trauma team leader.
- Distinguish clinical responsibility from procedural responsibility.
- Establish priorities during major trauma resuscitation.
- Coordinate the primary survey without unnecessarily performing it personally.
- Allocate tasks according to competence and workload.
- Maintain a shared mental model within the trauma team.
- Recognise when the current resuscitation strategy is failing.
- Coordinate escalation to surgery, interventional radiology, critical care or another definitive pathway.
- Maintain effective communication during rapidly changing circumstances.
- Prevent fixation on individual injuries.
- Integrate physiological response with anatomical findings.
- Maintain situational awareness while receiving information from multiple team members.
- Coordinate transfer and handover safely.
- Recognise common leadership failures.
- Conduct a structured reassessment of team performance.

---

# 1. The Role of the Trauma Team Leader

The trauma team leader has overall responsibility for coordinating the trauma resuscitation.

This does not mean that the leader owns every clinical decision independently.

Major trauma is a multidisciplinary process.

The leader must integrate:

- Pre-hospital information
- Initial clinical assessment
- Physiological data
- Anatomical findings
- Point-of-care investigations
- Imaging
- Response to treatment
- Specialist input
- Available resources
- Definitive treatment options

The leader therefore acts as the central coordinating node of the resuscitation.

A useful mental model is:

> **Observe → Prioritise → Delegate → Reassess → Decide → Communicate.**

This cycle repeats continuously.

The leader should not become permanently attached to one task.

---

# 2. What the Trauma Team Leader Is Responsible For

The responsibilities of the trauma team leader can be grouped into several domains.

## 2.1 Clinical priorities

The leader must ensure that immediately life-threatening problems are identified and treated.

These include:

- Catastrophic haemorrhage
- Airway obstruction
- Threatened airway
- Tension pneumothorax
- Massive haemothorax
- Open pneumothorax
- Severe traumatic brain injury
- Major pelvic haemorrhage
- Critical extremity vascular injury
- Severe burns and inhalational injury
- Other immediately reversible causes of deterioration

The leader must continuously ask:

> What is most likely to kill this patient next?

---

## 2.2 Team coordination

The leader coordinates the activities of:

- Emergency physicians
- Trauma surgeons
- Anaesthesia clinicians
- Orthopaedic surgeons
- Neurosurgeons
- Radiologists
- Interventional radiologists
- Emergency nurses
- Operating theatre staff
- Radiographers
- Blood bank personnel
- Porters
- Pre-hospital clinicians
- Other specialists as required

The leader does not need to personally supervise every individual.

Instead, the leader ensures that the overall team is functioning toward the same objective.

---

## 2.3 Decision-making

The leader must make or coordinate decisions such as:

- Does the patient require immediate intervention?
- Is the airway secure?
- Is the patient responding to haemorrhage control?
- Is the patient sufficiently stable for imaging?
- Is CT appropriate?
- Is operative intervention required?
- Is interventional radiology required?
- Does the patient require transfer?
- Does the patient require critical care?
- Is further resuscitation likely to be beneficial?

The decision-making process should be dynamic.

A decision made five minutes ago may no longer be appropriate.

---

# 3. The Leader Must Establish Priorities

Major trauma creates competing priorities.

A patient may simultaneously have:

- A difficult airway
- Severe haemorrhage
- A suspected pelvic fracture
- Traumatic brain injury
- Long-bone fractures
- Hypothermia
- Coagulopathy

Not every problem can be addressed simultaneously.

The leader must determine the sequence of interventions.

A practical hierarchy is:

### Immediate threats

Treat problems that can rapidly cause death.

### Physiological instability

Identify why the patient remains unstable.

### Reversible causes

Prioritise interventions that can rapidly improve survival.

### Definitive management

Move the patient toward the appropriate definitive treatment pathway.

---

# 4. The Leader Should Not Become the Primary Operator

One of the most common errors in trauma leadership is procedural capture.

The leader begins performing a procedure and becomes absorbed in it.

Examples include:

- Intubation
- Chest drain insertion
- Central venous access
- Arterial line insertion
- FAST examination
- Wound management
- Fracture reduction

These procedures may be necessary.

However, if the leader becomes occupied with one procedure, several problems may develop.

The leader may fail to:

- Hear important information.
- Recognise deterioration.
- Coordinate blood products.
- Reassess other anatomical regions.
- Anticipate the next decision.
- Communicate with the operating theatre.
- Coordinate specialist involvement.
- Recognise failure of the current strategy.

The principle is:

> **If someone else can safely perform the procedure, the leader should generally lead rather than perform it.**

This does not mean the leader should never perform procedures.

In smaller teams or situations where no appropriately skilled clinician is available, the leader may need to become an operator.

However, this should be recognised as a resource limitation rather than the ideal leadership model.

---

# 5. Maintain a Global View

The leader should maintain a broad view of the patient and team.

A useful internal checklist is:

### Patient

- What is the physiology?
- What injuries are known?
- What injuries are suspected?
- What has changed?

### Treatment

- What has been done?
- What is currently being done?
- What intervention is next?

### Team

- Who is doing what?
- Is anyone overloaded?
- Is anyone duplicating another task?
- Is an important task unassigned?

### Resources

- Is blood available?
- Is the operating theatre aware?
- Is CT available?
- Is interventional radiology available?
- Is specialist help arriving?

### Destination

- Where is this patient going?
- What must happen before transfer?
- What is the definitive treatment pathway?

---

# 6. Assign Tasks Explicitly

Tasks should be allocated clearly.

Avoid:

> "Can someone get another IV?"

Prefer:

> "Sarah, please obtain large-bore intravenous access."

Avoid:

> "We need blood."

Prefer:

> "Blood bank: activate the major haemorrhage protocol and confirm when blood is available."

Avoid:

> "Someone call surgery."

Prefer:

> "Dr Ahmed, please contact the trauma surgeon and update them on the patient's physiology."

Specific task allocation reduces ambiguity.

---

# 7. Match Tasks to Competence

Delegation is not simply distributing work.

The leader must consider:

- Clinical competence
- Experience
- Procedural skill
- Familiarity with the environment
- Current workload
- Ability to recognise complications

A task should be assigned to the most appropriate available person.

The most senior clinician is not necessarily the best person for every task.

Likewise, delegation should not be based purely on hierarchy.

The objective is:

> **Right task, right person, right time.**

---

# 8. Avoid Unnecessary Micromanagement

A leader who attempts to control every action can become a bottleneck.

For example:

> "Connect the ECG now."

> "Move the monitor."

> "Take the blood pressure."

> "Give another IV fluid bolus."

> "Get another cannula."

Such instructions may be appropriate individually but become counterproductive if the leader is controlling minor operational details while major decisions remain unattended.

The leader should delegate routine tasks and retain responsibility for:

- Priorities
- Critical decisions
- Reassessment
- Escalation
- Coordination
- Disposition

---

# 9. Establish Closed-Loop Communication

Important instructions should be acknowledged.

Leader:

> "Administer one unit of packed red cells now."

Team member:

> "One unit packed red cells now."

Leader:

> "Confirmed."

This provides a closed communication loop.

For critical tasks, the leader should ideally receive confirmation of completion.

For example:

> "Prepare the patient for CT."

The team member should subsequently report:

> "CT is ready."

The leader can then incorporate that information into the next decision.

---

# 10. Maintain a Shared Mental Model

All important team members should understand:

- What is happening.
- What the major problem is.
- What has been done.
- What is being done.
- What will happen next.

A shared mental model reduces contradictory actions.

For example:

> "This patient remains hypotensive despite blood products and pelvic stabilisation. We suspect ongoing pelvic or intra-abdominal haemorrhage. We are continuing haemorrhage control while preparing for definitive intervention. CT will only occur if physiology permits."

This statement provides a common framework.

---

# 11. Continually Reassess the Patient

Trauma management is not a linear checklist.

The patient must be reassessed repeatedly.

After every major intervention, ask:

> Did the patient's physiology improve?

Examples:

After haemorrhage control:

- Has blood pressure improved?
- Has heart rate changed?
- Is perfusion improving?

After airway intervention:

- Is oxygenation adequate?
- Is ventilation adequate?
- Is the tube position confirmed?
- Has haemodynamic deterioration occurred?

After chest decompression:

- Has respiratory distress improved?
- Has oxygenation improved?
- Has ventilation improved?
- Is there persistent shock?

After blood products:

- Is haemodynamic status improving?
- Is bleeding controlled?
- Is ongoing transfusion required?

---

# 12. Recognise Treatment Failure

The leader must identify when the current strategy is not working.

A useful question is:

> **What did I expect to happen, and did it happen?**

If not, reconsider the diagnosis and strategy.

For example:

Expected:

> Blood pressure should improve after haemorrhage control and blood products.

Observed:

> Blood pressure remains critically low.

The leader should not simply continue the same treatment indefinitely.

Consider:

- Ongoing bleeding
- Inadequate haemorrhage control
- Incorrect diagnosis
- Additional injury
- Tension pneumothorax
- Cardiac tamponade
- Severe traumatic brain injury
- Medication effects
- Hypothermia
- Coagulopathy
- Inadequate resuscitation
- Measurement error

---

# 13. Manage Competing Clinical Priorities

Trauma leadership frequently involves competing priorities.

For example:

A patient has:

- Severe hypotension
- Reduced GCS
- Suspected intracranial haemorrhage
- Pelvic fracture

The leader must integrate the injuries rather than treating each in isolation.

A patient with severe traumatic brain injury may require urgent airway and physiological optimisation.

At the same time, uncontrolled haemorrhage may be the immediate cause of cardiovascular collapse.

The leader must continually determine:

> Which intervention has the greatest immediate effect on survival?

---

# 14. Recognise the Difference Between Anatomy and Physiology

An impressive anatomical injury does not always represent the immediate threat.

Conversely, a patient with relatively limited external injury may have catastrophic internal bleeding.

The leader should therefore integrate:

### Anatomy

What injuries are present?

### Physiology

How sick is the patient?

### Mechanism

What injuries are plausible?

### Response

How is the patient responding to treatment?

### Trajectory

Is the patient improving, stable or deteriorating?

This approach reduces fixation on visible injuries.

---

# 15. Control the Pace of the Resuscitation

The trauma leader controls the rhythm of the room.

The room should be:

- Urgent
- Structured
- Focused
- Deliberate

Urgency does not require chaos.

A useful distinction is:

> **Fast does not mean rushed.**

The leader should intervene when the team becomes:

- Too slow
- Too chaotic
- Too fragmented
- Overly focused on one problem
- Repetitive
- Uncertain about priorities

---

# 16. Anticipate the Next Problem

Strong trauma leadership is anticipatory.

The leader should think one or two steps ahead.

For example:

If the patient is receiving massive transfusion:

Anticipate:

- Hypothermia
- Hypocalcaemia
- Coagulopathy
- Potassium abnormalities
- Blood product requirements
- Ongoing haemorrhage
- Need for definitive haemorrhage control

If the patient requires emergency intubation:

Anticipate:

- Peri-intubation hypotension
- Difficult airway
- Cervical spine considerations
- Ventilation requirements
- Sedation
- Post-intubation monitoring

If the patient is going to CT:

Anticipate:

- Physiological deterioration
- Monitoring requirements
- Blood availability
- Escort personnel
- Need for immediate return to the resuscitation area

---

# 17. Know When to Escalate

The trauma leader should escalate early when:

- The patient remains unstable.
- The source of bleeding is unclear.
- Initial interventions fail.
- Specialist expertise is required.
- Operative management may be required.
- Interventional radiology may be required.
- Airway management is difficult.
- Neurosurgical intervention may be required.
- The patient requires transfer.
- Local resources are inadequate.

Early escalation is preferable to delayed escalation after physiological collapse.

---

# 18. Coordinate Specialist Involvement

Specialists should not be summoned merely because an injury exists.

The leader should communicate:

1. Mechanism.
2. Current physiology.
3. Major injuries or suspected injuries.
4. Interventions already performed.
5. Response to treatment.
6. Current concern.
7. Specific assistance required.

For example:

> "This is a 27-year-old male after high-speed collision. He remains hypotensive despite blood products and pelvic stabilisation. FAST is positive. We suspect ongoing intra-abdominal haemorrhage. We need the trauma surgeon immediately for operative haemorrhage control."

This is more useful than:

> "We have a trauma patient who needs surgery."

---

# 19. Coordinate Blood and Haemorrhage Control

In a bleeding patient, the leader should ensure that the team is addressing three linked problems:

### Recognition

Is major haemorrhage present?

### Resuscitation

Is the patient receiving appropriate blood products and physiological support?

### Definitive control

What will stop the bleeding?

These must occur in parallel.

The leader should not allow resuscitation to become an endless cycle of transfusion without definitive haemorrhage control.

A useful question is:

> **What is the next action that will actually stop the bleeding?**

---

# 20. Coordinate Imaging Strategically

Imaging is not automatically the next step.

The leader should consider:

- Physiological stability
- Suspected injuries
- Whether imaging will change management
- Availability
- Transport risk
- Need for immediate intervention

A critically unstable patient requiring immediate haemorrhage control should not be delayed unnecessarily for diagnostic imaging.

Conversely, a patient who is sufficiently stable may benefit substantially from CT to define injuries and guide definitive management.

The leader must therefore integrate imaging into the resuscitation strategy rather than treating it as an independent process.

---

# 21. Decide When to Move Beyond the Resuscitation Room

The trauma resuscitation room is not the destination.

Possible destinations include:

- Operating theatre
- Interventional radiology
- CT
- Intensive care
- High-dependency care
- Another trauma centre
- Specialist referral centre
- Ward in selected stable patients

The leader should continually ask:

> **What does this patient need next, and where can that treatment be delivered?**

---

# 22. Coordinate Transfer of Care

A transfer is a high-risk period.

The leader should ensure:

- Airway security
- Adequate ventilation
- Appropriate monitoring
- Vascular access
- Blood products if required
- Analgesia and sedation
- Immobilisation where appropriate
- Documentation
- Imaging availability
- Escort by appropriate personnel
- Receiving team awareness

Before departure, the leader should communicate:

- Mechanism
- Major injuries
- Current physiology
- Interventions
- Response
- Outstanding problems
- Current treatment
- Anticipated deterioration
- Required next steps

---

# 23. The Leader Must Protect Team Communication

During major trauma, communication may become noisy.

Multiple people may speak simultaneously.

The leader should create structure.

Useful phrases include:

> "One person at a time."

> "Let's pause for an update."

> "Give me the current physiology."

> "What has changed?"

> "What are we doing for haemorrhage control?"

> "Who is responsible for that?"

> "Confirm when complete."

> "Let's reassess."

These phrases are simple but powerful.

---

# 24. Conduct Periodic Team Updates

Long resuscitations benefit from structured pauses.

For example:

> "Team update."

Then summarise:

- Airway
- Breathing
- Circulation
- Neurology
- Injuries
- Treatment
- Response
- Current priorities
- Next destination

This restores the shared mental model.

---

# 25. Manage the Human Factors of the Room

The leader must recognise that clinicians are affected by:

- Stress
- Fatigue
- Noise
- Time pressure
- Cognitive overload
- Hierarchy
- Uncertainty
- Emotional responses

A clinician under pressure may:

- Miss information.
- Forget a task.
- Fixate on a problem.
- Fail to speak up.
- Duplicate another task.

The leader should therefore create an environment where concerns can be voiced.

Useful language:

> "If anyone sees a problem with the current plan, speak up."

And:

> "Does anyone see anything we are missing?"

---

# 26. Encourage Speaking Up

Every team member should be able to raise a safety concern.

For example:

> "Doctor, the blood pressure is falling."

The leader should respond:

> "Thank you. Confirm the current blood pressure."

Then act on the information.

A team in which junior staff are afraid to speak is an unsafe trauma team.

---

# 27. Resolve Conflicting Opinions

Trauma teams may contain clinicians with different opinions.

For example:

- CT versus operating theatre
- Intubation versus continued spontaneous ventilation
- Operative management versus interventional radiology
- Transfer versus local management

The leader should:

1. Clarify the clinical question.
2. Request concise evidence.
3. Reassess physiology.
4. Consider available resources.
5. Make or coordinate the decision.
6. Communicate the final plan.

A disagreement should not become a prolonged debate while the patient deteriorates.

---

# 28. Avoid Authority Bias

The leader should not automatically accept an opinion because it comes from the most senior clinician.

Likewise, a junior clinician may identify an important problem.

The relevant question is:

> **Is the information correct and clinically important?**

not:

> **Who said it?**

---

# 29. Maintain Documentation Awareness

Although the leader should not personally document every detail, the team must maintain an accurate record.

Important documentation includes:

- Time of arrival
- Mechanism
- Initial observations
- Major injuries
- Procedures
- Drugs
- Blood products
- Imaging
- Physiological response
- Specialist discussions
- Major decisions
- Transfer destination
- Time of transfer

Documentation should support clinical continuity and later review.

---

# 30. Know the Patient's Trajectory

A single set of observations is less useful than the trend.

The leader should know:

- Initial blood pressure
- Current blood pressure
- Initial heart rate
- Current heart rate
- Oxygenation
- Temperature
- Conscious level
- Blood gas trends
- Lactate where available
- Haemoglobin trends
- Response to interventions

The question is:

> **Is the patient getting better, worse, or simply being maintained temporarily?**

---

# 31. Identify the Point of Failure

When a patient deteriorates, the leader should rapidly determine where the current strategy has failed.

Consider:

### Airway

Is the airway patent and adequately protected?

### Breathing

Is there an immediately reversible thoracic cause?

### Circulation

Is haemorrhage controlled?

### Disability

Has neurological status changed?

### Exposure

Has an important injury been missed?

Then ask:

> What has changed since the last assessment?

---

# 32. Do Not Allow Diagnostic Completion to Replace Treatment

The team may be tempted to obtain more information before acting.

Examples:

- Waiting for a scan in an unstable patient.
- Repeating investigations that will not change immediate management.
- Delaying haemorrhage control while searching for anatomical certainty.

The leader must distinguish:

> **Information that changes management**

from:

> **Information that is interesting but not immediately actionable.**

---

# 33. Manage the Transition From Resuscitation to Definitive Care

The leader's role does not end when the immediate ABC problems are addressed.

The next phase must be coordinated.

For example:

> "The airway is secure, breathing is adequate, and haemorrhage remains the major issue. The patient is not stable for CT. We are proceeding to emergency haemorrhage control."

This represents a transition from:

**Resuscitation**

to:

**Definitive treatment.**

---

# 34. The Leader's Responsibility Is Continuous

Leadership begins before patient arrival and continues through:

- Team briefing
- Patient arrival
- Primary survey
- Secondary survey
- Resuscitation
- Imaging
- Definitive intervention
- Transfer
- Handover
- Debrief

The leader should not disappear from the process immediately after the initial survey.

---

# 36. Case-Based Discussion

## Case

A 42-year-old man is brought following a motorcycle collision.

On arrival:

- HR 138/min
- BP 72/46 mmHg
- RR 30/min
- SpO₂ 94%
- GCS 13
- Cool peripheries
- Obvious deformity of the pelvis
- Distended abdomen

A pelvic binder is already in place.

The team begins blood transfusion.

FAST is positive.

The surgeon suggests CT to identify the source of bleeding.

### Question

What is the leader's responsibility?

### Discussion

The leader should integrate the mechanism, physiology and findings.

The patient is profoundly unstable.

The combination of:

- Severe hypotension
- Tachycardia
- Positive FAST
- Suspected pelvic injury

raises immediate concern for life-threatening haemorrhage.

The leader should coordinate ongoing haemorrhage resuscitation and urgent definitive haemorrhage control rather than allowing diagnostic imaging to delay necessary treatment.

The leader should explicitly state the plan.

For example:

> "This patient is physiologically unstable with evidence of major haemorrhage. We will continue blood-based resuscitation and proceed to definitive haemorrhage control. CT is not the priority while the patient remains unstable."

The exact definitive pathway depends on the injury pattern, institutional capability and specialist assessment.

The important leadership principle is the integration of physiology with anatomy and the avoidance of unnecessary delay.

---

# 37. Case: Procedural Capture

A trauma team leader begins intubating a patient with a difficult airway.

The procedure becomes prolonged.

During the attempt:

- Blood pressure falls.
- The patient's abdomen becomes increasingly distended.
- The massive haemorrhage protocol has been activated.
- No one is clearly coordinating blood products.
- The surgeon is waiting for a decision.
- The team has not discussed definitive haemorrhage control.

### Leadership problem

The leader has become procedurally captured.

The airway is important, but the leader's attention has narrowed to one task.

### Corrective strategy

If another appropriately skilled clinician can safely take over airway management, the leader should return to overall coordination.

The leader should then:

- Reassess physiology.
- Confirm airway status.
- Coordinate haemorrhage control.
- Ensure blood products are being administered.
- Engage the appropriate surgical team.
- Determine definitive destination.

---

# 38. Case: Conflicting Information

The anaesthetist reports:

> "The blood pressure is improving."

The nurse reports:

> "The arterial pressure is still falling."

The monitor shows fluctuating values.

### Leader response

Do not immediately choose one statement.

Clarify:

> "Give me the current blood pressure from the arterial line and cuff."

Then assess:

- Signal quality
- Trend
- Heart rate
- Peripheral perfusion
- Mental status
- Other physiological markers

The leader should resolve the discrepancy before making a major decision based on unreliable information.

---

# 39. Case: Deterioration During Transfer to CT

A patient initially considered stable becomes hypotensive while being transported to CT.

The leader must reconsider the original plan.

The correct response is not:

> "We have already started the CT process."

Instead:

> "The patient is now unstable. Stop the transfer and return to the resuscitation area."

The destination decision is dynamic.

---

# 40. Common Leadership Errors

## Error 1 — Doing everything personally

The leader performs:

- Airway
- Procedures
- Ultrasound
- Lines
- Documentation

while neglecting coordination.

### Correction

Delegate appropriately.

---

## Error 2 — Failure to establish priorities

The team performs many tasks without a clear sequence.

### Correction

State the immediate priorities explicitly.

---

## Error 3 — Failure to reassess

The leader assumes that an intervention has worked.

### Correction

Ask:

> "What changed after that intervention?"

---

## Error 4 — Diagnostic fixation

The team focuses on identifying every injury before treating immediate threats.

### Correction

Treat immediate life threats while continuing diagnostic assessment.

---

## Error 5 — Failure to escalate

The leader waits for certainty before involving definitive services.

### Correction

Escalate early when serious injury or treatment failure is suspected.

---

## Error 6 — Poor delegation

Instructions are vague or addressed to the entire room.

### Correction

Name the person and task.

---

## Error 7 — Failure to communicate the plan

The team performs multiple actions without knowing the overall strategy.

### Correction

State the working diagnosis, priorities and next destination.

---

## Error 8 — Ignoring physiology

The leader focuses on anatomical findings while the patient remains unstable.

### Correction

Use physiology to guide urgency and disposition.

---

## Error 9 — Failure to anticipate

The leader reacts to events rather than preparing for them.

### Correction

Think one or two steps ahead.

---

## Error 10 — Allowing hierarchy to silence concerns

Junior staff recognise deterioration but do not speak.

### Correction

Create an environment where concerns are welcomed.

---

# 41. Practical Leadership Language

Effective trauma leaders often use short, unambiguous statements.

### To establish priorities

> "The immediate priority is haemorrhage control."

### To delegate

> "Dr Ahmed, manage the airway."

### To confirm

> "Confirm when the blood has arrived."

### To reassess

> "Let's reassess the physiology now."

### To challenge the plan

> "What is the evidence that the patient is stable enough for CT?"

### To anticipate

> "If the patient remains unstable, we will proceed directly to definitive haemorrhage control."

### To involve the team

> "Does anyone see a life threat we have missed?"

### To resolve confusion

> "Stop for a moment. I want the current situation clearly stated."

### To transition

> "The immediate resuscitation is complete. We are now moving to definitive management."

---

# 42. Leadership During Extreme Pressure

The most difficult trauma cases are often characterised by:

- Multiple simultaneous injuries
- Severe physiological instability
- Incomplete information
- Limited resources
- Conflicting opinions
- Time pressure
- Emotional stress

In these situations, the leader should simplify rather than complicate.

Return to:

1. What is killing the patient?
2. What has been done?
3. Did it work?
4. What must happen next?
5. Who is responsible?
6. Where is the patient going?

This provides a stable cognitive framework when the environment becomes chaotic.

---

# 43. The Leader as the Keeper of the Big Picture

Every team member sees a different part of the resuscitation.

The airway clinician sees:

- Airway
- Ventilation
- Oxygenation

The nurse sees:

- Access
- Drugs
- Blood products
- Monitoring

The surgeon sees:

- Injuries
- Operative possibilities

The radiographer sees:

- Imaging requirements

The leader must integrate all these perspectives.

The leader therefore acts as:

> **The keeper of the big picture.**

---

# 44. The Leader Should Know What Is Not Being Done

An advanced leader does not only ask:

> "What are we doing?"

They also ask:

> "What important thing are we not doing?"

Examples:

- No haemorrhage control strategy.
- No warming strategy.
- No definitive destination.
- No specialist escalation.
- No repeat assessment.
- No plan for deterioration.
- No clear handover.
- No blood product coordination.

This question can reveal omissions that are otherwise difficult to identify.

---

# 45. The Leader Must Protect Time-Critical Actions

Some trauma interventions are highly time sensitive.

The leader should prevent unnecessary delays caused by:

- Repeated discussions
- Unclear delegation
- Waiting for nonessential information
- Searching for equipment
- Poor communication
- Failure to escalate

The leader should ask:

> "What is delaying definitive treatment?"

Then remove that obstacle.

---

# 46. The Leader and Team Safety

Patient safety and team safety are linked.

The leader should ensure:

- Safe movement of the patient.
- Appropriate personal protective equipment.
- Safe handling of sharps.
- Safe blood administration.
- Appropriate radiation precautions.
- Safe transfer procedures.
- Adequate staffing.

A high-acuity environment should not become an excuse for unsafe practice.

---

# 47. When the Leader Should Step In Personally

There are situations where the leader may need to become an operator.

Examples include:

- No other appropriately skilled clinician is available.
- Immediate life-saving intervention is required.
- Delegation would cause unacceptable delay.
- The leader has the highest relevant procedural competence.
- The team is severely resource limited.

Even then, the leader should recognise the leadership cost.

If the leader becomes the operator, another clinician may need to assume coordination of the broader team.

This is particularly important in smaller trauma teams.

---

# 48. Leadership Handover

If leadership must change during a resuscitation, the transition should be explicit.

The outgoing leader should state:

- Current physiology
- Major injuries
- Treatment completed
- Response
- Current concerns
- Pending investigations
- Planned destination
- Immediate next actions

For example:

> "The patient remains hypotensive despite blood products. Pelvic injury is suspected and FAST is positive. The airway is secured. We are preparing for definitive haemorrhage control. The trauma surgeon is present and the operating theatre is being contacted."

The incoming leader should acknowledge the transfer of responsibility.

---

# 49. The Leader's End-of-Resuscitation Review

Before leaving the resuscitation area, ask:

### Patient

- Is the airway secure?
- Is ventilation adequate?
- Is haemorrhage controlled or actively managed?
- Is physiology acceptable for the next destination?

### Treatment

- What has been given?
- What procedures were performed?
- What remains outstanding?

### Injuries

- What injuries are known?
- What injuries remain suspected?

### Destination

- Where is the patient going?
- Who is receiving the patient?

### Communication

- Has the receiving team been informed?
- Has a structured handover occurred?

### Documentation

- Are major events documented?
- Are blood products and procedures recorded?

---

# 50. Key Takeaways

1. The trauma team leader is responsible for coordinating the overall resuscitation.
2. Leadership is different from procedural performance.
3. The leader should maintain the big picture.
4. Immediate life threats must be prioritised.
5. Tasks should be delegated explicitly.
6. Delegation should match competence and workload.
7. Critical instructions should use closed-loop communication.
8. The leader should continuously reassess the patient's response.
9. Failure to respond should trigger reconsideration of the diagnosis and strategy.
10. Physiology must be integrated with anatomy and mechanism.
11. Definitive haemorrhage control should not be delayed unnecessarily.
12. Imaging should support management rather than delay necessary treatment.
13. Specialist escalation should occur early when required.
14. The leader should anticipate the next problem.
15. Team members should be encouraged to speak up.
16. The leader should prevent procedural capture and diagnostic fixation.
17. Destination decisions must be dynamic.
18. Transfer and handover are part of trauma leadership.
19. The leader should know what is being done and what is not being done.
20. The ultimate responsibility is to ensure that the right patient receives the right intervention, from the right person, at the right time.

# 52. Final Principle

The trauma team leader does not need to be the fastest operator in the room.

The leader needs to be the person who can maintain clarity when everyone else is focused on individual tasks.

The leader must continually connect:

**Patient → Problem → Priority → Person → Procedure → Response → Destination.**

The most effective trauma leader therefore does not ask:

> "What can I do?"

The better question is:

> **"What does this patient need next, who is best placed to do it, and how will I know that it worked?"**

That is the essence of trauma team leadership.
