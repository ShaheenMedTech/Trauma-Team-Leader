# Chapter 8 — Disability, Neurological Assessment and Head Injury

## Clinical Scenario

A 29-year-old man is brought to the resuscitation room following a high-speed motorcycle collision.

He was initially found confused at the scene and had a brief period of reduced consciousness.

The pre-hospital team reports that his Glasgow Coma Scale has fluctuated during transport.

On arrival, he opens his eyes to voice, is confused in conversation, and obeys commands.

His GCS is 13/15.

He is haemodynamically stable after initial resuscitation, and his oxygenation is currently adequate.

There is a scalp laceration and evidence of facial trauma.

The pupils are initially equal and reactive.

The Trauma Team Leader recognises that the neurological assessment cannot be considered in isolation.

The patient's neurological state may be affected by traumatic brain injury, hypoxia, hypotension, hypoglycaemia, intoxication, medication, pain, or other systemic problems.

The leader therefore asks the team to establish a reliable neurological baseline while continuing to protect the patient's airway, oxygenation, circulation, and temperature.

A short time later, the patient's GCS falls to 10/15.

The leader must now determine whether this represents evolving intracranial pathology, an extracranial physiological problem, or both.

The key question is no longer simply:

> **"What is the patient's GCS?"**

It is:

> **"What has changed, why has it changed, and what needs to happen now?"**

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain the purpose of the disability assessment during trauma resuscitation.
2. Establish and document a reliable neurological baseline.
3. Perform a structured assessment of consciousness, pupils, motor function, and neurological trend.
4. Use the Glasgow Coma Scale appropriately without allowing the score to replace clinical judgement.
5. Recognise clinical features suggesting significant traumatic brain injury.
6. Identify potentially reversible extracranial causes of neurological deterioration.
7. Recognise signs of neurological deterioration and possible cerebral herniation.
8. Understand the importance of preventing secondary brain injury.
9. Integrate neurological assessment with the wider trauma resuscitation.
10. Recognise when urgent imaging, specialist review, critical care, or transfer is required.
11. Coordinate the team when neurological status changes during resuscitation.
12. Reassess the patient after interventions and whenever the clinical picture changes.

## Key Concepts

### Disability Is Part of the Primary Survey

The disability assessment is a rapid assessment of neurological function within the primary survey.

Its purpose is not to provide a complete neurological examination.

It is to identify immediately important neurological abnormalities, establish a baseline, recognise deterioration, and identify reversible physiological causes of impaired consciousness.

The assessment should occur alongside continued attention to:

- airway;
- breathing;
- circulation;
- oxygenation;
- perfusion;
- temperature;
- and glucose when clinically indicated.

A neurological abnormality should therefore never be interpreted in isolation from the patient's overall physiology.

### Neurological Assessment Is a Trend

A single GCS value provides only a snapshot.

The direction of change may be more clinically important than the absolute score.

The leader should therefore establish:

- the neurological state before arrival when available;
- the neurological state on arrival;
- the response to interventions;
- and the subsequent trend.

A patient whose GCS changes from 14 to 11 requires a different level of concern from a patient who remains consistently at 11 after a known severe injury.

The important information is not simply the number.

It is:

> **GCS → Components → Trend → Context → Cause → Action**

### The Leader Must Ask Why the Patient Is Abnormal

Reduced consciousness after trauma may result from:

- traumatic brain injury;
- hypoxia;
- hypotension;
- hypoperfusion;
- hypoglycaemia;
- intoxication;
- sedative or analgesic medication;
- seizures;
- post-ictal state;
- metabolic disturbance;
- or other systemic pathology.

A deteriorating neurological state should therefore trigger a rapid reassessment of the whole patient.

The leader should not automatically assume that every change in consciousness represents an intracranial haemorrhage.

At the same time, an intracranial cause should not be dismissed simply because an extracranial explanation appears possible.

Both processes may coexist.

### Preventing Secondary Brain Injury

The initial traumatic injury may be followed by secondary physiological insults.

These may include:

- hypoxia;
- hypotension;
- inadequate cerebral perfusion;
- hyperthermia or significant temperature disturbance;
- abnormal ventilation;
- seizures;
- hypoglycaemia or significant metabolic disturbance;
- and delayed recognition of neurological deterioration.

The Trauma Team Leader should therefore treat preservation of oxygenation and perfusion as part of neurological management.

The practical principle is:

> **Protect the brain while diagnosing the brain injury.**

### The Neurological Assessment Should Not Delay Life-Saving Treatment

A detailed neurological examination is not a reason to delay treatment of a life-threatening airway, breathing, or circulation problem.

If the patient is hypoxic, the team should correct the oxygenation problem.

If the patient is in haemorrhagic shock, haemorrhage control and resuscitation remain priorities.

If the airway is failing, airway management takes precedence.

The disability assessment should be integrated into the resuscitation rather than performed as an isolated exercise.

## Establishing the Neurological Baseline

### What Should Be Established?

The initial neurological assessment should establish, as quickly as practical:

- level of consciousness;
- GCS and its individual components;
- pupil size and reactivity;
- limb movement;
- obvious focal neurological deficits;
- glucose when indicated;
- seizures or abnormal movements;
- and any significant change from the pre-hospital assessment.

The assessment should also consider factors that may make the examination unreliable.

These include:

- sedation;
- analgesia;
- intoxication;
- language barriers;
- hearing impairment;
- facial trauma;
- paralysis;
- intubation;
- and severe agitation.

### Obtain the Pre-Hospital Trend

The pre-hospital neurological assessment may provide valuable information that cannot be recreated later.

The leader should ask:

- What was the patient's initial GCS?
- Did it change during transport?
- Was there a period of loss of consciousness?
- Was there seizure activity?
- Were sedatives or analgesics administered?
- Was the patient intubated?
- Were there episodes of hypoxia or hypotension?
- Was there any abnormal pupil finding?
- Was there a focal neurological deficit?

A patient who has deteriorated before arrival may already have an evolving intracranial injury even if the current examination appears relatively reassuring.

### Establish the Best Reliable Examination

The leader should distinguish between:

- what the patient can currently do;
- what the patient could do previously;
- and what cannot be assessed reliably.

For example, an intubated patient cannot provide a verbal GCS component in the same way as an awake patient.

The neurological record should therefore communicate the limitations of the examination rather than create a false impression of precision.

## Glasgow Coma Scale

### Use the Components

The Glasgow Coma Scale assesses:

- eye opening;
- verbal response;
- motor response.

The individual components should be recorded where possible rather than documenting only a total score.

The trend in the components may provide useful information.

A change in motor response, for example, may be particularly concerning even if the total score changes only modestly.

### Eye Opening

Eye opening should be assessed according to the patient's response to:

- spontaneous activity;
- speech;
- or an appropriate stimulus when necessary.

The assessment should be performed consistently.

The leader should avoid interpreting eye opening in isolation.

### Verbal Response

Verbal assessment includes consideration of:

- orientation;
- confusion;
- inappropriate responses;
- incomprehensible sounds;
- or absence of verbal response.

The patient's language, hearing, facial trauma, and ability to communicate should be considered when interpreting the result.

### Motor Response

Motor assessment should establish whether the patient:

- obeys commands;
- localises an appropriate stimulus;
- withdraws;
- demonstrates abnormal flexion;
- demonstrates abnormal extension;
- or shows no motor response.

Asymmetry should be actively sought.

A unilateral change in motor function may provide important information about a focal neurological process.

### Do Not Let the Number Replace the Examination

A GCS score is a communication tool.

It should not replace clinical judgement.

Two patients with the same GCS may have very different clinical problems.

For example:

- one patient may be confused but otherwise stable;
- another may have a declining motor response and an evolving intracranial lesion.

The leader should therefore ask:

> **"What is the patient doing, what has changed, and what does the examination suggest?"**

rather than:

> **"What is the GCS?"**

## Pupillary Assessment

### Assess Both Pupils

The leader should establish:

- pupil size;
- symmetry;
- and response to light.

The examination should be compared over time.

A new asymmetry or deterioration in reactivity may be clinically important.

### Interpret Pupils in Context

Pupil abnormalities may be associated with:

- intracranial mass effect;
- herniation;
- direct ocular injury;
- orbital trauma;
- medication effects;
- or other causes.

The presence of an abnormal pupil should therefore trigger clinical assessment rather than automatic attribution to a single diagnosis.

### A New Fixed or Markedly Abnormal Pupil Is a Time-Critical Finding

A new major pupillary abnormality, particularly when accompanied by deterioration in consciousness or motor function, should raise concern for significant intracranial pathology.

The leader should immediately reassess:

- airway;
- oxygenation;
- ventilation;
- circulation;
- GCS;
- pupils;
- motor findings;
- and recent events.

Urgent escalation and definitive assessment may be required.

The team should not allow routine processes to create avoidable delay when the clinical picture suggests neurological deterioration.

## Motor Examination and Focal Deficits

### Look for Asymmetry

The neurological examination should identify obvious asymmetry in:

- upper limb movement;
- lower limb movement;
- facial movement;
- sensation where assessable;
- and response to commands.

The presence of a focal deficit may indicate a structural neurological problem.

### Compare With the Previous Examination

The significance of weakness depends partly on whether it is:

- new;
- improving;
- persistent;
- or longstanding.

The leader should therefore establish the baseline whenever possible.

A new unilateral weakness during resuscitation is particularly important.

### Do Not Assume Reduced Movement Is Neurological

Reduced limb movement may result from:

- pain;
- fracture;
- splinting;
- sedation;
- spinal cord injury;
- peripheral nerve injury;
- or impaired consciousness.

The finding must be interpreted in context.

## Traumatic Brain Injury

### Recognise the Possibility Early

Traumatic brain injury may occur with:

- loss of consciousness;
- altered mental status;
- amnesia;
- vomiting;
- seizure;
- headache;
- focal neurological deficit;
- skull fracture;
- or deterioration in consciousness.

The absence of an obvious external head injury does not exclude significant intracranial injury.

### Mechanism Matters

The leader should consider:

- high-speed road traffic collision;
- motorcycle collision;
- fall from height;
- pedestrian impact;
- assault;
- penetrating injury;
- blast;
- and other high-energy mechanisms.

The mechanism should modify the level of suspicion but should not replace clinical assessment.

### The Examination May Initially Be Reassuring

A patient with intracranial injury may initially have:

- a relatively preserved GCS;
- normal pupils;
- no obvious focal deficit;
- and apparently stable physiology.

This does not eliminate the possibility of evolving intracranial pathology.

The leader should therefore maintain an appropriate level of vigilance when the mechanism and clinical context are concerning.

## Recognising Neurological Deterioration

### Deterioration May Be Subtle

Neurological deterioration may present as:

- increasing confusion;
- reduced interaction;
- increasing drowsiness;
- repeated vomiting;
- worsening headache;
- new agitation;
- new weakness;
- new pupillary abnormality;
- seizure;
- or a falling GCS.

A small change may be clinically important when it represents a clear departure from the previous state.

### Reassess the Whole Patient

When neurological status worsens, the leader should immediately ask:

- Is the airway still adequate?
- Is oxygenation adequate?
- Is ventilation adequate?
- Has hypotension developed?
- Is there ongoing haemorrhage?
- Has the patient received sedative or analgesic medication?
- Is glucose abnormal?
- Is there evidence of seizure activity?
- Is there a new neurological deficit?
- Is intracranial pathology becoming more likely?

This prevents premature fixation on a single explanation.

### Return to the Primary Survey

Neurological deterioration should prompt reconsideration of the primary survey.

The leader should not continue with a planned sequence simply because the team has already moved beyond the initial assessment.

A changing patient requires a changing plan.

The practical cycle is:

> **Recognise → Reassess <C>ABCDE → Identify Cause → Intervene → Reassess**

## Signs Suggesting Raised Intracranial Pressure or Herniation

### Clinical Concern

The team should be alert to combinations of:

- declining consciousness;
- new pupillary asymmetry or reduced reactivity;
- new focal neurological deficit;
- abnormal motor responses;
- progressive deterioration;
- or other signs suggesting increasing intracranial pressure.

No single clinical finding should be interpreted in isolation.

The pattern and trend are important.

### Do Not Wait for Complete Neurological Collapse

If the clinical picture suggests serious intracranial deterioration, the leader should escalate early.

Potential actions may include:

- optimising oxygenation;
- maintaining adequate perfusion;
- securing or protecting the airway when clinically indicated;
- urgent senior review;
- urgent neuroimaging when appropriate;
- neurosurgical consultation;
- critical care involvement;
- and preparation for definitive management.

The exact treatment pathway depends on local capability, patient physiology, imaging findings, and specialist advice.

### Hyperventilation Is Not a Routine Treatment

Routine aggressive hyperventilation should not be used simply because a patient has a head injury.

Excessive reduction in carbon dioxide can reduce cerebral blood flow.

If severe intracranial hypertension or impending herniation is suspected, temporary changes in ventilation may form part of an emergency strategy under appropriate expert management.

The priority remains adequate oxygenation, appropriate ventilation, perfusion, and definitive treatment of the underlying problem.

## Preventing Secondary Brain Injury

### Hypoxia

Hypoxia is a major preventable threat to the injured brain.

The leader should ensure that oxygenation is monitored and corrected promptly when abnormal.

If oxygenation is unexpectedly poor, the team should reassess:

- airway patency;
- ventilation;
- chest injury;
- equipment;
- circulation;
- and the reliability of the measurement.

### Hypotension

Hypotension is particularly concerning in a patient with significant brain injury.

The leader should actively search for the cause.

In trauma, hypotension should prompt consideration of:

- ongoing haemorrhage;
- obstructive shock;
- cardiac injury;
- and other causes of circulatory compromise.

The presence of a head injury should not distract the team from controlling extracranial haemorrhage.

### Ventilation

Ventilation should be managed to avoid both inadequate ventilation and unnecessary excessive ventilation.

The leader should integrate:

- respiratory effort;
- oxygenation;
- clinical examination;
- end-tidal carbon dioxide where available and appropriate;
- and the overall trauma picture.

### Temperature

Temperature should be monitored and significant abnormalities addressed.

The general trauma principle of preventing avoidable heat loss remains important, particularly in patients with major haemorrhage and severe injury.

### Glucose

Glucose should be checked when clinically indicated.

Hypoglycaemia can cause altered consciousness and may mimic or worsen neurological pathology.

A reversible metabolic abnormality should be identified and treated without abandoning the possibility of concurrent traumatic brain injury.

### Seizures

Seizure activity after trauma may indicate significant brain injury.

The leader should ensure:

- immediate safety;
- airway and oxygenation assessment;
- appropriate monitoring;
- correction of reversible physiological abnormalities;
- and timely anticonvulsant treatment according to local protocol and specialist guidance when indicated.

A patient who remains altered after a witnessed seizure should not automatically be assumed to have only a post-ictal state.

## Spinal Cord Injury

### Consider the Spine as Part of the Neurological Assessment

Neurological assessment after trauma should include consideration of spinal cord injury.

Potential clues include:

- limb weakness;
- sensory disturbance;
- paralysis;
- altered reflexes;
- loss of motor function;
- loss of sensation;
- priapism;
- respiratory weakness;
- or neurological findings inconsistent with an isolated head injury.

The absence of severe pain does not exclude spinal injury.

### Protect While Assessing

Spinal precautions and movement strategies should be applied according to the mechanism, examination, local protocol, and current trauma guidance.

The priority remains prevention of secondary neurological injury while ensuring that life-threatening problems are treated promptly.

Suspected spinal injury should not prevent appropriate airway management when the airway is threatened.

### Cervical Spinal Cord Injury and Breathing

High cervical spinal cord injury may impair respiratory function.

A patient may initially appear to have an airway problem when the underlying issue includes neurological impairment of ventilation.

The leader should therefore integrate:

- respiratory effort;
- chest movement;
- cough strength;
- oxygenation;
- neurological findings;
- and the level of suspected spinal injury.

### Neurogenic Shock

Neurogenic shock should be considered in an appropriate trauma context, particularly when hypotension occurs with features suggesting loss of sympathetic vascular tone.

The clinical picture may include:

- hypotension;
- relative or inappropriate bradycardia;
- warm peripheries;
- and a compatible spinal injury.

However, haemorrhage must not be excluded simply because neurogenic shock appears possible.

A patient with spinal injury can also have major haemorrhage.

The leader should therefore continue to search actively for bleeding.

## Imaging and Definitive Assessment

### Imaging Should Answer a Clinical Question

The decision to obtain neuroimaging should be based on:

- clinical findings;
- mechanism;
- neurological trend;
- risk factors;
- examination;
- and the likely impact of the result on management.

The leader should ask:

> **"What decision will this scan help us make?"**

### CT Is Often the Key Initial Investigation

In patients with clinically important head trauma, CT is commonly the principal imaging modality for identifying acute intracranial haemorrhage and other significant traumatic abnormalities.

The precise indication and timing should follow current trauma guidance and local pathways.

The decision should also take into account whether the patient is stable enough to leave the resuscitation environment.

### An Unstable Patient May Not Be Suitable for Routine Imaging

A patient who requires immediate life-saving intervention should not be sent to imaging simply because a scan has been requested.

The leader must balance:

- diagnostic benefit;
- transport risk;
- monitoring requirements;
- available personnel;
- and the urgency of definitive treatment.

The question is:

> **"Is the patient stable enough to leave the resuscitation room?"**

### Imaging Does Not Replace Clinical Reassessment

A normal or apparently reassuring scan does not eliminate the need for clinical observation when the patient's neurological state remains abnormal or deteriorates.

Likewise, a scan should not become the endpoint of management.

The result must be integrated into the definitive plan.

## Sedation, Intubation and the Neurological Examination

### Establish the Examination Before Sedation When Practical

When clinically possible, a reliable neurological assessment should be documented before interventions that will alter the examination.

This may include:

- GCS;
- pupils;
- motor function;
- and relevant focal findings.

However, this should never delay an emergency intervention required to protect the patient.

### Document What Can No Longer Be Assessed

After intubation or sedation, the neurological examination may become less informative.

The leader should ensure that the record communicates:

- the pre-intervention examination;
- the time of intervention;
- relevant medications;
- and the limitations of subsequent assessment.

This allows future clinicians to understand the neurological trajectory.

### Do Not Misinterpret Drug Effects

Sedation and analgesia may cause:

- reduced consciousness;
- reduced motor response;
- altered pupil findings;
- or impaired communication.

The team should therefore interpret subsequent neurological observations in the context of administered medications.

A change should not automatically be attributed to drugs when other causes remain possible.

## The Trauma Team Leader's Role

### Maintain the Neurological Trend

The leader should know:

- the initial GCS;
- the current GCS;
- the direction of change;
- pupil findings;
- focal neurological findings;
- and the interventions that may affect the examination.

The leader should not delegate neurological awareness completely to another team member.

### Assign a Clear Owner

One clinician should be responsible for performing and communicating the neurological assessment.

For example:

> **"Ahmed, assess and document the GCS, pupils and limb movement, and tell me immediately if there is any change."**

This creates a clear communication pathway.

### Communicate Deterioration Explicitly

If the patient's GCS falls, the leader should state the change clearly.

For example:

> **"GCS has fallen from 14 to 10. Stop and reassess the patient. Check airway, oxygenation, blood pressure, glucose and pupils now."**

This prevents the change from being treated as an isolated observation.

### Escalate Early

When the neurological trajectory is concerning, the leader should activate the appropriate senior and specialist resources.

This may include:

- anaesthesia;
- emergency medicine;
- neurosurgery;
- trauma surgery;
- critical care;
- radiology;
- and transfer services.

The exact pathway depends on local trauma capability.

### Prepare for the Next Step

If significant traumatic brain injury is suspected, the leader should anticipate:

- airway deterioration;
- need for controlled ventilation;
- urgent imaging;
- neurosurgical assessment;
- critical care;
- transfer;
- and ongoing neurological monitoring.

The goal is to avoid discovering the next requirement only after deterioration occurs.

## Common Cognitive Errors

### Anchoring on the Head Injury

A patient with trauma and altered consciousness may have multiple simultaneous problems.

The team may incorrectly attribute hypotension, agitation, or reduced consciousness to head injury alone.

The leader should repeatedly ask:

> **"What else could explain this finding?"**

### Anchoring on Intoxication

Intoxication may coexist with significant traumatic brain injury.

The presence of alcohol or recreational drug use should not automatically explain neurological abnormalities.

A patient with suspected intoxication should still receive appropriate trauma assessment.

### False Reassurance From a Single GCS

A relatively preserved GCS does not exclude significant injury.

The mechanism, examination, trend, and other clinical findings remain important.

### Treating the GCS as the Diagnosis

GCS describes neurological responsiveness.

It does not identify the underlying cause.

The leader must continue to consider:

- intracranial injury;
- hypoxia;
- shock;
- metabolic abnormalities;
- drugs;
- seizures;
- and spinal injury.

### Forgetting Extracranial Haemorrhage

A patient with a head injury may also have severe bleeding.

Neurological findings should never cause the team to abandon the haemorrhage assessment.

### Delaying Treatment for Documentation

Accurate documentation is important.

However, documentation should not delay treatment of an immediately life-threatening problem.

The leader should obtain the best possible baseline while maintaining the priority of resuscitation.

## Practical Approach to Neurological Deterioration

When a patient's neurological state worsens, use a structured response.

### Step 1 — Recognise

Identify the change.

Examples include:

- falling GCS;
- new pupil asymmetry;
- new weakness;
- new seizure;
- increasing agitation;
- or reduced responsiveness.

### Step 2 — Confirm

Repeat the assessment.

Check:

- GCS components;
- pupils;
- motor findings;
- vital signs;
- oxygenation;
- and glucose when indicated.

### Step 3 — Reassess the Primary Survey

Return to:

> **<C>ABCDE**

Do not assume the cause is neurological before reassessing the whole patient.

### Step 4 — Correct Reversible Threats

Address:

- airway compromise;
- hypoxia;
- inadequate ventilation;
- hypotension;
- haemorrhage;
- hypoglycaemia;
- seizures;
- and other immediately reversible abnormalities.

### Step 5 — Identify the Likely Cause

Consider:

- traumatic intracranial injury;
- expanding haemorrhage;
- cerebral oedema;
- seizure;
- hypoxia;
- hypotension;
- medication;
- intoxication;
- metabolic disturbance;
- or spinal injury.

### Step 6 — Escalate

Activate the appropriate:

- senior clinician;
- neurosurgical service;
- critical care team;
- imaging pathway;
- trauma service;
- or transfer pathway.

### Step 7 — Reassess After Intervention

The patient's response provides additional information.

Ask:

> **"Did the neurological and physiological state improve as expected?"**

If not, reconsider the diagnosis and strategy.

## Practical Mental Model

The disability assessment can be summarised as:

> **Baseline → GCS → Pupils → Motor → Physiology → Trend → Cause → Action → Reassess**

This is not a rigid sequence.

Several components should occur in parallel.

The leader should continuously integrate the neurological examination with the patient's overall physiology.

## Case-Based Leadership Discussion

### The Initial Assessment

The patient arrives after a high-energy motorcycle collision.

His GCS is 13/15.

The pupils are equal and reactive.

He has facial trauma and a scalp wound.

The initial neurological assessment is abnormal but not profoundly depressed.

The leader should:

- document the baseline GCS;
- document the individual components;
- assess pupils;
- assess motor function;
- establish the pre-hospital neurological trend;
- consider the mechanism;
- assess for other traumatic injuries;
- and ensure adequate oxygenation and perfusion.

The leader should also anticipate the possibility of deterioration.

### Ten Minutes Later

The patient's GCS falls to 10/15.

He becomes increasingly drowsy.

The right pupil appears larger than the left.

The team begins to focus on the head injury.

The leader interrupts the sequence:

> **"We have a significant neurological deterioration. Reassess <C>ABCDE now."**

The team checks:

- airway;
- oxygenation;
- ventilation;
- blood pressure;
- glucose;
- pupils;
- motor response;
- and recent medications.

The leader then activates the appropriate escalation pathway.

The important leadership decision is not simply recognising that the GCS has fallen.

It is recognising that the patient's trajectory has changed and that the plan must change with it.

### Leadership Lessons

The case illustrates several principles:

1. Establish a neurological baseline early.
2. Record the individual GCS components where possible.
3. Use the trend rather than a single score.
4. Treat hypoxia and hypotension promptly.
5. Reassess the entire patient when neurological status changes.
6. Do not assume intoxication explains altered consciousness.
7. Do not allow a head injury to distract from extracranial haemorrhage.
8. Escalate early when the trajectory suggests significant intracranial pathology.
9. Anticipate the need for imaging, specialist review, and critical care.
10. Communicate deterioration explicitly to the entire team.

## Practical Checklist for the Trauma Team Leader

### Before the Assessment

- Establish the pre-hospital neurological trend.
- Ask about loss of consciousness.
- Ask about seizure activity.
- Ask about medications and sedation.
- Review episodes of hypoxia or hypotension.
- Identify factors that may make the examination unreliable.

### During the Assessment

- Record GCS.
- Record individual components where possible.
- Assess pupils.
- Assess motor symmetry.
- Look for focal neurological deficits.
- Check glucose when indicated.
- Assess oxygenation and circulation.
- Consider spinal cord injury.

### When the Patient Deteriorates

- State the change clearly.
- Reassess <C>ABCDE.
- Check airway and oxygenation.
- Check circulation and haemorrhage.
- Check glucose.
- Repeat GCS and pupils.
- Review medications and sedation.
- Consider intracranial pathology.
- Escalate early.
- Arrange appropriate definitive assessment.
- Reassess response.

## Error Prevention

### Error 1 — Documenting Only the Total GCS

A total score without the components provides less information about the neurological trajectory.

**Prevention:**

Record the individual components whenever possible.

### Error 2 — Assuming a Low GCS Is the Diagnosis

The GCS describes responsiveness but does not identify the cause.

**Prevention:**

Always interpret the score within the clinical context.

### Error 3 — Ignoring the Trend

A falling GCS may be more important than a single absolute value.

**Prevention:**

Compare every assessment with the previous one.

### Error 4 — Attributing Deterioration to Intoxication

Intoxication may coexist with significant intracranial injury.

**Prevention:**

Do not use intoxication as a diagnosis of exclusion for traumatic neurological deterioration.

### Error 5 — Forgetting Hypoxia and Hypotension

Secondary physiological insults may worsen neurological outcome.

**Prevention:**

Reassess oxygenation and perfusion whenever neurological status changes.

### Error 6 — Forgetting Spinal Cord Injury

Weakness or altered consciousness may occur in patients with spinal injury.

**Prevention:**

Integrate motor and sensory findings with mechanism and examination.

### Error 7 — Sending an Unstable Patient to Imaging

Diagnostic imaging is not automatically the next step for every patient.

**Prevention:**

Ask whether the patient is stable enough to leave the resuscitation environment.

### Error 8 — Waiting for Complete Neurological Collapse

A patient may deteriorate progressively before catastrophic signs appear.

**Prevention:**

Escalate when the trajectory is concerning rather than waiting for irreversible deterioration.

## Clinical Pearl

> **A falling GCS is not merely a number that has changed. It is a change in the patient's clinical trajectory until proven otherwise.**

## Leadership Pearl

> **The Trauma Team Leader should know the patient's neurological baseline, the current neurological state, and the direction of travel at all times.**

## Final Takeaway

The disability assessment is a rapid, structured neurological assessment performed within the wider trauma resuscitation.

Its value lies not in producing a single number but in recognising clinically important change.

The Trauma Team Leader should continuously integrate:

> **Neurological examination + physiology + mechanism + treatment + trajectory**

A patient with traumatic brain injury is particularly vulnerable to secondary physiological insults.

The leader must therefore protect oxygenation and perfusion while simultaneously identifying the cause of neurological abnormality and planning definitive management.

When the neurological state changes, the response should be immediate and structured:

> **Recognise → Reassess <C>ABCDE → Correct reversible threats → Identify cause → Escalate → Reassess**

The purpose of the disability assessment is ultimately simple:

> **Recognise neurological deterioration early enough to change the patient's outcome.**

## Summary

The key principles of disability assessment in trauma are:

- establish a reliable neurological baseline;
- record the GCS and its components where possible;
- assess pupils and motor function;
- look for focal neurological deficits;
- interpret findings in the context of the entire patient;
- treat hypoxia and hypotension promptly;
- consider extracranial causes of altered consciousness;
- maintain awareness of spinal cord injury;
- recognise neurological deterioration early;
- reassess <C>ABCDE when the patient's condition changes;
- do not allow intoxication or another presumed diagnosis to create false reassurance;
- use imaging to answer clinical questions;
- avoid unnecessary delay in definitive management;
- communicate deterioration clearly;
- and anticipate the next step before the patient deteriorates further.

> **Disability assessment is not the final step of the primary survey. It is a continuous process of recognising neurological change, protecting the injured brain, and directing the patient towards definitive care.**
