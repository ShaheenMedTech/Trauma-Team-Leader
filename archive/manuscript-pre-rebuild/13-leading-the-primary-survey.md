# Chapter 13 — Leading the Primary Survey

## Clinical Scenario

A 34-year-old man is brought to the resuscitation room after a high-speed road traffic collision.

He was the restrained driver of a vehicle involved in a frontal impact. The pre-hospital team reports significant vehicle damage and a prolonged extrication.

On arrival:

- Airway: patent, but the patient is agitated and speaking in short phrases.
- Breathing: respiratory rate 30/min, SpO₂ 91% on high-flow oxygen, reduced air entry on the right.
- Circulation: heart rate 128/min, blood pressure 88/54 mmHg, cool peripheries.
- Disability: GCS 13/15.
- Exposure: obvious deformity of the right thigh and blood on the floor beneath the patient.

The team is assembled, but several clinicians begin acting simultaneously.

The airway doctor is preparing for intubation.

The procedure doctor is requesting a chest drain.

The nurse is asking for blood.

The orthopaedic clinician is focusing on the femoral deformity.

Someone is attempting to obtain intravenous access.

The trauma team leader looks at the monitor and begins calling out individual instructions.

The problem is not a lack of expertise.

The problem is **coordination**.

A trauma team can perform every individual task correctly and still fail the patient if the leader does not maintain a coherent primary survey.

The leader's responsibility is not to personally perform the survey.

The leader's responsibility is to **lead the survey**.

That means:

1. Establishing the sequence.
2. Identifying immediate threats.
3. Assigning actions to appropriate team members.
4. Maintaining situational awareness.
5. Reassessing after every major intervention.
6. Preventing fixation on one injury.
7. Integrating information from the whole team.
8. Deciding when the team should move from one priority to another.
9. Recognising when the initial assessment is no longer adequate.
10. Maintaining control of the overall resuscitation.

> The primary survey is not simply a checklist.
>
> It is a dynamic process of threat identification, intervention, reassessment and prioritisation.

---

# Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain the purpose of the primary survey from the perspective of the trauma team leader.
2. Describe how the leader establishes and maintains a structured `<C>ABCDE` assessment.
3. Distinguish between performing a primary survey and leading a primary survey.
4. Allocate primary survey tasks to appropriate team members.
5. Identify when a problem requires immediate intervention rather than further assessment.
6. Maintain a shared mental model during the primary survey.
7. Use explicit verbalisation to communicate priorities.
8. Recognise and manage competing clinical priorities.
9. Avoid premature progression through the primary survey.
10. Use reassessment as an integral component of the primary survey.
11. Recognise deterioration during the survey.
12. Coordinate simultaneous interventions without losing overall control.
13. Integrate clinical findings, monitoring and response to treatment.
14. Identify common leadership failures during the primary survey.
15. Apply a practical leadership framework to real trauma cases.

# 1. What Is the Primary Survey?

The primary survey is the structured initial assessment of the critically injured patient designed to identify and treat immediately life-threatening problems.

In major trauma, the traditional sequence is commonly expressed as:

> `<C>ABCDE`

Where:

- `<C>` = catastrophic haemorrhage
- `A` = airway with cervical spine protection
- `B` = breathing and ventilation
- `C` = circulation
- `D` = disability
- `E` = exposure and environmental control

The exact sequence and terminology may vary between trauma systems and clinical environments.

The underlying principle does not.

> Identify the most immediately reversible threats to life and treat them without unnecessary delay.

For the trauma team leader, the primary survey is therefore both:

- a **clinical process**, and
- a **team coordination process**.

The leader must ensure that the clinical assessment occurs while simultaneously maintaining control of the team.

---

# 2. Performing the Survey Versus Leading the Survey

This distinction is fundamental.

A clinician performing the primary survey may personally assess:

- airway,
- breathing,
- circulation,
- neurological status,
- exposure.

A trauma team leader should instead ensure that the team collectively assesses these domains.

The leader may examine the patient at any point.

However, the leader should avoid becoming absorbed in one task.

For example:

A leader who spends several minutes personally attempting difficult intravenous access is no longer leading the resuscitation effectively.

A leader who personally performs every examination risks losing awareness of:

- the monitor,
- the interventions being performed,
- new information,
- team performance,
- changes in physiology,
- competing threats.

The leader should therefore think:

> "What needs to happen, who should do it, and what information do I need back?"

Rather than:

> "What task should I perform next?"

---

# 3. The Primary Survey Is a Dynamic Cycle

The primary survey should not be regarded as a single pass through `<C>ABCDE`.

It is better understood as a cycle:

**Assess → Identify threat → Intervene → Reassess → Reprioritise**

A patient may initially appear to have a breathing problem.

After intervention, severe haemorrhage may become the dominant threat.

A patient may initially have a patent airway.

After deterioration in consciousness, the airway may become threatened.

A patient who initially has a blood pressure of 100 mmHg may become profoundly hypotensive several minutes later.

Therefore:

> Completing the primary survey does not mean the primary survey is finished.

The patient must be continuously reassessed.

---

# 4. The Leader's First Task: Establish Control

Before leading the primary survey, the leader should establish control of the room.

This does not require authoritarian behaviour.

It requires clarity.

The team should understand:

- who the leader is,
- who is responsible for each major task,
- what the immediate priorities are,
- how information will be communicated,
- when reassessment will occur.

A useful opening statement is:

> "I am leading the trauma resuscitation. Please direct major findings and concerns to me. We will work through the primary survey while treating life threats immediately."

This establishes a shared mental model.

---

# 5. The Leader Must See the Whole Room

A common failure is tunnel vision.

The leader focuses on the patient but loses awareness of the team.

Another failure is the opposite.

The leader focuses on team coordination but does not understand the patient's physiological deterioration.

Effective leadership requires both.

The leader should continuously monitor:

### Patient

- airway,
- respiratory effort,
- oxygenation,
- circulation,
- mental state,
- temperature,
- visible injuries.

### Monitor

- heart rate,
- blood pressure,
- oxygen saturation,
- ECG,
- respiratory rate,
- temperature where available,
- end-tidal CO₂ when appropriate.

### Team

- who is performing each task,
- what has been completed,
- what remains outstanding,
- whether the team understands the plan.

### Environment

- equipment availability,
- blood products,
- imaging access,
- surgical capability,
- anaesthetic support,
- transfer requirements.

This is situational awareness in practice.

---

# 6. `<C>` — Catastrophic Haemorrhage

The primary survey begins with the immediate identification and control of catastrophic external haemorrhage.

The leader should rapidly ask:

> "Is there catastrophic bleeding?"

This question should be answered immediately.

The assessment includes:

- visual inspection,
- active external bleeding,
- amputations,
- major limb wounds,
- junctional bleeding,
- blood pooling,
- bleeding from penetrating wounds.

The team should simultaneously identify potential sources of internal haemorrhage.

These may include:

- chest,
- abdomen,
- pelvis,
- retroperitoneum,
- long bones,
- external wounds.

---

# 7. The Leader Should Not Wait for the Survey to Finish

A major leadership principle is:

> **Treat life-threatening problems when they are identified.**

If catastrophic external haemorrhage is identified, it should not wait until the rest of the primary survey has been completed.

The appropriate team member should immediately:

- apply direct pressure,
- apply a haemostatic dressing where appropriate,
- apply a tourniquet when indicated,
- initiate other appropriate haemorrhage-control measures.

The leader should simultaneously move the overall assessment forward.

For example:

> "Major external haemorrhage from the left thigh. Apply the tourniquet now. Continue the primary survey."

The intervention occurs while the team continues assessing the patient.

---

# 8. Airway: The Leader's Perspective

The airway assessment is not simply:

> "Is the airway open?"

The leader must determine whether the airway is:

- currently patent,
- threatened,
- likely to deteriorate,
- already failing.

Important findings may include:

- inability to speak normally,
- obstruction,
- blood or secretions,
- facial trauma,
- burns,
- expanding neck injury,
- altered consciousness,
- severe agitation,
- vomiting,
- airway noise,
- loss of protective reflexes.

The leader should ask:

> "Is the airway currently adequate, and can the patient maintain it?"

---

# 9. Airway and Cervical Spine

Airway management must be coordinated with appropriate cervical spine precautions in patients at risk of spinal injury.

The leader should avoid allowing concern about cervical spine movement to delay a necessary airway intervention.

At the same time, unnecessary movement should be minimised.

The team should understand:

- who is controlling the airway,
- who is responsible for cervical spine protection,
- what airway strategy is being used,
- what the backup plan is.

---

# 10. Anticipating Airway Deterioration

The leader should not only assess the current airway.

The leader should anticipate what may happen next.

Examples include:

- facial swelling,
- inhalational injury,
- expanding neck haematoma,
- worsening consciousness,
- exhaustion,
- persistent vomiting,
- progressive hypoxia.

The important question is:

> "Will this airway remain safe?"

A currently patent airway can become difficult or impossible to manage later.

---

# 11. Airway Leadership During Intubation

If advanced airway management is required, the leader should avoid becoming distracted by the technical procedure.

The airway clinician should lead the airway procedure.

The trauma team leader should coordinate the overall resuscitation.

The leader should know:

- why intubation is required,
- whether the patient is physiologically prepared,
- whether oxygenation is adequate,
- whether haemodynamic support is required,
- what the backup airway plan is,
- what happens if intubation fails.

A useful command is:

> "Airway team, proceed with your plan. Please tell me before induction and immediately report any difficulty."

This maintains coordination without micromanagement.

---

# 12. Breathing: Assess Before Assuming

Breathing assessment should identify immediately life-threatening thoracic conditions.

The leader should ensure assessment of:

- respiratory rate,
- respiratory effort,
- oxygen saturation,
- chest movement,
- chest wall injury,
- breath sounds,
- tracheal position where relevant,
- signs of tension physiology,
- open chest wounds,
- massive haemothorax,
- flail chest,
- pulmonary contusion,
- associated injuries.

The leader should listen to the clinician performing the chest assessment.

A useful prompt is:

> "Breathing findings?"

The response should be concise.

For example:

> "Reduced right air entry, severe respiratory distress and hypotension."

This should trigger immediate consideration of a life-threatening thoracic injury.

---

# 13. Treat the Life Threat, Not the Label

The leader should avoid becoming dependent on diagnostic labels.

For example, the important question is not:

> "Is this definitely a tension pneumothorax?"

The immediate question may be:

> "Does this patient have a life-threatening thoracic problem requiring immediate treatment?"

When the clinical picture is compelling, treatment should not be unnecessarily delayed while waiting for confirmatory testing.

---

# 14. Circulation: Look Beyond the Blood Pressure

Circulation assessment should consider the entire physiological picture.

The leader should integrate:

- heart rate,
- blood pressure,
- pulse quality,
- skin temperature,
- capillary refill,
- mental state,
- visible bleeding,
- mechanism,
- suspected internal bleeding,
- response to intervention.

A normal blood pressure does not exclude major haemorrhage.

Likewise, hypotension is a late or severe finding in some patients.

The leader should ask:

> "Where could this patient be bleeding?"

---

# 15. Search for the Source of Shock

In major trauma, significant haemorrhage may arise from several anatomical compartments.

The leader should maintain a mental model of the major potential sources:

- external,
- chest,
- abdomen,
- pelvis,
- retroperitoneum,
- long bones,
- soft tissues.

This can be expressed as:

> "Where is the blood going?"

The question is particularly important when the patient's physiology is worse than the visible injuries suggest.

---

# 16. Resuscitation Must Run in Parallel

A trauma resuscitation is rarely sequential in a literal sense.

While one clinician assesses the airway:

- another may obtain vascular access,
- another may control bleeding,
- another may prepare blood products,
- another may monitor the patient,
- another may obtain focused imaging.

The leader coordinates these activities.

The goal is not:

> "Everyone wait until I finish A before doing B."

The goal is:

> "Maintain the structured priorities while allowing appropriate parallel actions."

---

# 17. Disability

Neurological assessment should be rapid and repeatable.

The leader should ensure assessment of:

- GCS,
- pupils,
- limb movement,
- glucose when clinically appropriate,
- obvious neurological deficits,
- seizure activity.

The leader should pay particular attention to changes from the initial neurological state.

A deteriorating GCS may represent:

- traumatic brain injury,
- hypoxia,
- hypercapnia,
- hypoperfusion,
- intoxication,
- hypoglycaemia,
- seizure,
- other causes.

The leader should avoid assuming that every change in consciousness is caused by head injury.

---

# 18. Exposure and Environmental Control

Exposure is essential for identifying injuries that may otherwise remain hidden.

The patient should be adequately exposed to permit examination.

However:

> Exposure without environmental control can create a second physiological problem.

The trauma team leader should therefore ensure:

- appropriate warming,
- minimisation of unnecessary exposure,
- warm fluids and blood products where appropriate,
- active temperature management,
- prompt covering after examination.

Hypothermia is particularly important in severely injured and bleeding patients.

---

# 19. The Leader Should Verbalise the Survey

Silent leadership is often ineffective.

The team cannot reliably follow a plan that exists only in the leader's head.

The leader should therefore verbalise key findings and priorities.

For example:

> "Catastrophic haemorrhage controlled."

> "Airway currently patent."

> "Breathing is abnormal with reduced right air entry."

> "Circulation is compromised; major haemorrhage is suspected."

This creates a shared mental model.

---

# 20. Closed-Loop Communication During the Survey

Commands should be directed to a specific person whenever possible.

Weak:

> "Someone get another IV."

Better:

> "Dr Ahmed, obtain a second large-bore IV."

Then:

> "Second IV being obtained."

And finally:

> "Second IV secured."

The leader should acknowledge completion and continue.

This reduces ambiguity.

---

# 21. The Leader Should Ask for Findings, Not Just Actions

A common mistake is to ask only:

> "Is the airway done?"

Instead, ask:

> "What is the current airway status?"

Similarly:

> "What are the breathing findings?"

> "What is the circulation assessment?"

> "What is the current GCS?"

The leader needs information, not merely confirmation that a task occurred.

---

# 22. Use Explicit Time Points

During a complex resuscitation, the leader should periodically pause briefly and summarise.

For example:

> "Let's pause for a 20-second update."

Then:

- airway?
- breathing?
- circulation?
- disability?
- major interventions?
- response?

This prevents the team from becoming fragmented.

---

# 23. Reassessment After Intervention

Every major intervention should be followed by reassessment.

Examples:

### After airway intervention

Reassess:

- oxygenation,
- ventilation,
- airway patency,
- tube position where applicable,
- haemodynamics.

### After chest intervention

Reassess:

- respiratory effort,
- oxygenation,
- chest findings,
- haemodynamics.

### After haemorrhage control

Reassess:

- bleeding,
- perfusion,
- haemodynamics,
- mental status.

### After blood product administration

Reassess:

- physiology,
- ongoing bleeding,
- response,
- need for definitive haemorrhage control.

The leader should ask:

> "Did the patient improve?"

---

# 24. Failure to Improve Is Information

If the patient does not respond as expected, the leader should reconsider the working diagnosis.

For example:

> Persistent shock after an intervention does not automatically mean the intervention failed.

It may mean:

- there is another bleeding source,
- the diagnosis was incomplete,
- the intervention was inadequate,
- the patient has multiple simultaneous life threats.

The leader should avoid anchoring.
