# Chapter 12 — Paediatric Trauma

## Clinical Scenario

A 7-year-old child is brought to the resuscitation room following a high-speed road traffic collision.

The child was restrained in the rear seat but sustained significant vehicle intrusion.

The pre-hospital team reports a brief period of reduced consciousness followed by agitation.

On arrival, the child is frightened and crying.

Initial observations are:

- respiratory rate 34/min;
- oxygen saturation 95% on supplemental oxygen;
- heart rate 148/min;
- systolic blood pressure 92 mmHg;
- Glasgow Coma Scale 13/15;
- cool peripheries.

There is bruising across the chest and lower abdomen.

The child has tenderness over the left thigh.

The Trauma Team Leader recognises that paediatric trauma cannot simply be managed as a smaller version of adult trauma.

Children may compensate for significant physiological injury before sudden deterioration occurs.

The team must therefore integrate mechanism, physiology, age-specific normal observations, anatomy, weight, and clinical trajectory while maintaining a structured trauma approach.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Recognise the important physiological and anatomical differences between children and adults following trauma.
2. Apply the primary survey to the injured child.
3. Recognise compensated shock and the significance of physiological deterioration.
4. Assess and manage the paediatric airway while considering cervical spine injury.
5. Recognise important thoracic injuries in children.
6. Assess circulation and initiate appropriate haemorrhage management.
7. Use weight-based principles safely during paediatric resuscitation.
8. Recognise traumatic brain injury and neurological deterioration.
9. Identify occult injuries using mechanism and clinical findings.
10. Recognise the importance of safeguarding and non-accidental injury.
11. Coordinate imaging and definitive management according to the child's condition.
12. Communicate effectively with the child, parents, carers, and multidisciplinary team.
13. Recognise when specialist paediatric trauma input or transfer is required.
14. Reassess continuously and recognise deterioration early.

---

## Key Concepts

### Children Are Not Small Adults

Paediatric trauma requires an age-specific approach.

Children differ from adults in:

- airway anatomy;
- respiratory physiology;
- cardiovascular physiology;
- body proportions;
- heat regulation;
- medication requirements;
- equipment requirements;
- communication;
- and patterns of injury.

The Trauma Team Leader must therefore ensure that appropriate paediatric equipment, expertise, and protocols are available.

---

### Compensation Can Mask Severe Injury

Children may maintain blood pressure despite significant blood loss.

Compensatory mechanisms may preserve systolic blood pressure until relatively late in the course of shock.

A child with:

- tachycardia;
- cool peripheries;
- delayed capillary refill;
- altered behaviour;
- reduced urine output;
- or increasing respiratory effort

may already have significant physiological compromise even if the blood pressure appears acceptable.

The leader should therefore assess the whole physiological picture.

---

### Age Matters

Normal physiological observations vary considerably with age.

Heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, and neurological behaviour should therefore be interpreted against appropriate age-specific reference ranges.

The team should avoid applying adult thresholds automatically to children.

---

## The Paediatric Trauma Team

### Preparation

Before the child's arrival, the leader should ensure that the team is prepared for:

- the child's estimated age and weight;
- appropriate airway equipment;
- appropriate vascular access equipment;
- paediatric monitoring;
- appropriate immobilisation equipment where indicated;
- warming equipment;
- blood products;
- appropriate medications;
- and specialist paediatric support.

Whenever possible, the child's weight should be established early.

If an accurate weight is unavailable, an appropriate validated weight-estimation method may be used according to local protocol.

---

### Equipment

Equipment should be appropriate for the child's:

- age;
- size;
- airway anatomy;
- vascular access requirements;
- and injury pattern.

A major source of avoidable error is using equipment or medication doses without confirming the appropriate size or weight.

The leader should ensure that critical calculations are checked.

---

## Primary Survey

The paediatric trauma patient should be assessed using the same fundamental priorities:

**<C>ABCDE**

However, the interpretation and management must be adapted to the child's physiology and anatomy.

---

# <C> Catastrophic Haemorrhage

### Identify Major Bleeding

The first priority remains identification and control of catastrophic haemorrhage.

The team should look for:

- external bleeding;
- penetrating injury;
- major limb injury;
- pelvic injury;
- chest injury;
- abdominal injury;
- and other possible sources of internal haemorrhage.

Direct pressure and appropriate haemorrhage-control measures should be applied immediately.

---

### Internal Haemorrhage

Children may lose a significant volume of blood internally before obvious external signs develop.

Potential sources include:

- chest;
- abdomen;
- pelvis;
- retroperitoneum;
- and long bones.

Mechanism should therefore influence the level of suspicion.

A child involved in high-energy trauma should not be considered stable simply because there is little visible bleeding.

---

### Paediatric Blood Volume

Children have a smaller absolute circulating blood volume than adults.

Relatively modest absolute blood loss may therefore represent a significant proportion of the child's circulating volume.

This is why apparently small amounts of blood loss should be interpreted in relation to:

- body weight;
- age;
- estimated circulating volume;
- and physiological response.

---

## A — Airway

### Airway Anatomy

The paediatric airway differs from the adult airway.

Important considerations include:

- smaller airway diameter;
- proportionally larger tongue;
- relatively higher and more anterior laryngeal anatomy in younger children;
- and increased susceptibility to obstruction from oedema and secretions.

Small changes in airway calibre may therefore produce significant increases in airway resistance.

---

### Airway Assessment

The leader should assess:

- ability to vocalise or cry;
- airway sounds;
- stridor;
- secretions;
- blood;
- facial trauma;
- oral injury;
- reduced consciousness;
- and signs of obstruction.

A child who is crying or speaking effectively currently has evidence of airway patency, but the airway must continue to be monitored.

---

### Cervical Spine

The mechanism should determine concern for cervical spine injury.

The team should maintain appropriate spinal precautions while avoiding unnecessary delay to airway management.

The leader should ensure that cervical spine protection does not compromise:

- oxygenation;
- ventilation;
- airway access;
- or overall resuscitation.

---

### Airway Obstruction

Children may deteriorate rapidly from:

- swelling;
- blood;
- secretions;
- facial trauma;
- reduced consciousness;
- or foreign material.

The leader should anticipate deterioration rather than waiting for complete obstruction.

---

### Advanced Airway Management

If advanced airway management is required, the team should ensure:

- appropriately sized equipment;
- appropriate expertise;
- monitoring;
- preoxygenation where feasible;
- backup plans;
- and confirmation of successful airway placement.

Paediatric airway management should follow local specialist protocols.

The leader should ensure that weight-based medication calculations are checked before administration.

---

# B — Breathing

### Respiratory Physiology

Children generally have higher metabolic oxygen requirements than adults.

They may therefore deteriorate quickly when ventilation or oxygenation is impaired.

The leader should pay particular attention to:

- respiratory rate;
- work of breathing;
- chest movement;
- oxygenation;
- breath sounds;
- and mental status.

---

### Chest Injury

Important thoracic injuries include:

- tension pneumothorax;
- open pneumothorax;
- haemothorax;
- pulmonary contusion;
- and major airway injury.

Children may sustain significant internal chest injury despite limited external chest-wall findings.

The relative flexibility of the paediatric chest wall may allow substantial energy transmission to internal structures without obvious rib fractures.

---

### Tension Pneumothorax

A child with:

- severe respiratory distress;
- unilateral reduced air entry;
- circulatory compromise;
- or rapidly worsening physiology

should be assessed immediately for tension pneumothorax.

When the diagnosis is sufficiently likely clinically, treatment should not be delayed unnecessarily for imaging.

---

### Pulmonary Contusion

Pulmonary contusion may develop following blunt chest trauma.

Respiratory compromise may worsen over time.

The leader should therefore consider the patient's trajectory rather than relying solely on the initial examination.

---

## C — Circulation

### Recognising Shock

The leader should assess:

- heart rate;
- blood pressure;
- capillary refill;
- peripheral temperature;
- pulse quality;
- mental status;
- urine output where appropriate;
- and response to resuscitation.

No single measurement should be interpreted in isolation.

---

### Tachycardia

Tachycardia is an important physiological sign in children.

However, it may also occur because of:

- pain;
- anxiety;
- crying;
- fever;
- medications;
- or hypovolaemia.

The leader should integrate the heart rate with the rest of the clinical picture.

Persistent tachycardia despite appropriate treatment should prompt reassessment.

---

### Hypotension

Hypotension may be a late sign of significant paediatric shock.

A child with hypotension following trauma should therefore be regarded as critically unwell until proven otherwise.

The leader should immediately reassess for:

- major haemorrhage;
- tension pneumothorax;
- cardiac injury;
- and other causes of circulatory collapse.

---

## Vascular Access

### Peripheral Access

The first approach should generally be appropriate peripheral intravenous access.

The team should avoid repeated prolonged attempts when rapid access is required.

---

### Intraosseous Access

If vascular access cannot be established rapidly in a critically injured child, intraosseous access may provide a rapid route for:

- fluids;
- blood products;
- and emergency medications.

The decision should follow local paediatric trauma protocols.

---

### Access and Team Coordination

The leader should assign clear responsibility for vascular access.

At the same time, other team members should continue:

- airway management;
- chest assessment;
- haemorrhage control;
- monitoring;
- and preparation of definitive treatment.

---

## Fluid and Blood Resuscitation

### Avoid Blind Volume Administration

Paediatric resuscitation should not rely on indiscriminate administration of large fluid volumes.

The team should prioritise:

- haemorrhage control;
- appropriate blood products when indicated;
- careful reassessment;
- and avoidance of unnecessary dilution.

---

### Weight-Based Management

Many paediatric interventions are weight-based.

These may include:

- fluid therapy;
- medication doses;
- blood products;
- airway equipment;
- and some procedural decisions.

The leader should ensure that weight-based calculations are clearly communicated and independently checked where appropriate.

---

### Major Haemorrhage

If significant haemorrhage is suspected, the team should activate the appropriate paediatric major haemorrhage pathway.

The leader should coordinate:

- haemorrhage control;
- blood product availability;
- vascular access;
- laboratory testing;
- warming;
- and definitive haemorrhage control.

---

## D — Disability

### Neurological Assessment

Neurological assessment should include:

- Glasgow Coma Scale;
- pupils;
- limb movement;
- glucose where appropriate;
- and behaviour.

The child's normal behaviour should be considered when interpreting the examination.

---

### Behavioural Change

In children, agitation may represent:

- pain;
- fear;
- hypoxia;
- hypovolaemia;
- head injury;
- or neurological deterioration.

Similarly, a quiet or unusually sleepy child may be significantly unwell.

Changes from the child's baseline should therefore be taken seriously.

---

## Paediatric Traumatic Brain Injury

### Primary Brain Injury

Traumatic brain injury may result from:

- direct impact;
- acceleration-deceleration;
- or penetrating injury.

The leader should consider the mechanism and neurological findings together.

---

### Secondary Brain Injury

Preventing secondary brain injury is a major priority.

The team should minimise:

- hypoxia;
- hypotension;
- hypoglycaemia;
- hyperthermia;
- and other physiological disturbances.

Adequate oxygenation and cerebral perfusion should be maintained.

---

### Deterioration

A declining GCS, new pupillary abnormality, seizure, or worsening neurological state should trigger immediate reassessment.

The team should return to the primary survey and reconsider:

- airway;
- breathing;
- circulation;
- and intracranial injury.

---

# E — Exposure and Environmental Control

### Full Exposure

The child should be adequately exposed to identify injuries.

The team should inspect:

- head;
- neck;
- chest;
- abdomen;
- pelvis;
- back;
- limbs;
- and perineum where clinically appropriate.

Clothing may conceal significant injuries.

---

### Prevent Hypothermia

Children are particularly vulnerable to heat loss.

The leader should therefore:

- minimise unnecessary exposure;
- use warm blankets;
- maintain an appropriate room temperature;
- warm fluids where appropriate;
- and monitor temperature.

Hypothermia can worsen physiological instability and coagulopathy.

---

## Paediatric Secondary Survey

Once immediate life threats have been addressed, the team should perform a structured secondary survey.

This should include:

- head and face;
- neck;
- chest;
- abdomen;
- pelvis;
- perineum;
- back;
- limbs;
- and neurological status.

The examination should remain guided by:

**Mechanism → Injury pattern → Examination → Physiology → Investigation**

---

## Mechanism-Specific Injuries

### Road Traffic Collision

The leader should consider:

- occupant position;
- restraint use;
- vehicle intrusion;
- direction of impact;
- ejection;
- rollover;
- and other passengers' injuries.

A child who was restrained may still sustain significant internal injury.

---

### Seat-Belt Injury

Bruising across the abdomen or chest following restraint should increase suspicion for underlying injury.

The leader should not dismiss a seat-belt mark as a superficial finding.

Potential associated injuries may include:

- abdominal injury;
- spinal injury;
- and bowel or mesenteric injury.

---

### Falls

Children may sustain significant injury following falls.

The assessment should consider:

- height;
- landing surface;
- body position;
- head impact;
- and associated injuries.

---

## Occult Injury

Children may have significant internal injury with relatively limited external signs.

The leader should therefore maintain suspicion when:

- mechanism is high energy;
- physiology is abnormal;
- pain is disproportionate;
- examination is limited;
- or the child cannot communicate symptoms reliably.

---

## Imaging

### Imaging Should Answer a Clinical Question

Investigations should be selected according to:

- mechanism;
- clinical findings;
- physiological status;
- suspected injuries;
- and the likely effect of the result on management.

The availability of imaging should not replace clinical judgement.

---

### Radiation Exposure

Children are more sensitive to ionising radiation than adults.

The leader should therefore avoid unnecessary imaging.

Imaging should be justified by the clinical question and use appropriate paediatric protocols.

---

### CT

CT may be appropriate when the clinical probability of significant injury is sufficient to justify the examination.

However, the decision should consider:

- neurological findings;
- mechanism;
- clinical examination;
- physiological status;
- and whether the result will alter management.

---

### Unstable Patient

An unstable child should not be sent away from the resuscitation environment for imaging simply because a scan has been ordered.

If immediate intervention is required, definitive treatment takes priority.

---

## Safeguarding and Non-Accidental Injury

### Consider Safeguarding

Trauma assessment should include consideration of whether the reported mechanism is consistent with the injuries.

The team should remain alert to possible:

- non-accidental injury;
- neglect;
- inconsistent history;
- unexplained injuries;
- or repeated presentations.

---

### Concerning Findings

Features that may raise concern include:

- injuries inconsistent with the reported mechanism;
- changing accounts;
- unexplained injuries;
- injuries in unusual locations;
- multiple injuries of different ages;
- or delay in seeking medical attention.

The leader should avoid premature accusations while ensuring appropriate safeguarding processes are followed.

---

### The Child's Safety Comes First

When safeguarding concerns arise, the priority is the child's immediate safety.

Appropriate senior, safeguarding, paediatric, and social-care pathways should be activated according to local policy.

---

## Communication With Children

### Communication Is Part of Resuscitation

A frightened child may be unable to cooperate fully with assessment.

The team should use:

- simple language;
- calm explanations;
- reassurance;
- age-appropriate communication;
- and appropriate involvement of parents or carers.

---

### Behaviour as a Clinical Sign

A sudden change from crying and interacting to quietness or reduced responsiveness may represent physiological deterioration.

The leader should therefore distinguish between:

- emotional distress;
- pain;
- fear;
- and genuine neurological or physiological deterioration.

---

## Communication With Parents and Carers

Parents and carers may provide important information regarding:

- baseline behaviour;
- medical history;
- medications;
- allergies;
- developmental status;
- and the mechanism.

They may also help calm the child.

However, the need for immediate resuscitation should take priority when the child is critically unwell.

---

## Pain Management

Pain should be assessed and treated appropriately.

Pain may cause:

- tachycardia;
- hypertension;
- agitation;
- crying;
- and increased respiratory effort.

However, analgesia should be administered with appropriate monitoring.

The leader should avoid attributing all abnormal physiology to pain without considering other causes.

---

## Medication Safety

### Weight-Based Dosing

Many paediatric medications are prescribed according to body weight.

The team should confirm:

- weight;
- concentration;
- dose;
- route;
- and maximum dose where applicable.

A medication error can occur when the wrong concentration or adult preparation is used.

---

### Double-Checking

High-risk medication calculations should be checked by another trained team member whenever feasible.

The leader should encourage a culture in which team members can challenge an uncertain dose.

---

## Immobilisation and Spinal Considerations

Spinal precautions should be based on:

- mechanism;
- neurological findings;
- examination;
- and appropriate paediatric trauma guidance.

The team should avoid unnecessary immobilisation when it provides no clinical benefit.

At the same time, suspected significant spinal injury should be managed appropriately.

---

## Pelvic and Limb Injury

The secondary survey should include careful assessment of:

- pelvic stability where clinically appropriate;
- limb deformity;
- pulses;
- motor function;
- sensation;
- and compartment concerns.

A child's smaller blood volume means that significant bleeding from a limb injury may have important physiological consequences.

---

## Temperature and Environment

The resuscitation environment should be warm and controlled.

The team should minimise:

- unnecessary exposure;
- wet clothing;
- cold intravenous fluids where avoidable;
- and prolonged procedures without active warming.

Temperature should be reassessed throughout prolonged resuscitation.

---

## Reassessment

The paediatric trauma patient should be reassessed frequently.

Reassessment should occur:

- after major interventions;
- following changes in vital signs;
- after analgesia or sedation;
- before transfer;
- and whenever the clinical condition changes.

The leader should repeatedly ask:

> **"Is the child improving as expected?"**

Failure to improve should trigger reconsideration of:

- diagnosis;
- adequacy of treatment;
- ongoing haemorrhage;
- airway or breathing problems;
- and occult injury.

---

## Definitive Care

The leader should determine whether the child requires:

- local management;
- paediatric trauma surgery;
- neurosurgical input;
- orthopaedic intervention;
- paediatric intensive care;
- specialist burns care;
- or transfer to a major paediatric trauma centre.

The decision should be made early when the injury pattern is severe.

---

## Transfer

Transfer may be required when the facility cannot provide the necessary:

- paediatric surgical expertise;
- intensive care;
- specialist imaging;
- operative intervention;
- or subspecialty support.

Before transfer, the team should ensure:

- airway stability;
- adequate ventilation;
- haemodynamic support;
- appropriate vascular access;
- analgesia;
- immobilisation where indicated;
- monitoring;
- documentation;
- and clear clinical handover.

---

## Trauma Team Leader Responsibilities

The Trauma Team Leader should:

1. Confirm the child's age and estimated weight.
2. Ensure appropriate paediatric equipment is available.
3. Establish the mechanism and likely injury pattern.
4. Coordinate the <C>ABCDE assessment.
5. Recognise compensated shock.
6. Ensure early control of catastrophic haemorrhage.
7. Anticipate airway deterioration.
8. Coordinate respiratory management.
9. Ensure appropriate vascular access.
10. Coordinate weight-based resuscitation.
11. Prevent hypothermia.
12. Assess neurological status repeatedly.
13. Search actively for occult injury.
14. Use imaging selectively.
15. Consider safeguarding concerns.
16. Communicate clearly with the child and family.
17. Coordinate specialist consultation.
18. Determine the definitive destination.
19. Reassess after major interventions.
20. Escalate early when the child's trajectory is worsening.

---

## Common Cognitive Errors

### Treating the Child as a Small Adult

Adult thresholds, equipment, doses, and physiological assumptions may be inappropriate.

The leader should ensure age- and weight-appropriate management.

---

### Reassurance From Normal Blood Pressure

A normal blood pressure does not exclude significant blood loss.

The entire physiological picture must be considered.

---

### Attributing Tachycardia to Distress

Pain and fear may cause tachycardia.

However, persistent tachycardia may also indicate:

- haemorrhage;
- hypoxia;
- or other physiological compromise.

The cause must be actively assessed.

---

### Delaying Treatment for Imaging

A critically unstable child should not be sent for imaging when immediate treatment is required.

The leader should prioritise life-saving intervention.

---

### Missing Hypothermia

Children lose heat rapidly.

Hypothermia prevention should begin early and continue throughout resuscitation.

---

### Underestimating Occult Injury

Limited external findings do not exclude significant internal injury.

Mechanism and physiology should guide suspicion.

---

### Failing to Consider Safeguarding

Not every injury is accidental.

The team should recognise concerning patterns and activate appropriate safeguarding processes when required.

---

## Case Resolution

Returning to the opening scenario, the child has several findings that require immediate attention.

The mechanism indicates high-energy trauma.

The combination of:

- altered consciousness;
- tachycardia;
- cool peripheries;
- chest bruising;
- abdominal bruising;
- and a painful thigh injury

requires a systematic trauma assessment.

The leader should not be reassured by the relatively preserved blood pressure.

The team should immediately assess for:

- major haemorrhage;
- thoracic injury;
- traumatic brain injury;
- abdominal injury;
- spinal injury;
- and limb injury.

Appropriate vascular access should be obtained rapidly, and haemorrhage control and resuscitation should proceed according to the child's condition and local paediatric trauma protocol.

The child should be kept warm and reassessed frequently.

Further imaging should be guided by the clinical questions and the child's physiological stability.

The overall objective is to identify life-threatening injury early while preventing secondary physiological deterioration.

---

## Clinical Pearl

> **In paediatric trauma, deterioration may occur before hypotension becomes obvious.**

The Trauma Team Leader must therefore recognise the early signs of physiological compromise and integrate:

**Mechanism → Age → Weight → Injury pattern → Physiology → Response → Trajectory**

---

## Key Takeaways

- Children are not simply small adults.
- Age and weight influence assessment, equipment, medication, and resuscitation.
- Children may compensate for significant blood loss before hypotension develops.
- Tachycardia, altered behaviour, poor peripheral perfusion, and increasing respiratory effort may be important early warning signs.
- Major haemorrhage should be identified and controlled rapidly.
- Paediatric airway anatomy increases the consequences of airway obstruction and oedema.
- Significant chest injury may occur without obvious rib fractures.
- Weight-based management requires careful calculation and communication.
- Imaging should answer a clinical question and minimise unnecessary radiation exposure.
- A stable blood pressure does not exclude significant injury.
- Hypothermia prevention is an important component of paediatric trauma care.
- Associated injuries may be occult.
- Safeguarding concerns should be recognised and managed through appropriate pathways.
- Communication with children and families is an important part of trauma leadership.
- Specialist paediatric trauma input and transfer should be considered early when required.
- Reassessment remains essential throughout the child's care.
