# Chapter 7 — Roles and Responsibilities

## Clinical Scenario

A major trauma patient is arriving after a high-speed road traffic collision.

The pre-alert reports:

- Adult patient
- High-energy mechanism
- Suspected chest and abdominal trauma
- Possible pelvic injury
- Ongoing haemodynamic instability
- Estimated arrival in 5 minutes

The trauma team is assembled.

One doctor is preparing the airway equipment. Another is checking vascular access and blood products. Nursing staff are preparing monitoring and warming equipment. A surgical clinician is reviewing the mechanism and anticipating operative needs.

The patient arrives.

Within seconds, several people begin speaking simultaneously.

One clinician starts assessing the airway.

Another asks for a pelvic binder.

A nurse reports that the blood pressure is falling.

Someone asks whether a chest drain is required.

The radiographer is asking whether imaging is needed.

The team leader now has a problem that is not primarily clinical knowledge.

The problem is **coordination**.

A trauma team can contain highly capable clinicians and still perform poorly if responsibilities are unclear.

The trauma team leader must therefore establish:

> Who is doing what?

> Who is responsible for each critical task?

> Who is communicating the findings?

> Who is anticipating the next step?

> Who is maintaining the overall picture?

The purpose of this chapter is to define those responsibilities and explain how the trauma team leader allocates, supervises and reassigns tasks during major trauma resuscitation.

---

# Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the major roles within a trauma team.
2. Understand the distinction between clinical task performance and team leadership.
3. Allocate tasks according to competence and clinical need.
4. Establish clear responsibility for critical interventions.
5. Recognise when roles need to change during resuscitation.
6. Maintain clear communication between team members.
7. Avoid duplication and omission of important tasks.
8. Delegate effectively while retaining overall situational awareness.
9. Identify when additional expertise is required.
10. Use structured role allocation before and during trauma resuscitation.

---

# 1. Why Roles Matter

Major trauma is a team sport.

The outcome does not depend solely on the knowledge or technical ability of one clinician.

It depends on multiple people performing multiple tasks simultaneously while the patient's condition may change rapidly.

The team therefore needs:

- Clear leadership
- Clear roles
- Clear task ownership
- Clear communication
- Appropriate escalation
- Continuous reassessment

Without defined roles, several predictable problems occur.

## Common consequences of poorly defined roles

### Duplication

Two clinicians perform the same task while another critical task is neglected.

### Omission

Everybody assumes that somebody else is responsible.

### Conflicting instructions

Different clinicians give competing directions.

### Delayed intervention

A task is recognised but nobody has explicit responsibility for completing it.

### Loss of situational awareness

Multiple clinicians become focused on individual tasks and nobody maintains the overall picture.

### Communication overload

Too many people speak simultaneously.

### Unnecessary escalation

Senior clinicians become involved in tasks that could safely be delegated while critical decisions receive insufficient attention.

The solution is not simply to have more people.

The solution is to have **clear responsibility**.

---

# 2. The Principle of Task Ownership

Every important task should have an identifiable owner.

A useful rule is:

> If a task matters, someone should know that they own it.

Examples include:

- Airway assessment
- Airway intervention
- Cervical spine protection
- Oxygenation
- Ventilation assessment
- Chest examination
- Vascular access
- Blood sampling
- Blood product administration
- Pelvic stabilisation
- Neurological assessment
- Exposure
- Temperature control
- Monitoring
- Medication preparation
- Documentation
- Blood bank communication
- Imaging coordination
- Surgical escalation

Task ownership does not mean that one person must perform every component personally.

It means that one person is responsible for ensuring that the task happens.

---

# 3. The Trauma Team Leader

The trauma team leader has overall responsibility for coordinating the resuscitation.

The leader should not automatically perform the most technically demanding procedure.

The leader's primary responsibility is to maintain the global clinical picture and coordinate the team.

## Core responsibilities

The trauma team leader should:

- Establish priorities
- Allocate tasks
- Coordinate the primary survey
- Integrate clinical information
- Identify life-threatening problems
- Make or coordinate major decisions
- Request appropriate assistance
- Reassess response to interventions
- Anticipate deterioration
- Communicate the overall plan
- Ensure that important tasks have owners
- Maintain situational awareness
- Decide when the patient needs to move to the next phase of care

The leader should continually ask:

> What is killing this patient?

> What has already been done?

> What remains undone?

> What has changed?

> What needs to happen next?

---

# 4. The Leader Should Not Do Everything

One of the most important principles of trauma leadership is:

> The leader coordinates the work rather than becoming absorbed in it.

A common error is for the leader to start performing a procedure and lose awareness of the rest of the team.

For example, if the trauma team leader personally performs a difficult vascular access procedure while the patient is deteriorating, the team may temporarily lose its central coordinator.

This does not mean that the leader can never perform a procedure.

There may be circumstances in which this is appropriate.

However, before becoming task-focused, the leader should consider:

1. Is the procedure urgent?
2. Can another competent clinician perform it?
3. Is the team currently functioning effectively?
4. Will performing the procedure reduce my ability to coordinate?
5. Is there another task only I can perform?

The leader should retain the highest-level view whenever possible.

---

# 5. The Airway Clinician

A designated clinician should have responsibility for airway management when the team structure permits.

Responsibilities may include:

- Initial airway assessment
- Oxygenation
- Airway positioning
- Suction
- Airway adjuncts
- Preparation for definitive airway management
- Preoxygenation
- Intubation when indicated
- Confirmation of airway placement
- Ventilation
- Ongoing airway reassessment

The airway clinician should communicate significant findings to the team leader.

For example:

> "Airway currently patent. GCS 7. Poor airway protection. I recommend definitive airway management."

The airway clinician should also anticipate difficulty.

Potential concerns include:

- Facial trauma
- Blood or vomit
- Cervical spine injury
- Distorted anatomy
- Severe hypoxia
- Limited mouth opening
- Entrapped patient
- Haemodynamic instability

---

# 6. Airway Assistant

Airway management should not be considered a single-person task.

An assistant may be responsible for:

- Preparing equipment
- Checking suction
- Preparing airway adjuncts
- Passing equipment
- Assisting with positioning
- Applying external laryngeal manipulation when requested
- Preparing alternative airway equipment
- Securing equipment after intubation

The assistant should anticipate the next requirement rather than waiting passively for every instruction.

---

# 7. Breathing and Chest Team

A designated clinician should assess the patient's breathing and chest.

Responsibilities may include:

- Respiratory rate
- Oxygen saturation
- Chest movement
- Chest examination
- Identification of immediately life-threatening thoracic injury
- Assessment for tension pneumothorax
- Assessment for open chest injury
- Assessment for massive haemothorax
- Assessment for flail chest
- Recognition of respiratory deterioration
- Coordination of chest interventions

The clinician should communicate concise findings.

For example:

> "Absent air entry on the left with severe respiratory distress and hypotension."

The team leader should then integrate this information with the circulation findings.

---

# 8. Circulation Clinician

The circulation role focuses on perfusion and haemorrhage.

Responsibilities may include:

- Assessment of circulation
- Identification of external haemorrhage
- Vascular access
- Blood sampling
- Blood gas analysis
- Recognition of shock
- Initiation of haemorrhage control
- Coordination of blood products
- Assessment of response to resuscitation

The circulation clinician should identify whether shock is likely to be haemorrhagic and communicate the working diagnosis clearly.

For example:

> "Persistent shock despite initial intervention. Major haemorrhage remains the leading concern."

---

# 9. Haemorrhage Control Role

Depending on local team structure, a separate clinician or member of the circulation team may focus specifically on haemorrhage control.

Responsibilities may include:

- Direct pressure
- Tourniquet application
- Wound packing
- Pelvic binder application
- Identification of ongoing external bleeding
- Coordination with surgical teams
- Preparation for definitive haemorrhage control

The trauma team should distinguish between:

**Temporary control**

and

**Definitive control.**

A tourniquet may temporarily control bleeding.

A pelvic binder may reduce pelvic volume and haemorrhage.

Neither necessarily represents definitive management.

The leader must therefore ask:

> What is controlling the bleeding now?

and:

> What will definitively control it?

---

# 10. Nursing Team

Trauma nurses frequently have multiple simultaneous responsibilities.

These may include:

- Monitoring
- Vascular access assistance
- Blood sampling
- Medication preparation
- Blood product administration
- Equipment preparation
- Temperature management
- Documentation
- Medication recording
- Coordination with laboratory and blood bank services

The nursing team should have clear task allocation.

For example:

- Nurse 1 — monitoring and observations
- Nurse 2 — vascular access and blood sampling
- Nurse 3 — medications and blood products

The exact structure will depend on staffing and local practice.

The principle remains the same:

> Critical tasks require explicit ownership.

---

# 11. Monitoring Responsibility

One team member should have clear responsibility for monitoring.

This includes:

- Heart rate
- Blood pressure
- Oxygen saturation
- Respiratory rate
- Temperature
- ECG
- End-tidal carbon dioxide when applicable
- Trends rather than isolated values

The monitoring clinician should communicate important changes immediately.

For example:

> "Systolic pressure has fallen from 90 to 65."

This is more useful than simply reading individual values without interpretation.

---

# 12. Medication Responsibility

Medication administration can become chaotic during trauma resuscitation.

A designated clinician should, where possible, coordinate:

- Analgesia
- Sedation
- Induction drugs
- Paralytic agents when required
- Vasopressors when indicated
- Antifibrinolytic therapy when indicated
- Other emergency medications

Medication administration should be verbalised clearly.

A useful communication structure is:

> Drug — dose — route — indication.

For example:

> "Tranexamic acid, according to the major haemorrhage protocol."

Exact dosing should follow current institutional and national protocols.

---

# 13. Documentation Role

Documentation is often neglected during major trauma.

One person should ideally be responsible for recording:

- Arrival time
- Mechanism
- Initial observations
- Major findings
- Interventions
- Medication administration
- Blood products
- Procedures
- Imaging
- Clinical response
- Major decisions
- Times of escalation
- Transfer destination

The documentation role should not be confused with leadership.

The recorder records.

The leader leads.

---

# 14. The Scribe Should Not Become the Leader

A common failure occurs when the person documenting the case becomes responsible for coordinating it.

The roles are different.

The leader should make decisions.

The scribe should document decisions.

The scribe may appropriately clarify:

> "What time was the first unit started?"

But should not be expected to independently coordinate the resuscitation.

---

# 15. Surgical Team

The surgical team may become involved early when major trauma is suspected.

Responsibilities may include:

- Surgical assessment
- Identification of injuries requiring operative management
- Haemorrhage control planning
- Operative decision-making
- Coordination with theatre
- Coordination with other specialties

The trauma team leader should communicate the clinical problem rather than simply requesting "a surgeon."

A useful escalation statement is:

> "We have an unstable patient with suspected intra-abdominal haemorrhage despite ongoing resuscitation. We need urgent surgical assessment for definitive haemorrhage control."

---

# 16. Anaesthesia Team

Where available, anaesthesia clinicians may provide expertise in:

- Airway management
- Ventilation
- Anaesthesia
- Haemodynamic support
- Critical care
- Perioperative management

The trauma team leader should use this expertise early when appropriate.

However, responsibility for the overall trauma resuscitation should remain clearly defined.

---

# 17. Radiology and Imaging Staff

Imaging personnel are an important component of trauma care.

The trauma team leader should communicate:

- Patient stability
- Clinical question
- Urgency
- Relevant mechanism
- Suspected injuries

Imaging should support clinical decision-making rather than delay necessary life-saving interventions.

The question should be:

> What information do we need from imaging, and will obtaining it change management?

---

# 18. Blood Bank and Laboratory Communication

Major haemorrhage frequently requires coordination beyond the resuscitation room.

A designated team member may communicate with:

- Blood bank
- Laboratory
- Haematology
- Chemistry laboratory
- Transfusion services

The communication should be explicit.

For example:

> "Major haemorrhage protocol activated. We require ongoing blood product support."

The team should know:

- What has been requested
- What has arrived
- What has been administered
- What remains required

---

# 19. Team Leader and Role Allocation

Before patient arrival, the leader should allocate major roles whenever possible.

A simple briefing may sound like:

> "I am leading."

> "Dr A, airway."

> "Dr B, breathing."

> "Dr C, circulation."

> "Nurse A, monitoring."

> "Nurse B, access and bloods."

> "Nurse C, drugs and blood products."

> "Scribe, documentation."

> "Please speak up immediately if you identify a life threat."

This takes only seconds.

It can prevent significant confusion.

---

# 20. Role Allocation Should Be Explicit

Avoid vague instructions.

### Weak

> "Someone get IV access."

### Better

> "Sarah, obtain two large-bore IVs."

### Weak

> "Can someone call blood bank?"

### Better

> "Ahmed, activate the major haemorrhage protocol and confirm when blood is available."

The second form creates accountability.

---

# 21. Closed-Loop Task Allocation

When a task is assigned, the recipient should acknowledge it.

Leader:

> "Ahmed, activate the major haemorrhage protocol."

Team member:

> "Activating the major haemorrhage protocol."

Later:

> "Major haemorrhage protocol activated. Blood is on the way."

This creates a closed communication loop.

It confirms:

1. The instruction was heard.
2. The instruction was understood.
3. The task was performed.
4. The leader receives confirmation.

---

# 22. Delegation Does Not Mean Abandonment

Delegation transfers responsibility for a task.

It does not mean that the leader forgets about it.

If the leader asks:

> "Please obtain vascular access."

The leader should later establish:

> "Do we have access?"

and:

> "What is the current status of access?"

Delegation therefore requires **follow-through**.

---

# 23. Matching Tasks to Competence

Tasks should be allocated according to:

- Competence
- Experience
- Scope of practice
- Availability
- Urgency
- Complexity

The most senior clinician should not necessarily perform every task.

Similarly, a junior clinician should not be assigned a task beyond their competence simply because nobody else is available without appropriate supervision.

The leader must understand the capabilities of the team.

---

# 24. Dynamic Role Allocation

Roles are not fixed permanently.

The team should adapt as the situation changes.

For example:

During initial resuscitation:

- One clinician focuses on airway.
- One focuses on breathing.
- One focuses on circulation.

Later, once the airway is secured:

The airway clinician may become available to assist with:

- Ventilation
- Ultrasound
- Procedures
- Preparation for transfer

The leader should consciously redistribute available resources.

---

# 25. Avoid Role Drift

Role drift occurs when a team member gradually moves away from their assigned responsibility without the team recognising it.

For example:

A clinician assigned to circulation becomes absorbed in a neurological assessment while nobody is monitoring haemorrhage.

The leader should notice this.

Useful phrases include:

> "Stay with circulation."

or:

> "I need you to continue monitoring the blood loss."

Clear language prevents ambiguity.

---

# 26. The Leader's Resource Management

The leader should continuously assess:

- What resources are available?
- What resources are missing?
- Which tasks are currently covered?
- Which tasks are becoming overloaded?
- What additional expertise is required?

Resources include:

- People
- Equipment
- Blood products
- Theatre
- Interventional radiology
- Imaging
- Anaesthesia
- Surgical specialties
- Intensive care

The leader should escalate before resources become critical.

---

# 27. Calling for Help Early

A major trauma team should not wait until the patient has deteriorated completely before requesting assistance.

Early escalation may involve:

- Senior trauma clinician
- Anaesthesia
- Surgery
- Orthopaedics
- Neurosurgery
- Interventional radiology
- Intensive care
- Paediatrics
- Obstetrics

The exact team depends on the injury pattern and local system.

A useful principle is:

> Anticipate the next problem, not only the current problem.

---

# 28. The Two-Level Model

A useful mental model is to divide the team into two levels.

## Level 1 — Task performers

These clinicians perform specific tasks.

Examples:

- Airway management
- Vascular access
- Chest decompression
- Haemorrhage control
- Ultrasound
- Medication administration

## Level 2 — Coordination

The trauma team leader:

- Integrates findings
- Establishes priorities
- Coordinates tasks
- Anticipates deterioration
- Makes major decisions
- Communicates the plan

The leader must avoid becoming trapped at Level 1.

---

# 29. Maintaining the Big Picture

The trauma team leader should periodically pause mentally and ask:

> What is happening now?

> What are the major threats?

> What has been treated?

> What remains untreated?

> Is the patient responding?

> What is the next major decision?

This prevents task fixation.

---

# 30. When Roles Become Unclear

If the team becomes chaotic, the leader can reset the structure.

For example:

> "Pause for a moment."

> "I am leading."

> "Airway — status?"

> "Breathing — status?"

> "Circulation — status?"

> "What are we doing for haemorrhage control?"

> "What is the next priority?"

A brief reset can restore control.

---

# 31. Role Allocation in a Small Team

Not every trauma centre has a large team.

A small team may require individuals to hold multiple responsibilities.

For example:

| Team member | Responsibilities |
|---|---|
| Team leader | Overall coordination |
| Airway clinician | Airway + breathing |
| Circulation clinician | Access + haemorrhage control |
| Nurse 1 | Monitoring + medications |
| Nurse 2 | Access + blood products |
| Scribe | Documentation + communication |

The exact structure should be adapted to available staff.

The principle remains:

> Every critical task must have an owner.

---

# 32. Role Allocation in a Large Team

A larger team allows greater specialisation.

Possible roles include:

- Team leader
- Airway lead
- Airway assistant
- Breathing clinician
- Circulation clinician
- Procedure clinician
- Nurse coordinator
- Medication nurse
- Blood product nurse
- Monitor nurse
- Scribe
- Runner
- Radiographer
- Surgical representative
- Anaesthetic representative

The leader should prevent unnecessary duplication.

More people do not automatically mean better care.

---

# 33. Communication Between Roles

Each role should communicate information that affects the overall plan.

For example:

Airway:

> "Intubation successful. End-tidal CO₂ confirmed."

Breathing:

> "Persistent unilateral absent breath sounds."

Circulation:

> "Blood pressure remains low despite transfusion."

Nursing:

> "Second unit is running."

Scribe:

> "The first blood product started at 14:32."

These reports allow the leader to maintain a coherent picture.

---

# 34. Speak Up

Every team member should feel able to identify a concern.

Examples:

> "I am concerned the patient is deteriorating."

> "We have not addressed the pelvic haemorrhage."

> "The oxygen saturation is falling."

> "We have not confirmed the airway."

The leader should actively encourage this behaviour.

A useful statement during the briefing is:

> "If you see something that concerns you, say it immediately."

---

# 35. Psychological Safety

Effective trauma teams require an environment where appropriate challenge is accepted.

A junior clinician should be able to say:

> "I am concerned this patient has a tension pneumothorax."

without fear of inappropriate criticism.

The leader should respond to concerns professionally and rapidly.

This does not mean that every suggestion must be accepted.

It means that clinically relevant concerns should be heard and evaluated.

---

# 36. The Leader Should Name the Problem

When possible, the leader should verbalise the working diagnosis.

For example:

> "This patient is in haemorrhagic shock until proven otherwise."

or:

> "The immediate threat is obstructive shock from suspected tension pneumothorax."

Naming the problem aligns the team.

It tells the team:

- What matters
- What should be prioritised
- What interventions are likely to follow

---

# 37. Anticipatory Delegation

Good leaders delegate before the next problem occurs.

For example:

> "While we establish access, prepare the blood warmer and activate the major haemorrhage protocol."

Or:

> "While the airway is being secured, prepare for chest decompression if the patient's respiratory status deteriorates."

Anticipatory delegation reduces delay.

---

# 38. The One-Step-Ahead Principle

The leader should try to remain at least one step ahead.

If the patient is unstable:

Do not wait until the patient arrests to consider where definitive haemorrhage control will occur.

If the airway is difficult:

Do not wait until failed intubation to consider the rescue strategy.

If blood products are being administered rapidly:

Do not wait until they run out to request more.

This is the difference between reactive and anticipatory leadership.

---

# 39. Handover Between Team Members

If a team member needs to leave or change roles, responsibility should be explicitly transferred.

For example:

> "I am handing circulation to Dr Ahmed."

Receiving clinician:

> "I have circulation."

The outgoing clinician should briefly communicate:

- Current problem
- Interventions completed
- Pending tasks
- Concerns

This prevents loss of information.

---

# 40. Role Transfer During Deterioration

When the patient's condition changes, the leader may need to reorganise the team.

For example:

> "We now have worsening shock. Dr Ahmed, stay with circulation. Dr Sara, focus on haemorrhage control. Nurse Ali, blood products. I will coordinate definitive haemorrhage control."

The leader should state the new priorities clearly.

---

# 41. Common Pitfall: Everybody Does Everything

This produces:

- Duplication
- Confusion
- Delayed intervention
- Poor communication

The solution is explicit task ownership.

---

# 42. Common Pitfall: Nobody Owns the Task

Example:

> "Someone should call theatre."

No one does.

Better:

> "Dr Ahmed, call theatre now and tell them we have an unstable patient with suspected intra-abdominal haemorrhage."

---

# 43. Common Pitfall: The Leader Becomes a Proceduralist

The leader performs a procedure and stops coordinating the team.

The solution is to delegate where possible and periodically step back to reassess the entire resuscitation.

---

# 44. Common Pitfall: Excessive Delegation

Delegation can also go too far.

The leader should not issue instructions without understanding what is happening.

Delegation requires supervision.

---

# 45. Common Pitfall: Micromanagement

Micromanagement occurs when the leader attempts to control every minor detail.

Examples:

- Giving unnecessary instructions about routine tasks
- Repeatedly interrupting competent clinicians
- Focusing on equipment details rather than priorities

The leader should intervene when necessary while allowing competent team members to perform their roles.

---

# 46. Common Pitfall: Failure to Reassign Resources

A task may be completed while another task becomes urgent.

If the leader does not redistribute staff, resources may remain attached to low-priority work.

The leader should ask:

> "Where is the greatest current need?"

---

# 47. Common Pitfall: Silent Team Members

A quiet team is not necessarily a safe team.

The leader should actively request updates.

For example:

> "Airway status?"

> "Breathing?"

> "Circulation?"

> "What is the latest blood pressure?"

> "How much blood has been given?"

---

# 48. Common Pitfall: Unclear Authority

Everyone should know who is leading.

At the start:

> "I am the trauma team leader."

If leadership changes:

> "Dr Ahmed is now leading the resuscitation."

Clear leadership reduces conflicting decisions.

---

# 51. Case-Based Discussion

## Case

A 28-year-old man is brought after a motorcycle collision.

On arrival:

- GCS 13
- HR 135
- BP 78/48
- RR 30
- SpO₂ 92%
- Obvious deformity of the pelvis
- Active bleeding from the thigh
- Cold and confused

The team leader assigns:

- Airway clinician
- Breathing clinician
- Circulation clinician
- Haemorrhage control clinician
- Nursing roles
- Scribe

The circulation clinician reports:

> "Severe shock."

The haemorrhage control clinician reports:

> "Active thigh haemorrhage controlled with a tourniquet."

The team leader asks:

> "What is our next major threat?"

The team recognises possible pelvic and internal haemorrhage.

A pelvic binder is applied.

Blood products are started.

The leader then asks:

> "What is our definitive haemorrhage control plan?"

The team begins preparation for definitive management.

## Discussion

The important point is not simply that individual interventions were performed.

The important point is that:

- Tasks were allocated.
- Responsibilities were explicit.
- Findings were communicated.
- The leader integrated the information.
- Temporary haemorrhage control was distinguished from definitive control.
- The team anticipated the next stage of care.

This is effective trauma team coordination.

---

# 52. Leadership During Multiple Simultaneous Problems

Major trauma frequently produces several competing priorities.

For example:

- Difficult airway
- Hypoxia
- Severe haemorrhage
- Pelvic injury
- Traumatic brain injury

The team leader cannot personally manage all of these.

Instead, the leader should:

1. Identify immediate threats.
2. Allocate tasks.
3. Establish priorities.
4. Ensure parallel work.
5. Integrate the results.
6. Reassess continuously.

This is the essence of team-based resuscitation.

---

# 53. Parallel Processing

A major advantage of trauma teams is the ability to perform tasks simultaneously.

For example:

While:

- Airway is assessed,

another clinician can:

- Assess breathing,

while another:

- Establishes vascular access,

while nursing staff:

- Prepare blood products,

and the leader:

- Coordinates the overall resuscitation.

The leader should actively use this parallel capacity.

---

# 54. Avoiding Sequential Thinking

A poorly coordinated team may work sequentially:

1. Finish airway.
2. Then assess breathing.
3. Then obtain access.
4. Then think about blood.

A trauma team should instead perform appropriate tasks in parallel.

The primary survey provides a structure for prioritisation, but it does not require the team to behave as though only one person can work at a time.

---

# 55. Role Clarity and Patient Safety

Clear roles improve:

- Accountability
- Communication
- Efficiency
- Situational awareness
- Error detection
- Team coordination

They also make it easier to identify when something has not happened.

The leader can ask:

> "Who owns this task?"

If nobody can answer, the task has not been adequately allocated.

---

# 56. Role Clarity and Cognitive Load

Major trauma produces high cognitive workload.

Clear roles reduce unnecessary cognitive demand.

Instead of every clinician asking:

> "What should I be doing?"

they know:

> "This is my responsibility."

This allows the team to focus cognitive resources on clinical care.

---

# 57. Role Clarity and Human Factors

Human factors are not separate from clinical care.

They directly influence:

- Communication
- Decision-making
- Situational awareness
- Error detection
- Workload
- Team performance

Role allocation is therefore a clinical safety intervention.

---

# 58. The Leader's Final Responsibility

Even when tasks are delegated, the leader retains responsibility for coordinating the overall resuscitation.

The leader should therefore remain aware of:

- The patient's physiological state
- The major injuries
- The interventions performed
- The response to treatment
- The unresolved problems
- The destination of the patient

The leader's job is not to personally perform every task.

It is to ensure that the right tasks are performed at the right time.

---

# Key Takeaways

1. Every critical task should have an identifiable owner.
2. The trauma team leader coordinates rather than performs everything.
3. Roles should be allocated explicitly.
4. Delegation should be matched to competence.
5. Delegated tasks require follow-through.
6. Team members should communicate important changes clearly.
7. Closed-loop communication improves reliability.
8. Roles should change dynamically as the resuscitation evolves.
9. The leader must maintain the global clinical picture.
10. Parallel processing is one of the major strengths of a trauma team.
11. Additional expertise should be requested early when needed.
12. Everyone should be empowered to speak up about safety concerns.
13. Clear leadership prevents conflicting decisions.
14. Role clarity reduces duplication and omission.
15. The ultimate responsibility of the leader is to ensure that important things get done in the right order, by the right person, at the right time.
