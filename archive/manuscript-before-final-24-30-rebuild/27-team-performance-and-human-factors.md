# Chapter 27 — Team Performance and Human Factors

## Team Performance and Human Factors in Trauma Leadership

> A trauma team can contain highly skilled individuals and still perform poorly as a team.

The trauma team leader is therefore not responsible only for the clinical management of the injured patient.

The leader is also responsible for creating the conditions in which the team can perform safely.

That means managing:

- people
- information
- workload
- communication
- priorities
- interruptions
- uncertainty
- competing demands
- cognitive bias
- stress
- fatigue
- conflict
- hierarchy
- equipment
- time
- and the team's overall performance

Trauma resuscitation is a high-risk human performance environment.

The patient may be deteriorating rapidly.

The diagnosis may be incomplete.

Multiple clinicians may be performing simultaneous interventions.

Several decisions may need to be made within seconds.

Information may arrive from different sources.

The team may contain clinicians who have never worked together before.

A specialist may arrive midway through the resuscitation.

A procedure may fail.

A physiological parameter may change unexpectedly.

A second life-threatening problem may appear while the team is still managing the first.

Under these conditions, technical competence is necessary but insufficient.

The trauma team leader must understand how humans actually behave under pressure.

That is the foundation of human factors in trauma leadership.

---

# 27.1 What Are Human Factors?

Human factors describes the interaction between:

- people
- tasks
- technology
- environment
- organisations
- and systems

Human factors asks a practical question:

> Why does performance change when the environment changes?

A clinician may perform an excellent procedure during a quiet daytime shift.

The same clinician may make an error during a noisy, crowded, sleep-deprived, high-acuity resuscitation.

The difference may not be knowledge.

The difference may be the system surrounding the person.

Human factors therefore moves the focus away from:

> "Who made the mistake?"

towards:

> "What conditions made the mistake more likely?"

This does not remove individual accountability.

It improves understanding of why errors occur and how they can be prevented.

In trauma care, human factors are particularly important because the environment contains many conditions known to impair performance:

- time pressure
- uncertainty
- high cognitive workload
- emotional stress
- interruptions
- noise
- fatigue
- incomplete information
- rapidly changing priorities
- team turnover
- unfamiliar personnel
- hierarchy
- communication failures
- equipment problems
- and competing clinical tasks

The leader must actively manage these conditions.

---

# 27.2 Technical Skills Are Only One Part of Performance

Trauma education traditionally focuses heavily on technical competence.

Examples include:

- airway management
- chest decompression
- haemorrhage control
- vascular access
- pelvic stabilisation
- blood product administration
- imaging
- operative decision-making
- and procedural skills

These skills are essential.

However, trauma team performance also depends on non-technical skills.

These include:

- leadership
- communication
- situational awareness
- decision-making
- teamwork
- task management
- resource management
- conflict management
- anticipation
- mutual support
- and the ability to recognise deteriorating team performance

A technically excellent clinician can therefore become an ineffective trauma team leader if they:

- become task-focused
- stop communicating
- fail to delegate
- ignore concerns
- lose situational awareness
- become overwhelmed
- become fixated on one diagnosis
- or attempt to perform too many tasks personally

The leader's job is not simply to perform.

The leader's job is to make the team perform.

---

# 27.3 The Trauma Team Is a High-Reliability System

A trauma team should function as a coordinated system rather than a collection of individuals.

The team should have:

1. a shared objective
2. defined roles
3. a clear leader
4. shared situational awareness
5. structured communication
6. task allocation
7. cross-monitoring
8. mutual support
9. escalation pathways
10. and mechanisms for recovery when something goes wrong

The team should continuously answer:

> What is happening?

> What matters most now?

> What could kill the patient next?

> Who is doing what?

> What information are we missing?

> What has changed?

> What are we going to do next?

> What could prevent us from doing it?

These questions are not administrative.

They are clinical safety mechanisms.

---

# 27.4 The Difference Between Individual Performance and Team Performance

Individual performance asks:

> "Can this clinician perform the task?"

Team performance asks:

> "Can the team coordinate the tasks required to care for the patient?"

Consider an airway emergency.

The airway clinician may be highly competent.

However:

- the monitor may not be visible
- suction may not be available
- drugs may not be prepared
- the tube may not be secured
- ventilation may not be confirmed
- the surgeon may not know that the airway is failing
- the blood bank may not know that massive haemorrhage is occurring

The individual procedure may therefore be technically successful while the overall resuscitation is failing.

The leader must monitor both levels.

---

# 27.5 Team Performance Is Dynamic

Team performance changes throughout a resuscitation.

A team may begin well.

Then:

- the patient deteriorates
- additional staff arrive
- the room becomes crowded
- several people start giving instructions
- the diagnosis becomes uncertain
- an intervention fails
- a consultant disagrees with the current plan
- blood products arrive
- imaging becomes available
- or the patient develops a second major problem

The team that performed well five minutes ago may no longer be functioning well.

The leader must therefore continuously reassess the team itself.

Not only:

> "How is the patient?"

but also:

> "How is the team functioning?"

---

# 27.6 The Four Domains of Effective Team Performance

A useful framework is to continuously monitor four domains:

## 27.6.1 Leadership

The leader:

- establishes priorities
- communicates the plan
- allocates tasks
- coordinates specialists
- monitors progress
- manages resources
- anticipates deterioration
- and maintains overall situational awareness

## 27.6.2 Situation Monitoring

The team:

- observes the patient
- monitors physiological changes
- tracks procedures
- recognises emerging problems
- watches workload
- and identifies threats to the plan

## 27.6.3 Mutual Support

Team members:

- help one another
- redistribute workload
- identify problems
- speak up
- provide assistance
- and prevent individual overload

## 27.6.4 Communication

The team:

- shares relevant information
- gives clear instructions
- confirms critical information
- communicates changes
- and closes the communication loop

These domains are interconnected.

Poor leadership produces poor coordination.

Poor coordination produces poor situational awareness.

Poor situational awareness produces poor decisions.

Poor communication prevents the team from correcting the problem.

---

# 27.7 The Leader Must Remain Hands-Off

One of the most important principles in trauma team leadership is:

> The leader should lead the resuscitation rather than become the resuscitationist.

The leader should avoid unnecessary technical tasks when another appropriately skilled team member can perform them.

This allows the leader to:

- observe the entire room
- monitor the patient
- monitor the team
- anticipate problems
- make decisions
- communicate priorities
- coordinate specialists
- and maintain the overall plan

The leader should be prepared to intervene directly when necessary.

However, performing a task should be a deliberate decision.

Ask:

> "Does the team need me to perform this task, or does the team need me to lead?"

---

# 27.8 The Cost of Task Fixation

Task fixation occurs when attention becomes concentrated on one activity while other important information is neglected.

Examples include:

- repeated attempts at intubation
- prolonged vascular access attempts
- focusing exclusively on a fracture
- repeatedly interpreting one imaging abnormality
- continuing CPR without reassessing reversible causes
- or concentrating on a procedure while haemorrhage continues elsewhere

The task may be clinically important.

The problem is not performing it.

The problem is allowing it to dominate attention.

The leader should periodically ask:

> "What else is happening?"

and:

> "What problem is currently killing the patient?"

---

# 27.9 Cognitive Load

Cognitive load refers to the amount of mental processing required at a given time.

Trauma creates substantial cognitive load.

The leader may need to process:

- airway status
- breathing
- circulation
- neurological status
- exposure
- vital signs
- blood loss
- procedures
- medications
- investigations
- consultant input
- blood availability
- theatre availability
- transfer requirements
- and competing team requests

Trying to remember everything internally is unsafe.

The solution is to externalise information.

Use:

- verbal summaries
- written documentation
- visible monitors
- task allocation
- checklists
- structured handovers
- repeated reassessment
- and closed-loop communication

The team should function as an external cognitive support system.

---

# 27.10 Cognitive Offloading

The leader should deliberately reduce unnecessary cognitive load.

For example:

Instead of remembering:

> "We need two large-bore IVs, bloods, crossmatch, TXA, warming, pelvic binder, blood products and anaesthetic review."

Delegate:

> "Sarah, obtain two large-bore IVs and send the trauma blood panel."

> "Ahmed, activate the massive haemorrhage protocol."

> "Anaesthesia, please assess the airway and prepare for RSI."

> "Nurse in charge, start active warming."

The leader can then monitor completion rather than trying to perform or remember every task.

---

# 27.11 Workload Management

Workload is not distributed equally during resuscitation.

One team member may become overwhelmed.

Another may have little to do.

This creates an opportunity for the leader.

The leader should continually ask:

> "Who is overloaded?"

and:

> "Who has capacity?"

Work can then be redistributed.

For example:

> "Ahmed is managing airway and ventilation. Sarah, please take over medication preparation."

or:

> "I need someone to monitor the blood products and temperature."

or:

> "Can someone take over documentation so that the nurse can assist with the procedure?"

Workload management is a patient-safety intervention.

---

# 27.12 Signs of Team Overload

The leader should recognise early warning signs.

These include:

- repeated requests for the same information
- missed tasks
- delayed responses
- unclear instructions
- multiple people talking simultaneously
- increasing noise
- forgotten medications
- incomplete documentation
- repeated procedural attempts
- team members becoming visibly distressed
- equipment not being prepared
- failure to respond to monitor changes
- conflicting instructions
- and loss of the overall plan

When these occur:

> Stop.

> Reorganise.

> Reprioritise.

> Communicate.

The goal is not to maintain the appearance of control.

The goal is to restore functional control.

---

# 27.13 Stress and Performance

Stress affects human performance.

A moderate degree of arousal may increase attention and performance.

Excessive stress can impair:

- working memory
- communication
- decision-making
- fine motor skills
- situational awareness
- flexibility
- and ability to process new information

This is particularly relevant during:

- traumatic cardiac arrest
- paediatric trauma
- major haemorrhage
- airway failure
- difficult intubation
- unexpected deterioration
- death of a patient
- or a resuscitation involving multiple casualties

The leader cannot remove stress.

The leader can structure the environment around the team.

---

# 27.14 Calm Is a Leadership Tool

The leader's behaviour influences the team.

If the leader becomes visibly panicked:

- team members may become anxious
- communication may deteriorate
- multiple people may act independently
- priorities may become unclear
- and the room may become chaotic

Calm leadership does not mean passive leadership.

A calm leader can be extremely directive.

For example:

> "We have a peri-arrest patient with uncontrolled haemorrhage."

> "Everyone stop for a moment."

> "We are prioritising haemorrhage control and oxygenation."

> "Ahmed, airway."

> "Sarah, activate massive haemorrhage."

> "James, prepare for chest decompression."

> "I will coordinate the overall resuscitation."

This is calm, clear and decisive.

---

# 27.15 Fatigue

Fatigue is a major human factor.

Trauma teams may operate:

- overnight
- after prolonged shifts
- during busy emergency department periods
- after multiple previous resuscitations
- or during periods of staff shortage

Fatigue can impair:

- attention
- reaction time
- memory
- judgement
- communication
- and decision-making

The leader should recognise that fatigue affects the whole team.

A fatigued clinician should not be treated as though performance is unchanged.

The appropriate response is not criticism.

It is risk management.

Possible strategies include:

- task redistribution
- additional supervision
- double-checking critical medications
- involving a second experienced clinician
- reducing unnecessary interruptions
- and escalating when capacity is insufficient

---

# 27.16 The Authority Gradient

Healthcare is hierarchical.

A consultant may be reluctant to challenge another consultant.

A junior doctor may hesitate to challenge a senior doctor.

A nurse may notice a problem but remain silent.

A paramedic may have important prehospital information but feel unable to interrupt.

This is dangerous.

The leader must actively reduce inappropriate authority gradients.

The team should understand:

> Patient safety outranks hierarchy.

A junior team member who identifies a life-threatening problem should be able to speak.

---

# 27.17 Speaking Up

Speaking up means communicating a concern when patient safety may be compromised.

Examples:

> "I am concerned that the patient's blood pressure is falling."

> "We have not confirmed tube placement."

> "There is still uncontrolled external bleeding."

> "The pelvic binder has not been applied."

> "I think we may be missing a chest injury."

> "I am not comfortable proceeding with this plan."

The correct leader response is:

> "Thank you. Tell me what you are seeing."

This converts a potential challenge into useful clinical information.

---

# 27.18 Psychological Safety

Psychological safety means that team members can raise concerns, ask questions and admit uncertainty without fear of humiliation.

A psychologically safe trauma team is not a team without authority.

It is a team where authority does not prevent information from travelling.

The leader should encourage:

- questions
- concerns
- alternative interpretations
- uncertainty
- requests for help
- and escalation

Useful phrases include:

> "If anyone sees something I am missing, say it."

> "I want concerns raised early."

> "If you disagree with the plan, tell me."

> "If you are overloaded, tell me."

> "If you are unsure, ask."

These statements should be supported by behaviour.

If a team member speaks up and is dismissed repeatedly, future speaking-up behaviour will decline.

---

# 27.19 Cross-Monitoring

Team members should monitor one another.

This is not micromanagement.

It is a safety net.

Examples:

- the airway clinician monitors ventilation
- the nurse monitors medication administration
- the surgeon monitors bleeding
- the anaesthetist monitors physiological response
- the team leader monitors the whole system

Cross-monitoring can detect:

- missed tasks
- incorrect assumptions
- deteriorating physiology
- procedural complications
- excessive workload
- and communication failures

The team should not depend on one individual noticing everything.

---

# 27.20 Mutual Support

Mutual support means that team members actively help one another achieve the team's objective.

It includes:

- assistance
- feedback
- workload redistribution
- error prevention
- and emotional support

For example:

> "You have been attempting the IV for several minutes. I'll take over."

or:

> "I'll document while you prepare the airway."

or:

> "I'll monitor the blood products while you perform the procedure."

The aim is not to protect individuals from responsibility.

The aim is to protect the patient from predictable human limitations.

---

# 27.21 Error Detection

Errors are inevitable in complex systems.

The goal of a high-performing team is not to create a team that never makes mistakes.

The goal is to create a team capable of:

1. detecting errors early
2. correcting them quickly
3. preventing propagation
4. and learning from them

Examples:

> "The wrong medication was prepared."

The team should correct it before administration.

> "The tube position is uncertain."

The team should stop and verify.

> "The blood product is incompatible."

The team should stop administration and escalate.

The leader should avoid creating an environment where people hide errors.

Hidden errors are more dangerous than acknowledged errors.

---

# 27.22 The Error Chain

A small error can become a major event.

For example:

1. unclear instruction
2. task misunderstood
3. task not completed
4. leader assumes task completed
5. patient deteriorates
6. team responds to the consequence rather than the original problem

Closed-loop communication interrupts this chain.

So does cross-monitoring.

So does explicit task tracking.

So does frequent reassessment.

---

# 27.23 Checkbacks

A checkback is confirmation that information has been received and understood.

Leader:

> "Give 1 gram calcium."

Team member:

> "1 gram calcium."

Team member:

> "Calcium given."

Leader:

> "Confirmed."

This creates a communication loop.

Critical information should not disappear into the room.

---

# 27.24 Shared Mental Model

A shared mental model means that team members have a common understanding of:

- the patient's condition
- current priorities
- treatment goals
- roles
- expected next steps
- and major threats

Without a shared mental model, individuals may work against one another.

For example:

The surgeon may think:

> "We are preparing for theatre."

The anaesthetist may think:

> "We are still attempting stabilisation."

The nurse may think:

> "We are waiting for CT."

The team leader may think:

> "We are activating massive haemorrhage."

All four may be acting rationally from their own information.

The system is still unsafe.

The leader must create a shared picture.

---

# 27.25 The Running Summary

A running summary is one of the simplest ways to maintain a shared mental model.

For example:

> "This is a 32-year-old male after high-speed collision."

> "He has severe chest trauma and suspected pelvic haemorrhage."

> "Airway is currently patent."

> "He remains hypotensive despite initial blood products."

> "We are controlling haemorrhage and reassessing the chest."

> "If he remains unstable, we are moving towards definitive haemorrhage control."

The summary should evolve as the situation changes.

---

# 27.26 Information Management

Not all information deserves equal attention.

The leader must distinguish:

### Critical information

Information that may immediately change management.

### Important information

Information that influences the next phase of treatment.

### Background information

Information that may be relevant but does not require immediate action.

During resuscitation:

> Critical information must reach the leader rapidly.

Too much information can be as harmful as too little.

---

# 27.27 Noise Management

Trauma rooms are noisy environments.

Sources include:

- monitors
- suction
- equipment alarms
- multiple conversations
- phones
- radio communication
- additional personnel
- imaging discussions
- and family or external communication

Noise increases the risk of missed communication.

The leader should control unnecessary noise.

Useful interventions include:

> "One voice at a time."

> "I need silence for the next instruction."

> "Only critical updates."

> "Please hold non-urgent discussions outside the room."

This is not authoritarian behaviour.

It is information management.

---

# 27.28 Interruptions

Interruptions are common during trauma care.

Some are important.

Others are not.

The leader should protect critical tasks from unnecessary interruption.

For example, during:

- medication preparation
- airway procedures
- blood product administration
- critical imaging interpretation
- or a major decision

the team should avoid irrelevant conversation.

A simple phrase can help:

> "Hold non-urgent issues until we complete this step."

---

# 27.29 Distractions

A distraction can cause attention to move away from the most important problem.

Examples:

- an abnormal but non-critical laboratory result
- a phone call
- a new person entering the room
- a minor injury discovered during examination
- a debate about a low-priority investigation

The leader should repeatedly ask:

> "Does this change what we need to do now?"

If not:

> "We will return to it later."

---

# 27.30 Confirmation Bias

Confirmation bias occurs when people favour information that supports an existing belief.

Example:

The team believes the patient has isolated abdominal bleeding.

A new chest finding is therefore interpreted as unimportant.

The leader should deliberately challenge assumptions.

Ask:

> "What else could explain this?"

> "What would we expect if our diagnosis is wrong?"

> "What are we missing?"

> "Is there a second life-threatening injury?"

---

# 27.31 Anchoring

Anchoring occurs when the team becomes attached to an initial diagnosis or interpretation.

Examples:

- "The paramedics said he has pelvic bleeding."
- "The scan shows a chest injury."
- "The patient is hypotensive because of blood loss."
- "The airway is difficult because of facial trauma."

These statements may be correct.

But the leader must remain willing to update the model.

The question is:

> "Does the current patient state still fit our explanation?"

If not, reassess.

---

# 27.32 Premature Closure

Premature closure occurs when the team stops searching after finding one explanation.

In trauma, multiple injuries are common.

Finding one major injury does not exclude another.

The leader should therefore maintain a systematic approach.

The primary survey remains important even when a dramatic injury has been identified.

---

# 27.33 Fixation Error

Fixation is excessive focus on one aspect of the problem.

Common examples include:

- airway fixation
- procedural fixation
- imaging fixation
- bleeding-source fixation
- diagnostic fixation

The leader should periodically force a reset.

A useful phrase is:

> "Let's step back."

Then:

> "What is the patient's current physiology?"

> "What remains uncontrolled?"

> "What is most likely to kill the patient next?"

---

# 27.34 Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

Trauma leaders rarely have complete information.

Decisions often have to be made before:

- imaging is available
- laboratory results return
- a specialist arrives
- blood products are fully prepared
- or the diagnosis is confirmed

The leader must therefore act on the best available information while remaining ready to revise the plan.

Good trauma leadership is not about being certain.

It is about being appropriately decisive despite uncertainty.

---

# 27.35 The OODA Concept

A useful conceptual model is:

### Observe

What is happening?

### Orient

What does it mean?

### Decide

What should we do?

### Act

Execute the decision.

Then repeat.

The cycle is continuous.

A leader who acts without observing may intervene incorrectly.

A leader who observes indefinitely without deciding may lose time.

A leader who decides without orienting may misinterpret the situation.

The best teams cycle rapidly through:

> Observe → Orient → Decide → Act → Reassess

---

# 27.36 Team Adaptability

A trauma team must adapt when circumstances change.

Examples:

- unexpected airway difficulty
- sudden cardiovascular collapse
- failed procedure
- massive haemorrhage
- equipment failure
- arrival of additional specialists
- transfer delay
- multiple casualties

The leader should recognise when the original plan is no longer appropriate.

Then:

1. stop
2. reassess
3. communicate
4. reallocate
5. act

A plan is not a commitment to continue doing something that is failing.

---

# 27.37 Failure Recovery

High-performing teams are distinguished by their ability to recover from problems.

A failed intubation does not mean failure of the team.

A missed piece of information does not have to become a missed diagnosis.

A delayed blood product does not have to become catastrophic deterioration.

The leader should convert:

> "Something went wrong."

into:

> "What do we need to do now?"

---

# 27.38 Escalation

The leader should recognise when the current team does not have sufficient capability.

Escalation may involve:

- senior trauma surgery
- anaesthesia
- emergency medicine
- neurosurgery
- orthopaedics
- interventional radiology
- blood bank
- operating theatre
- ICU
- paediatric specialists
- or external transfer resources

Escalation should occur early enough to be useful.

Calling for help is not a sign of weak leadership.

Failure to call for help when the situation exceeds team capability is poor leadership.

---

# 27.39 Resource Management

Human factors includes management of resources.

Resources include:

- personnel
- equipment
- blood products
- operating theatre
- imaging
- medications
- transport
- specialist expertise
- time

The leader should ask:

> "What resource will we need next?"

rather than:

> "What resource do we need now?"

Anticipation creates time.

---

# 27.40 The Principle of Early Activation

Some resources have significant lead times.

Examples:

- massive transfusion
- operating theatre
- interventional radiology
- specialist surgical teams
- transfer
- paediatric retrieval teams

The leader should activate these resources when the probability of needing them becomes significant.

Waiting for absolute certainty may create dangerous delays.

---

# 27.41 Managing the Arrival of New Team Members

New clinicians often enter during an active resuscitation.

This can disrupt team performance.

The leader should rapidly orient them.

For example:

> "We have a 24-year-old male after high-speed collision."

> "Current problems are severe haemorrhage and chest trauma."

> "Airway is currently controlled."

> "We are activating massive transfusion."

> "You are taking responsibility for the airway."

This prevents the new team member from trying to reconstruct the entire case independently.

---

# 27.42 Avoiding Team Crowding

More people do not automatically produce better care.

Additional personnel can improve capability.

They can also increase:

- noise
- confusion
- communication burden
- duplication
- unclear responsibility
- and interference with procedures

The leader should therefore distinguish:

> capability

from:

> crowding.

Every person in the room should have a useful role.

---

# 27.43 Role Clarity

Every team member should know:

- who is leading
- what their task is
- who they report to
- what information they should communicate
- and what they should do if the situation changes

Ambiguous roles create duplicated work and omissions.

A simple rule is:

> If a task is important, assign an owner.

Instead of:

> "Someone get blood."

say:

> "Sarah, activate massive transfusion."

---

# 27.44 Team Handover

When leadership changes, the transfer should be explicit.

The outgoing leader should communicate:

- patient identity
- mechanism
- current injuries
- physiological status
- interventions completed
- response to treatment
- unresolved problems
- current priorities
- and anticipated next steps

The incoming leader should confirm understanding.

Leadership should not change silently.

---

# 27.45 Managing Conflict

Conflict is inevitable in multidisciplinary trauma care.

It may arise from:

- different clinical opinions
- competing priorities
- unclear roles
- resource limitations
- stress
- hierarchy
- or previous disagreements

The leader should focus on the patient rather than personalities.

A useful structure is:

> "We have two competing priorities."

> "Let's identify the immediate threat."

> "What evidence supports each option?"

> "What is the safest next step?"

Conflict becomes dangerous when it consumes cognitive bandwidth.

---

# 27.46 Assertive Leadership

The leader must sometimes be directive.

Examples:

> "Stop the procedure."

> "We are moving to the next airway strategy."

> "Activate massive transfusion now."

> "Prepare for theatre."

> "Everyone pause."

Assertiveness is appropriate when:

- time is critical
- the patient is deteriorating
- the team is becoming disorganised
- or there is significant disagreement

Being assertive is different from being disrespectful.

The leader can be direct while maintaining professional respect.

---

# 27.47 Adaptive Leadership

There is no single leadership style appropriate for every trauma.

The leader may need to be:

### Directive

When the patient is crashing.

### Collaborative

When several options exist.

### Consultative

When specialist expertise is required.

### Delegative

When a competent team member can independently manage a defined task.

### Coaching

When developing less experienced team members during lower-risk situations.

Leadership should adapt to:

- patient severity
- team experience
- time pressure
- uncertainty
- and available expertise.

---

# 27.48 Team Experience

A highly experienced team may require less detailed direction.

A newly assembled or inexperienced team may require:

- more explicit instructions
- clearer task allocation
- more frequent summaries
- closer monitoring
- and earlier intervention

The leader should adjust the amount of structure accordingly.

---

# 27.49 Human Factors During Massive Haemorrhage

Massive haemorrhage is a particularly demanding human factors environment.

Potential problems include:

- multiple blood products
- medication administration
- warming
- monitoring
- laboratory testing
- procedures
- surgery
- communication with blood bank
- documentation
- and rapidly changing physiology

The leader should establish clear responsibilities.

For example:

> "One person owns blood product coordination."

> "One person tracks physiology."

> "One person communicates with the laboratory."

> "One person documents."

> "One clinician controls the haemorrhage."

This creates functional separation of tasks.

---

# 27.50 Human Factors During Difficult Airway

A difficult airway creates intense cognitive pressure.

The team may become fixated on securing the airway.

The leader should protect against tunnel vision.

Ask:

> "What is our oxygenation strategy?"

> "What is our backup plan?"

> "What happens if this attempt fails?"

> "Who is performing the procedure?"

> "Who is monitoring physiology?"

> "When do we stop?"

> "When do we move to the rescue strategy?"

The leader should ensure that the team has a shared escalation plan before repeated attempts consume time.

---

# 27.51 Human Factors During Traumatic Cardiac Arrest

Traumatic cardiac arrest is particularly vulnerable to cognitive overload.

The leader must ensure that the team does not simply continue a familiar algorithm without addressing reversible traumatic causes.

The team should maintain awareness of:

- haemorrhage
- airway and oxygenation
- tension pneumothorax
- tamponade where relevant
- and other reversible causes according to the clinical scenario and local protocol

The leader should verbalise priorities.

For example:

> "We are treating this as traumatic arrest."

> "I want simultaneous management of airway, haemorrhage and reversible chest causes."

This creates a shared mental model.

---

# 27.52 The Leader's Internal Checklist

The leader can periodically ask:

### Patient

- What is the patient's current physiology?
- What is killing the patient?
- What has changed?

### Team

- Is everyone clear about their role?
- Is anyone overloaded?
- Is anyone underutilised?
- Is anyone not being heard?

### Communication

- Are instructions clear?
- Are critical tasks confirmed?
- Is information reaching the right person?

### Resources

- Do we have what we need?
- What will we need next?
- Have escalation pathways been activated?

### Cognition

- Are we becoming fixated?
- Are we anchored?
- What are we missing?
- Does our diagnosis still fit?

### Plan

- What are we doing now?
- What is the next step?
- What happens if the current plan fails?

---

# 27.53 Recognising Deteriorating Team Performance

The leader should recognise patterns rather than isolated errors.

A team may be deteriorating when:

- the same question is asked repeatedly
- people give contradictory instructions
- tasks are duplicated
- tasks are forgotten
- the room becomes increasingly noisy
- nobody can state the current plan
- specialists work independently
- team members stop communicating
- the leader becomes absorbed in one task
- or the team stops reassessing the patient

These are warning signs.

The correct response is often a brief team reset.

---

# 27.54 The Team Reset

A team reset can take less than 20 seconds.

The leader can say:

> "Team, pause."

> "Current problem: ongoing shock."

> "Airway is controlled."

> "Haemorrhage remains the priority."

> "We are giving blood and preparing for definitive haemorrhage control."

> "Sarah, update me on blood."

> "Ahmed, update me on the airway."

> "James, what is the surgical plan?"

> "Any concerns or anything I am missing?"

Then continue.

A reset restores the shared mental model.

---

# 27.55 The Power of a Single Question

One of the most valuable questions in trauma leadership is:

> "What am I missing?"

This invites the team to contribute.

Another useful question is:

> "What is the biggest threat right now?"

Another:

> "What will happen next if we do nothing?"

And:

> "What do we need to prepare for now?"

These questions improve anticipation.

---

# 27.56 Team Performance Is Not Static

A trauma team can move through several phases:

### Formation

People assemble.

### Orientation

Roles and priorities are established.

### Action

Tasks are performed.

### Adaptation

The plan changes as information changes.

### Crisis

The patient deteriorates or the initial plan fails.

### Recovery

The team stabilises the patient and transitions to definitive care.

### Reflection

The team reviews performance.

The leader should recognise the phase and adjust leadership accordingly.

---

# 27.57 Training Human Factors

Human factors should not be learned only by reading.

They require practice.

Useful training methods include:

- simulation
- trauma team drills
- video review
- structured observation
- team performance scoring
- communication exercises
- crisis resource management
- scenario-based leadership training
- and structured debriefing

Simulation allows teams to practise behaviours that are difficult to teach during real emergencies.

---

# 27.58 What Should Be Practised?

Teams should deliberately practise:

- briefing
- role allocation
- closed-loop communication
- leadership transitions
- speaking up
- managing disagreement
- managing workload
- responding to failed procedures
- escalation
- crisis communication
- team reset
- and debriefing

Technical procedures should remain part of training.

But the team should also train how to coordinate those procedures.

---

# 27.59 Observation and Feedback

Human factors are observable.

A team can be assessed for:

### Leadership

- Is the leader identifiable?
- Are priorities clear?
- Is the plan communicated?
- Are tasks delegated?

### Communication

- Are instructions audible?
- Are critical instructions confirmed?
- Are changes communicated?

### Situational awareness

- Does the team recognise deterioration?
- Does the leader maintain the overview?
- Are emerging threats anticipated?

### Teamwork

- Do team members assist each other?
- Is workload distributed?
- Are concerns raised?

### Decision-making

- Are decisions timely?
- Is uncertainty acknowledged?
- Are assumptions challenged?

These behaviours can be improved through feedback.

---

# 27.60 Debriefing as a Human Factors Intervention

Debriefing should not be reserved for obvious failure.

A team can debrief after an excellent resuscitation.

Questions include:

> What went well?

> What did not go well?

> What surprised us?

> What nearly went wrong?

> Was everyone clear about their role?

> Did anyone have difficulty speaking up?

> Were there communication failures?

> Was workload appropriately distributed?

> What should we change next time?

The goal is learning, not blame.

---

# 27.61 The Leader's Responsibility for Team Culture

Team culture develops through repeated behaviour.

If the leader:

- listens
- respects contributions
- welcomes concerns
- acknowledges uncertainty
- admits mistakes
- asks for help
- and corrects errors constructively

the team learns that these behaviours are acceptable.

If the leader:

- humiliates people
- ignores concerns
- discourages questions
- rewards silence
- or treats hierarchy as more important than safety

the team learns to remain silent.

Leadership therefore shapes culture one resuscitation at a time.

---

# 27.62 The Leader Should Model the Behaviour

If the leader wants the team to:

- speak up
- communicate clearly
- admit uncertainty
- ask for help
- and reassess

the leader must demonstrate those behaviours.

For example:

> "I am not certain that we have identified the source of shock."

This is not weakness.

It is cognitive transparency.

The leader can then ask:

> "What are we missing?"

---

# 27.63 Managing the Emotional Environment

Trauma can be emotionally intense.

Patients may be young.

Family members may be present.

Injuries may be severe.

A death may occur.

Team members may know the patient.

The leader should recognise the emotional impact without allowing emotion to destroy team function.

The leader can say:

> "This is a difficult situation. We will stay focused on the patient and work through it systematically."

After the event, emotional support and appropriate debriefing may be required.

---

# 27.64 Human Factors and Patient Safety

Patient safety is not separate from human factors.

Many adverse events involve interactions between:

- people
- communication
- processes
- equipment
- environment
- and organisational systems

The leader cannot fix every system problem during a resuscitation.

But the leader can recognise them.

Examples:

- inadequate equipment
- staffing shortage
- unclear protocol
- delayed blood availability
- poor communication pathways
- lack of escalation access
- or inadequate training

These should be identified and addressed at the system level after the event.

---

# 27.65 Latent Conditions

Some risks exist before the patient arrives.

Examples:

- equipment not checked
- unclear role allocation
- inadequate staffing
- unavailable specialist
- poor room layout
- missing medication
- unclear escalation process
- or insufficient training

These are latent conditions.

The individual clinician may encounter the problem during the resuscitation, but the underlying risk existed beforehand.

Human factors therefore extends beyond the resuscitation room.

---

# 27.66 The Trauma Leader as a Systems Thinker

The trauma leader should think at three levels.

### Level 1 — Patient

What does the patient need?

### Level 2 — Team

What does the team need to deliver that care?

### Level 3 — System

What does the system need to allow the team to perform effectively?

A strong leader moves between all three levels.

---

# 27.67 Performance Under Pressure

Pressure changes behaviour.

Under extreme pressure, people may:

- narrow attention
- speak less
- speak louder
- become task-focused
- skip steps
- become less flexible
- or become excessively controlling

The leader should recognise these predictable responses.

The objective is not to eliminate pressure.

It is to build structures that allow safe performance despite pressure.

---

# 27.68 The Human Factors Approach to Error

When an error occurs, ask:

### Individual

Was the person appropriately trained?

### Task

Was the task clear?

### Team

Was communication effective?

### Environment

Was the environment suitable?

### Equipment

Was the required equipment available and functioning?

### System

Were protocols and escalation pathways clear?

### Organisation

Were staffing and resources adequate?

This produces a more useful analysis than simply asking:

> "Who made the mistake?"

---

# 27.69 The Five-Second Pause

Before major decisions, a brief pause can prevent errors.

The leader can ask:

> "Pause."

Then:

1. What is the patient's current physiology?
2. What is the immediate threat?
3. What are we doing?
4. What happens if it fails?
5. What do I need the team to do now?

This should not delay urgent life-saving treatment.

It is a cognitive reset when the situation allows.

---

# 27.70 When the Room Becomes Chaotic

Chaos is usually a symptom.

The leader should not respond to chaos by becoming part of it.

Instead:

> Stop unnecessary activity.

> Identify the current threat.

> Establish the plan.

> Assign roles.

> Restore communication.

> Resume action.

A useful command is:

> "Everyone pause. Listen."

Then give the plan.

---

# 27.71 The Leader's Voice

The leader's voice should be:

- audible
- calm
- direct
- specific
- respectful
- and predictable

Avoid vague instructions.

Instead of:

> "Someone should probably get some blood."

say:

> "Sarah, activate the massive haemorrhage protocol now."

Instead of:

> "We need to think about the airway."

say:

> "Anaesthesia, please assess the airway now and tell me your plan."

Precision reduces ambiguity.

---

# 27.72 The Team Member's Responsibility

Human factors is not only the leader's responsibility.

Every team member should:

- know their role
- listen
- communicate clearly
- monitor the patient
- monitor colleagues
- speak up
- request help
- acknowledge uncertainty
- and support the team

A team cannot be safe if every member waits for the leader to notice every problem.

---

# 27.73 The Leader Should Encourage Challenge

A useful team rule is:

> Challenge early, respectfully and specifically.

Instead of:

> "This isn't right."

say:

> "I am concerned because the blood pressure has fallen despite transfusion."

Instead of:

> "You shouldn't do that."

say:

> "I am concerned that another attempt may delay the rescue airway plan."

Specific concerns are easier to evaluate.

---

# 27.74 Escalation Language

Teams should have language that clearly signals urgency.

Examples:

> "I am concerned."

> "I am seriously concerned."

> "I think this is unsafe."

> "Stop."

> "We need senior help now."

The leader should respond to these statements.

A safety concern should never disappear into the noise of the room.

---

# 27.75 Human Factors and the Primary Survey

Human factors should support—not replace—the structured trauma assessment.

The primary survey provides a framework for clinical prioritisation.

Human factors ensures that the framework is executed effectively.

The leader therefore combines:

> clinical algorithm + team coordination + situational awareness

rather than relying on any one of them alone.

---

# 27.76 Human Factors and Secondary Survey

Once immediate threats are addressed, the team must avoid losing momentum.

The leader should ensure:

- completion of the secondary survey
- review of investigations
- reassessment of physiology
- identification of missed injuries
- and planning for definitive care

Team performance can deteriorate after initial stabilisation because urgency decreases.

The leader must maintain structured attention.

---

# 27.77 Human Factors During Transfer

Transfer creates another high-risk period.

Before transfer, the leader should confirm:

- airway
- breathing
- circulation
- monitoring
- access
- medications
- blood products
- documentation
- imaging
- receiving team
- destination
- and escort requirements

The team should know:

> Where are we going?

> Why are we going?

> Who is responsible?

> What could deteriorate during transfer?

---

# 27.78 Human Factors During Handover

Handover is a transfer of situational awareness.

A poor handover can undo excellent resuscitation.

The handover should communicate:

- mechanism
- injuries
- physiology
- interventions
- response
- current problems
- outstanding investigations
- treatment priorities
- and anticipated deterioration

The receiving team should have the opportunity to clarify.

---

# 27.79 The Transition From Resuscitation to Definitive Care

A successful resuscitation is not the end.

The leader must ensure that the patient transitions safely to:

- operating theatre
- interventional radiology
- ICU
- ward
- imaging
- transfer
- or another definitive care pathway

The team should know the destination and rationale.

---

# 27.80 A Practical Human Factors Model for the Trauma Leader

Throughout the resuscitation, continuously monitor:

## Patient

> What is happening?

## Priorities

> What matters most?

## People

> Who is doing what?

## Performance

> Is the team functioning effectively?

## Information

> What do we know and what are we missing?

## Resources

> What do we need next?

## Risks

> What could fail?

## Future

> What is likely to happen next?

This model keeps the leader above the individual tasks.

---

# 27.81 The "Hands-Off, Eyes-Up" Principle

A useful mental model is:

> Hands off. Eyes up.

The leader should remain physically and cognitively available to:

- observe
- listen
- decide
- communicate
- anticipate
- and coordinate

The leader should intervene directly when necessary.

But unnecessary technical involvement should be delegated.

---

# 27.82 The "Name, Task, Confirmation" Rule

For critical tasks:

### Name

Identify the person.

### Task

State exactly what needs to be done.

### Confirmation

Require acknowledgement.

Example:

> "Ahmed, obtain two large-bore IVs."

> "Two large-bore IVs."

Then later:

> "IV access obtained."

This reduces ambiguity.

---

# 27.83 The "Now, Next, If" Framework

A powerful leadership communication structure is:

### Now

What are we doing immediately?

### Next

What happens after that?

### If

What will we do if the current plan fails?

Example:

> "Now we are controlling the airway."

> "Next we will reassess oxygenation and ventilation."

> "If we cannot oxygenate, we move immediately to the rescue strategy."

This creates a shared mental model and prepares the team for failure.

---

# 27.84 The "What Could Kill the Patient Next?" Question

The leader should repeatedly ask:

> What could kill this patient next?

Possible answers change over time.

Initially:

- airway obstruction

Later:

- uncontrolled haemorrhage

Later:

- chest complication

Later:

- intracranial injury

Later:

- physiological deterioration during transfer

The answer is dynamic.

The leader must remain dynamic.

---

# 27.85 The "What Could Stop Us?" Question

A second useful question is:

> What could prevent us from executing the plan?

Examples:

- blood unavailable
- theatre unavailable
- specialist delayed
- equipment missing
- transfer delayed
- staff overloaded

The leader can act before the problem becomes critical.

---

# 27.86 The "Who Owns It?" Question

Every major task should have an owner.

Ask:

> Who owns this task?

If the answer is unclear:

> Assign someone.

This prevents:

- duplication
- omission
- assumptions
- and diffusion of responsibility.

---

# 27.87 The "Tell Me What You See" Strategy

Instead of asking:

> "Is everything okay?"

ask:

> "What are you seeing?"

This produces more useful information.

For example:

> "Anaesthesia, tell me what you are seeing with the airway."

> "Nurse, tell me what the latest observations are."

> "Surgeon, tell me what you think is driving the shock."

Specific questions improve information flow.

---

# 27.88 Managing the Expert in the Room

Experts can be extremely valuable.

However, expertise can sometimes create conflicting instructions.

The leader should acknowledge expertise while retaining overall coordination.

For example:

> "I want your input on the airway strategy."

Then:

> "I will coordinate the overall plan."

This preserves both specialist expertise and team structure.

---

# 27.89 Managing Multiple Specialists

When multiple specialties arrive, the leader should prevent fragmentation.

Each specialist should understand:

- the overall clinical picture
- their specific role
- the immediate priority
- and how their intervention fits the wider plan

The leader remains responsible for coordination unless leadership is explicitly transferred.

---

# 27.90 Leadership Transfer

Leadership may need to change.

Reasons include:

- arrival of a more appropriate leader
- deterioration requiring a different skill set
- shift change
- fatigue
- or transition to definitive care

The transfer should be explicit.

> "I am transferring leadership to Dr Ahmed."

The new leader should acknowledge:

> "I am taking leadership."

The team should know who is now responsible.

---

# 27.91 When the Leader Becomes Overloaded

Leaders are also human.

A leader should recognise their own cognitive overload.

Warning signs include:

- forgetting tasks
- difficulty processing information
- becoming unusually quiet
- becoming excessively controlling
- losing track of time
- fixation
- irritability
- or inability to summarise the situation

The correct response is to use the team.

Say:

> "I need a brief summary."

or:

> "Someone give me the current physiology."

or:

> "I need another senior clinician to support the decision-making."

Self-awareness is a leadership skill.

---

# 27.92 Asking for Help

The strongest leaders know when they need help.

Useful phrases:

> "I need another senior clinician."

> "I need anaesthesia now."

> "I need surgical leadership."

> "I need help coordinating the room."

> "I am becoming overloaded; I need someone to take over this task."

Help should be requested before the situation becomes unmanageable.

---

# 27.93 The Team as a Cognitive Network

A trauma team can function as a distributed cognitive system.

Different people hold different information.

For example:

- paramedics know the mechanism
- nurses know medication and physiological trends
- anaesthesia knows airway and ventilation
- surgeons know operative options
- radiology knows imaging
- blood bank knows product availability
- the leader integrates the information

No individual can know everything.

The team's safety depends on information being shared.

---

# 27.94 The Danger of Information Silos

An information silo occurs when important information remains with one person.

Examples:

> "The paramedic knows the patient deteriorated before arrival but nobody tells the team."

> "The nurse knows blood has been delayed but the leader is unaware."

> "Anaesthesia recognises impending airway failure but does not communicate it."

The leader must create pathways for information to travel.

---

# 27.95 Human Factors in Real-World Trauma Teams

Real trauma teams are often imperfect.

They may contain:

- different levels of experience
- temporary staff
- unfamiliar clinicians
- trainees
- rotating teams
- variable staffing
- and different communication styles

The leader should not expect perfect team familiarity.

Instead, create structure.

Structure compensates for unfamiliarity.

---

# 27.96 Standardisation

Standardisation reduces unnecessary variation.

Useful standardised elements include:

- team roles
- trauma call activation
- briefing
- primary survey sequence
- communication terminology
- escalation
- handover
- and debriefing

Standardisation does not mean every patient is managed identically.

It means the team shares a common foundation.

---

# 27.97 Flexibility Within Structure

The trauma leader should avoid two extremes.

### Excessive rigidity

> "We must follow the plan exactly."

### Excessive improvisation

> "Everyone just do what seems appropriate."

The safest approach is:

> Structured flexibility.

Use the standard framework while adapting to the patient.

---

# 27.98 Human Factors and Checklists

Checklists can support memory.

They should not replace clinical judgement.

A checklist is most useful when it:

- reduces omission
- prompts reassessment
- supports communication
- and creates a shared sequence

The leader should avoid turning the checklist into a ritual.

The patient remains the focus.

---

# 27.99 The Team Leader's Mental Position

The leader should mentally position themselves one level above the tasks.

Instead of thinking:

> "I need to intubate."

think:

> "The team needs a secure airway."

Instead of:

> "I need to insert the chest drain."

think:

> "The patient needs treatment of the chest threat."

Instead of:

> "I need to get the CT."

think:

> "We need information that will change management."

This distinction is fundamental.

---

# 27.100 The Leader's Core Function

The trauma team leader's core function can be summarised as:

> **Maintain the clinical overview, create team clarity, protect attention, coordinate resources, anticipate threats, and ensure that the right action is performed by the right person at the right time.**

That is human factors in practice.

---

# 27.101 Practical Trauma Team Performance Checklist

## Before the patient arrives

- Is the leader identified?
- Are roles allocated?
- Is the team briefed?
- Is equipment ready?
- Is communication clear?
- Are escalation pathways known?

## During the primary survey

- Is the leader hands-off?
- Are instructions clear?
- Are tasks acknowledged?
- Is information shared?
- Is the team maintaining situational awareness?
- Is workload distributed?

## During deterioration

- Has the team paused?
- Has the priority been restated?
- Has the plan changed?
- Has additional help been called?
- Is everyone clear about the new plan?

## During complex decision-making

- Are assumptions being challenged?
- Are alternative diagnoses considered?
- Is the team anchored?
- Is the current physiology consistent with the diagnosis?
- What could kill the patient next?

## Before transfer

- Is the destination clear?
- Is the patient stable enough for transfer?
- Are resources available?
- Is the receiving team prepared?
- Has a structured handover occurred?

## After the event

- Was there a debrief?
- What went well?
- What nearly went wrong?
- What should change?
- Were system problems identified?

---

# 27.102 The High-Performance Trauma Team

A high-performance trauma team is not one in which:

- nobody speaks
- nobody questions the leader
- everything goes according to plan
- or nobody makes mistakes.

A high-performance team is one in which:

- roles are clear
- information flows
- concerns are voiced
- workload is managed
- errors are detected
- plans are adapted
- leadership is visible
- specialists cooperate
- and the team continuously reassesses the patient.

The team is resilient because it can recover.

---

# 27.103 The Leader's Final Mental Model

Throughout the resuscitation, the leader should maintain five simultaneous perspectives:

### 1. Patient

> What is happening to the patient?

### 2. Team

> How is the team performing?

### 3. Information

> What do we know, and what are we missing?

### 4. Resources

> What do we need now and next?

### 5. Future

> What is likely to happen next?

If the leader loses one of these perspectives, team performance may deteriorate.

---

# 27.104 The Practical Rule

When the room becomes busy:

> Slow the thinking, not the treatment.

When the room becomes noisy:

> Simplify the communication.

When the team becomes overloaded:

> Redistribute the work.

When the diagnosis becomes uncertain:

> Reassess the patient.

When someone raises a concern:

> Listen.

When a procedure fails:

> Move to the next strategy.

When the leader becomes overloaded:

> Ask for help.

When the plan changes:

> Tell the team.

When the patient stabilises:

> Prepare for the next phase.

When the patient leaves the room:

> Reflect and learn.

---

# 27.105 Final Principle

Human factors is not an additional topic added to trauma leadership.

It is the way trauma leadership works in the real world.

The trauma leader is managing a patient.

At the same time, the leader is managing:

- attention
- information
- people
- workload
- resources
- communication
- uncertainty
- stress
- and time.

The leader must therefore remain clinically engaged without becoming task-fixated.

They must be decisive without becoming authoritarian.

They must invite challenge without losing control.

They must delegate without abandoning responsibility.

They must acknowledge uncertainty without becoming paralysed.

And they must continuously adapt as the patient and team change.

The most important question is not:

> "Can I perform this task?"

It is:

> "What does the patient need the team to achieve next, and how do I make that happen safely?"

That is the essence of human factors in trauma team leadership.

# Key Takeaways

1. **Technical competence alone does not guarantee effective trauma team performance.**

2. **Leadership, communication, situational awareness, teamwork and mutual support are clinical safety skills.**

3. **The trauma leader should generally remain hands-off and maintain the overall picture.**

4. **Human performance deteriorates under excessive workload, stress, fatigue and distraction.**

5. **The team should be used as a cognitive support system.**

6. **Clear role allocation reduces duplication and omission.**

7. **Closed-loop communication reduces communication failure.**

8. **Cross-monitoring allows team members to detect problems early.**

9. **Psychological safety allows concerns to reach the leader.**

10. **Hierarchy should never prevent a team member from raising a patient-safety concern.**

11. **Leaders should actively manage noise, interruptions and distractions.**

12. **Confirmation bias, anchoring and fixation can distort trauma decision-making.**

13. **A shared mental model keeps the team coordinated.**

14. **Team resets are useful when the room becomes chaotic or the clinical situation changes.**

15. **Escalation is a strength, not a failure of leadership.**

16. **Leadership style should adapt to patient severity, team experience and time pressure.**

17. **Human factors includes the environment and system, not just individual behaviour.**

18. **Simulation and structured feedback can improve team performance.**

19. **Debriefing converts experience into learning.**

20. **The leader's ultimate responsibility is to create the conditions in which the team can deliver the right care at the right time.**

# Chapter Summary

The trauma team is a complex human system operating under extreme conditions.

Its performance depends not only on what individual clinicians know or can do, but on how effectively they coordinate their knowledge, skills, information and resources.

The trauma team leader must therefore manage two simultaneous clinical problems:

> the patient's physiology

and

> the team's performance.

The leader maintains the overview, protects attention, distributes workload, creates shared situational awareness, encourages speaking up, anticipates problems, coordinates resources and adapts the plan as the situation evolves.

The safest trauma teams are not those that depend on one exceptional clinician.

They are teams in which every member can contribute information, challenge unsafe assumptions, support colleagues and execute clearly defined tasks within a shared plan.

The leader's role is to make that possible.

> **Good trauma leadership turns individual expertise into coordinated performance.**

> **Human factors turns coordinated performance into reliable patient care.**
