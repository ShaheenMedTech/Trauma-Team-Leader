# Chapter 28 — Error Management, Safety, and Clinical Governance

## Error Management, Safety, and Clinical Governance in Trauma Leadership

> Safe trauma leadership is not only about making the correct clinical decision. It is also about recognising error, protecting the patient, maintaining reliable systems, and ensuring that learning leads to change.

The trauma team leader operates at the intersection of clinical care, human performance, patient safety, and clinical governance.

This chapter therefore brings together two closely related responsibilities:

- preventing and managing error during trauma care
- creating accountability and governance structures that make safer care sustainable

The leader must protect the patient in the immediate moment while also recognising the system factors that determine whether similar problems will recur.


Trauma resuscitation is a high-risk clinical environment in which errors can occur despite good intentions, appropriate knowledge, and experienced clinicians.

The trauma team leader has a central responsibility for creating a system in which errors are recognised early, their consequences are limited, and the team can continue to function safely under pressure.

The objective is not to create an environment in which nobody ever makes a mistake.

The objective is to create an environment in which:

- important problems are identified early
- critical actions are not missed
- unsafe assumptions are challenged
- errors are detected before they cause harm whenever possible
- team members feel able to speak up
- the team responds to errors without losing situational awareness
- learning occurs after the event
- systems are improved rather than simply blaming individuals

The trauma team leader therefore acts not only as a clinical decision-maker, but also as a **safety controller**.

---

# 28.1 The Nature of Error in Trauma Resuscitation

Errors during trauma care can arise from many sources.

A clinician may have incomplete information.

The patient's condition may change rapidly.

The environment may be noisy.

Multiple interventions may occur simultaneously.

Several clinicians may be communicating at the same time.

Equipment may be unfamiliar or unavailable.

The team may be affected by fatigue, stress, interruptions, or cognitive overload.

Errors should therefore be understood as a product of both:

1. individual human performance
2. the system in which that performance occurs

A technically competent clinician can make an error in a poorly designed system.

Similarly, a well-designed system can reduce the consequences of individual mistakes.

The trauma team leader should constantly consider both.

---

# 28.2 Error Does Not Always Mean Negligence

It is important to distinguish between an error and misconduct.

A clinician may perform an action that was reasonable based on the information available at the time, but the action may later prove to have been incorrect.

This is different from deliberately ignoring a known safety requirement.

The team leader should therefore avoid immediately assigning blame when something goes wrong.

The immediate priority is:

> **Recognise the problem, protect the patient, correct the error, and then understand why it occurred.**

The patient's safety comes before analysis.

---

# 28.3 Types of Error

A practical classification includes:

### Slip

The clinician knows what should be done but performs the wrong action.

Examples:

- selecting the wrong medication
- connecting the wrong line
- pressing the wrong control
- attaching equipment incorrectly

The underlying knowledge may be correct.

The execution is wrong.

---

### Lapse

A lapse is an omission caused by a failure of memory or attention.

Examples:

- forgetting to reassess after an intervention
- failing to document an important finding
- forgetting to check blood availability
- failing to repeat a vital sign

Lapses are particularly important in prolonged resuscitations.

---

### Mistake

A mistake occurs when the plan itself is inappropriate.

Examples include:

- incorrect interpretation of the clinical situation
- inappropriate prioritisation
- failure to recognise deterioration
- selecting an unsuitable treatment strategy

Mistakes may result from inadequate knowledge, faulty reasoning, or incorrect assumptions.

---

### Violation

A violation occurs when a clinician deliberately departs from an accepted rule, protocol, or safety procedure.

Examples may include:

- knowingly bypassing a safety check
- deliberately using equipment in an unsafe manner
- ignoring an established escalation pathway

Not every departure from a protocol is inappropriate.

Clinical circumstances may require adaptation.

The important distinction is whether the deviation was deliberate and unjustified.

---

# 28.4 Active Errors and Latent Conditions

Errors may occur at the bedside, but the conditions producing them may have developed much earlier.

An **active error** occurs close to the patient.

Examples:

- incorrect drug administration
- failure to recognise tension pneumothorax
- incorrect equipment selection

A **latent condition** is a system weakness that makes an active error more likely.

Examples:

- poorly organised equipment
- unclear role allocation
- inadequate staffing
- confusing drug labelling
- insufficient training
- poor communication systems
- unavailable guidelines
- inadequate handover

The trauma team leader may not be able to correct every latent condition during the resuscitation.

However, recognising them is important.

A recurring error may be a symptom of a system problem rather than an isolated individual failure.

---

# 28.5 The First Rule: Protect the Patient

When an error is identified during resuscitation, the immediate response should be clinical.

The team leader should ask:

1. What happened?
2. Has the patient been harmed?
3. Is the error still occurring?
4. What must be done immediately?
5. What additional monitoring is required?
6. Does the treatment plan need to change?

The first priority is always:

> **Stabilise the patient.**

The detailed investigation can follow.

---

# 28.6 Detecting Error Early

Early detection is one of the most important safety functions of the trauma team.

The leader should continuously compare:

**Expected response**

with

**Observed response**

For example:

- Was blood pressure expected to improve?
- Did oxygenation improve?
- Did the respiratory examination change?
- Did the patient's mental state improve?
- Did the heart rate respond?
- Did bleeding actually decrease?
- Did the intervention achieve its intended physiological effect?

Failure to achieve the expected response should trigger reassessment.

A useful mental model is:

> **Intervention → Expected effect → Actual effect → Reassessment**

If the expected effect does not occur, do not simply repeat the same action automatically.

Ask:

> **Why did the patient not respond as expected?**

---

# 28.7 Closed-Loop Safety

Closed-loop communication is not only a communication technique.

It is a safety mechanism.

A typical loop consists of:

1. instruction
2. acknowledgement
3. action
4. confirmation

Example:

**Leader:**
"Give 1 gram of tranexamic acid intravenously."

**Clinician:**
"1 gram tranexamic acid IV."

**Clinician:**
"Tranexamic acid given."

**Leader:**
"Confirmed."

This creates multiple opportunities to detect an error.

The same principle should be applied to high-risk tasks such as:

- airway interventions
- blood administration
- medication administration
- chest decompression
- pelvic binder application
- transfer to theatre
- transfer to CT
- major procedural interventions

---

# 28.8 Read-Back for Critical Information

Some information should be explicitly repeated back.

Examples include:

- drug doses
- blood products
- patient identification
- critical imaging findings
- operative plans
- destination
- major changes in physiology

The leader should not assume that silence means understanding.

A short confirmation is safer than an unverified assumption.

---

# 28.9 Speaking Up

Every member of the trauma team should be able to raise a safety concern.

The team leader should actively create this environment.

Useful phrases include:

> "Stop for a moment."

> "I have a safety concern."

> "Can we reassess?"

> "I am not convinced that this is the correct priority."

> "The patient is deteriorating."

> "I think we may have missed something."

> "Let's confirm the plan."

These statements should be treated as clinical information, not as challenges to authority.

---

# 28.10 The Leader Must Invite Challenge

A strong leader does not simply allow questions.

A strong leader actively invites them.

Examples:

> "Does anyone see a problem with this plan?"

> "What are we missing?"

> "Anaesthesia, are you happy with the airway plan?"

> "Surgery, is there anything that changes your operative decision?"

> "Has anyone noticed a deterioration?"

This is particularly important when the team leader has formed a strong initial diagnosis.

The greater the leader's confidence, the greater the risk of premature closure if contradictory information is ignored.

---

# 28.11 Cognitive Bias and Error

Human reasoning is vulnerable to predictable cognitive biases.

Important examples in trauma include:

### Anchoring

The team becomes fixed on the first diagnosis.

Example:

A patient is labelled as having isolated head injury, and subsequent hypotension is attributed to neurological injury rather than haemorrhage.

---

### Premature Closure

The team stops searching once an apparently satisfactory explanation has been found.

The leader should ask:

> "What else could explain this?"

---

### Confirmation Bias

The team preferentially notices information supporting the existing diagnosis.

Contradictory evidence may be ignored.

---

### Availability Bias

A recently encountered diagnosis becomes disproportionately influential.

---

### Search Satisficing

Once one important injury is found, the team stops looking for additional injuries.

Trauma patients may have multiple simultaneous problems.

Finding one major injury does not exclude another.

---

### Framing Effect

The way information is presented influences clinical interpretation.

For example:

"Fall with chest pain"

may lead the team toward a different mental model than:

"Older patient with unexplained collapse and subsequent fall."

The underlying clinical information may be the same.

---

# 28.12 Countering Cognitive Bias

The team leader can deliberately introduce cognitive forcing strategies.

Useful questions include:

> "What is the most dangerous alternative diagnosis?"

> "What finding does not fit our current diagnosis?"

> "What would we expect to see if our diagnosis were wrong?"

> "What problem could kill this patient in the next five minutes?"

> "Have we explained all of the patient's physiology?"

These questions interrupt automatic thinking.

---

# 28.13 Reassessment After Every Major Intervention

A major intervention should be followed by reassessment.

Examples:

### Airway intervention

Reassess:

- oxygenation
- ventilation
- chest movement
- tube position where appropriate
- capnography where available
- clinical response

### Chest decompression

Reassess:

- respiratory effort
- oxygenation
- chest examination
- haemodynamic response
- ongoing evidence of tension physiology

### Haemorrhage control

Reassess:

- visible bleeding
- circulation
- perfusion
- haemodynamic trend
- need for definitive haemorrhage control

### Blood product administration

Reassess:

- physiological response
- ongoing blood loss
- transfusion requirements
- complications

The principle is:

> **Never assume success. Verify it.**

---

# 28.14 Trends Are Often More Important Than Single Measurements

A single vital sign may be misleading.

The direction of change may be more informative.

For example:

- falling blood pressure
- rising heart rate
- increasing respiratory rate
- worsening mental status
- decreasing oxygen saturation
- increasing vasopressor requirement

may indicate deterioration even when an individual value remains within an apparently acceptable range.

The leader should therefore monitor:

> **Where is the patient going?**

not simply:

> **Where is the patient now?**

---

# 28.15 Checklists as Safety Tools

Checklists can reduce omissions.

They should not replace clinical judgement.

A trauma checklist may include:

- team roles
- airway plan
- breathing assessment
- circulation and haemorrhage control
- disability assessment
- exposure
- analgesia
- imaging
- blood products
- antibiotics where indicated
- tetanus where indicated
- operative or interventional plan
- destination
- ongoing monitoring

The checklist should support the team's mental model rather than distract from the patient.

---

# 28.16 Avoiding Checklist Fatigue

A checklist becomes ineffective when:

- it is excessively long
- it is used mechanically
- nobody listens to it
- it duplicates obvious tasks
- it interrupts urgent clinical care
- responsibility for completing it is unclear

The leader should use checklists selectively and intelligently.

The goal is not to complete a document.

The goal is to prevent important omissions.

---

# 28.17 Medication Safety

Medication errors are particularly dangerous during trauma resuscitation.

Risk increases when:

- several medications are being administered simultaneously
- doses are calculated under pressure
- drug concentrations vary
- syringes are poorly labelled
- multiple clinicians administer drugs
- communication is unclear

The leader should encourage:

- verbal confirmation of high-risk medications
- clear labelling
- dose confirmation
- standard concentrations where possible
- one clinician taking responsibility for medication preparation when practical
- explicit confirmation after administration

---

# 28.18 Blood Product Safety

Major haemorrhage requires particular attention to process.

The team should confirm:

- patient identity
- blood product
- compatibility requirements
- quantity
- route
- indication
- ongoing requirements

The leader should ensure that blood product administration does not become an isolated task disconnected from the overall resuscitation strategy.

The question is not simply:

> "Have we given blood?"

It is:

> "Is haemorrhage still occurring, and is the current resuscitation strategy achieving control?"

---

# 28.19 Equipment-Related Errors

Equipment problems can mimic clinical deterioration.

Examples include:

- disconnected oxygen
- blocked tubing
- incorrect monitor configuration
- empty oxygen cylinder
- inappropriate ventilator settings
- incorrect suction connection
- malfunctioning monitoring equipment

When a patient deteriorates unexpectedly, the leader should consider both:

1. patient deterioration
2. equipment or process failure

A rapid equipment check should not delay essential treatment.

---

# 28.20 When Something Goes Wrong

When an error occurs, the leader should remain calm.

Avoid:

- shouting
- humiliating individuals
- immediate blame
- arguing about responsibility during active resuscitation
- allowing the team to lose focus

Instead:

1. identify the problem
2. stop further harm
3. correct the error
4. reassess the patient
5. communicate the new plan
6. document appropriately
7. escalate when necessary
8. review the event afterwards

The team should see the leader responding to errors as a clinical problem to solve.

---

# 28.21 The "Stop and Reset" Moment

When the situation becomes confused, the leader may need to pause the team.

A short reset can be extremely effective.

For example:

> "Everyone stop for a moment."

> "Current problem: persistent shock."

> "What do we know?"

> "What has already been done?"

> "What has worked?"

> "What has not worked?"

> "What is our next priority?"

> "Who is doing what?"

This takes only a few seconds but can restore a shared mental model.

---

# 28.22 Escalation

The trauma team leader should recognise when the problem exceeds the team's current capability.

Escalation may involve:

- senior surgical support
- anaesthetic support
- interventional radiology
- operating theatre
- blood bank
- intensive care
- specialist teams
- transfer to a higher-level trauma centre

Escalation is not a sign of failure.

Failure occurs when help is required but is not requested.

---

# 28.23 Safety-Critical Information

Certain information should be communicated explicitly.

Examples include:

- uncontrolled haemorrhage
- difficult airway
- failed airway attempts
- suspected spinal injury
- pelvic injury
- ongoing shock
- massive transfusion requirement
- suspected major vascular injury
- deterioration during transfer
- change in operative plan

The leader should ensure that these issues are not lost among routine conversation.

---

# 28.24 Managing Distraction

Trauma environments contain multiple potential distractions:

- telephones
- additional staff
- relatives
- imaging requests
- laboratory results
- equipment alarms
- multiple simultaneous conversations

The leader should protect the team's attention during critical moments.

Useful phrases include:

> "One conversation."

> "Let's finish this critical task first."

> "Hold non-urgent questions."

> "I need everyone's attention for the next step."

This is not about controlling every conversation.

It is about protecting critical cognitive bandwidth.

---

# 28.25 Human Factors Under Stress

Stress can produce:

- narrowed attention
- impaired working memory
- reduced flexibility of thinking
- poorer communication
- increased impulsivity
- fixation on a single problem

The leader should therefore simplify communication during periods of high stress.

Use:

- short instructions
- names
- explicit priorities
- confirmation
- repetition of critical information

Avoid:

- long explanations during immediate danger
- ambiguous instructions
- multiple simultaneous commands
- unnecessary technical discussion

---

# 28.26 Fatigue

Fatigue can impair:

- attention
- reaction time
- working memory
- judgement
- communication
- error detection

The trauma team leader should recognise fatigue as a safety issue.

Where possible:

- rotate demanding tasks
- ensure appropriate staffing
- use checklists
- encourage cross-checking
- avoid unnecessary multitasking
- obtain senior assistance when performance is compromised

---

# 28.27 Team Cross-Checking

Cross-checking is a powerful safety strategy.

For high-risk decisions, another clinician can independently verify:

- drug
- dose
- procedure
- patient identity
- blood product
- imaging interpretation
- destination

This should not become a ritual.

It should be focused on tasks where an error would have significant consequences.

---

# 28.28 Independent Challenge

In some situations, the leader should deliberately ask another clinician for an independent assessment.

For example:

> "Before we proceed, what do you think is causing the shock?"

This can reveal an alternative interpretation.

Independent thinking is particularly useful when:

- the diagnosis is uncertain
- the patient is deteriorating unexpectedly
- the team is divided
- the intervention has failed
- the leader feels strongly anchored to one explanation

---

# 28.29 Error During a Procedure

If an error occurs during a procedure, the leader should avoid concealment.

The immediate sequence is:

**Recognise → Stop → Stabilise → Correct → Reassess → Communicate → Escalate if required**

The exact response depends on the clinical situation.

The leader should ensure that the team knows:

- what happened
- whether the patient was affected
- what has been done to correct it
- what additional monitoring is required

---

# 28.30 Safety During Transfer

Patient transfer is a high-risk period.

Before transfer, confirm:

- airway
- oxygen supply
- monitoring
- vascular access
- haemorrhage control
- analgesia
- infusions
- blood products
- equipment
- destination
- receiving team
- communication of outstanding problems

A patient who is stable in the resuscitation room may deteriorate during transfer.

The leader should anticipate this.

---

# 28.31 Safety During Handover

Handover should include:

### Situation

What is happening now?

### Background

What happened and what injuries are known or suspected?

### Assessment

What are the current physiological and clinical findings?

### Actions

What has been done?

### Response

How has the patient responded?

### Risks

What remains dangerous?

### Plan

What needs to happen next?

A safe handover should clearly identify unfinished tasks.

---

# 28.32 Never Hand Over an Unnamed Problem

Avoid statements such as:

> "Someone needs to keep an eye on the blood pressure."

Instead:

> "Dr Ahmed, please continue monitoring the blood pressure every five minutes and call me if systolic pressure falls below the agreed threshold."

Specific responsibility reduces omission.

---

# 28.33 Incident Reporting

Significant errors, near misses, and safety events may require formal reporting according to local policy.

The purpose of incident reporting should be learning and system improvement.

Examples include:

- wrong medication
- delayed critical intervention
- equipment failure
- blood product error
- communication failure
- identification error
- transfer-related incident
- near miss that could have caused significant harm

The trauma team leader should know the local reporting process.

---

# 28.34 Near Misses Matter

A near miss is an event that could have caused harm but did not.

Near misses are valuable safety information.

For example:

A wrong medication is prepared but identified before administration.

The patient is not harmed.

However, the system has revealed a vulnerability.

The correct question is not:

> "Did the patient come to harm?"

It is:

> "Why did the error get this far?"

---

# 28.35 Learning From Error

After the immediate clinical problem is resolved, the team should consider:

1. What happened?
2. What was expected to happen?
3. What actually happened?
4. Why was the difference not recognised earlier?
5. What prevented greater harm?
6. What factors contributed?
7. What could be changed?
8. Who needs to know?
9. Does the system require modification?

This approach shifts the focus from blame to learning.

---

# 28.36 Psychological Safety

A team member is more likely to speak up when they believe that doing so will be respected.

The leader should therefore respond positively to concerns.

Instead of:

> "I know what I'm doing."

Use:

> "Thank you. Let's check it."

Instead of:

> "Don't interrupt."

Use:

> "Give me the concern briefly."

This maintains authority while protecting psychological safety.

---

# 28.37 The Leader's Response to Being Corrected

A leader will sometimes be wrong.

When corrected, the safest response is:

> "You're right. Let's change the plan."

This demonstrates that patient safety is more important than personal authority.

A leader who admits an error early can prevent the team from following an incorrect plan simply because the leader initially proposed it.

---

# 28.38 Avoiding Blame During Resuscitation

Blame consumes cognitive bandwidth.

During active resuscitation:

> **Solve the problem first. Analyse responsibility later.**

The leader should maintain professional behaviour even when an error is serious.

There may be a time for formal review.

There is rarely a benefit to humiliating a team member in front of the patient and team.

---

# 28.39 Safety as a Continuous Process

Safety is not a single checklist item.

It occurs throughout the resuscitation.

The leader should repeatedly ask:

- What could go wrong now?
- What are we most worried about?
- What has changed?
- What have we assumed?
- What has not responded as expected?
- What have we not checked?
- Who needs to know?
- What is the next major risk?

This creates continuous error detection.

---

# 28.40 A Practical Safety Loop

A useful model for the trauma leader is:

> **Observe → Recognise → Verify → Act → Reassess → Communicate → Escalate → Learn**

### Observe

Monitor the patient, team, equipment, and environment.

### Recognise

Identify deterioration, discrepancy, omission, or error.

### Verify

Confirm what has actually happened.

### Act

Correct the immediate problem.

### Reassess

Determine whether the intervention worked.

### Communicate

Ensure the team shares the updated mental model.

### Escalate

Obtain additional support when required.

### Learn

Review the event and improve the system.

---

# 28.41 The Safety Pause

Before a major transition, the leader should consider a brief safety pause.

Examples:

- before transfer to CT
- before transfer to theatre
- before leaving the resuscitation room
- before changing the primary treatment strategy
- after unexpected deterioration

A safety pause may include:

> "Current diagnosis?"

> "Current physiological status?"

> "What has been done?"

> "What remains uncontrolled?"

> "What is our immediate priority?"

> "What could go wrong during the next step?"

This takes little time and can prevent major errors.

---

# 28.42 The Five-Question Error Check

When the situation becomes uncertain, ask:

1. **What changed?**
2. **What did we expect to happen?**
3. **What actually happened?**
4. **What could explain the difference?**
5. **What is the safest next action?**

This framework is particularly useful when the patient deteriorates unexpectedly.

---

# 28.43 The Three-Horizon Safety Model

The trauma leader should think simultaneously about three time horizons.

### Immediate

What could harm the patient in the next few minutes?

### Near-term

What is likely to become a problem during the next phase of care?

### Later

What complication or management issue must be communicated to the receiving team?

This prevents the team from becoming trapped entirely in the immediate problem.

---

# 28.44 Safety and Leadership

Safety leadership requires a balance between:

- decisiveness
- openness
- vigilance
- humility
- authority
- flexibility

The leader must be decisive enough to act.

But open enough to change the plan.

The leader must maintain authority.

But allow challenge.

The leader must maintain speed.

But prevent avoidable errors.

The leader must remain calm.

But never become complacent.

---

# 28.45 When the Leader Suspects an Error

If the leader suspects that something may have gone wrong, do not ignore the concern simply because the consequences are uncertain.

Use:

> "I have a concern. Let's check."

This is often safer than waiting for certainty.

The cost of a brief verification is usually small.

The cost of an unrecognised critical error may be enormous.

---

# 28.46 Safety Culture

A mature trauma team develops a culture in which:

- concerns are welcomed
- critical information is repeated
- errors are identified early
- near misses are discussed
- responsibility is clear
- escalation is encouraged
- learning follows adverse events
- staff are treated with respect
- patient safety remains the central objective

Culture is created through repeated behaviour.

The leader demonstrates the culture through actions, not statements.

---

# 28.47 Practical Examples

### Example 1: Unexpected deterioration

A patient initially improves after resuscitation but then becomes hypotensive again.

Poor response:

> "Give more fluid."

Better response:

> "The patient has deteriorated again. What changed? Has bleeding increased? What has not been controlled?"

The leader reassesses the entire situation rather than automatically repeating the previous intervention.

---

### Example 2: Medication discrepancy

A clinician prepares a medication but another team member notices that the concentration appears incorrect.

The leader should respond:

> "Stop. Let's verify the medication and concentration before administration."

The concern should be treated as a safety intervention.

---

### Example 3: Conflicting opinions

One clinician believes the patient requires immediate theatre.

Another believes further imaging is required.

The leader should clarify:

> "Let's define the immediate threat, what information we still need, and whether obtaining that information is safe."

The objective is not to determine who is right.

The objective is to make the safest decision for the patient.

---

### Example 4: Equipment failure

The patient's oxygen saturation suddenly falls.

Before assuming clinical deterioration, the team checks:

- oxygen supply
- probe position
- tubing
- airway
- ventilation
- patient condition

The leader avoids anchoring on a single explanation.

---

# 28.48 A Leader's Error-Management Checklist

### Recognition

- [ ] Has the patient changed?
- [ ] Has the expected response occurred?
- [ ] Is there a discrepancy?
- [ ] Could an error have occurred?

### Immediate safety

- [ ] Is the patient currently at risk?
- [ ] Can further harm be stopped?
- [ ] Does treatment need to change?

### Verification

- [ ] What actually happened?
- [ ] What was expected?
- [ ] Is the information reliable?

### Correction

- [ ] Has the error been corrected?
- [ ] Has the patient been reassessed?
- [ ] Has the team been informed?

### Escalation

- [ ] Does senior help need to be called?
- [ ] Does the patient require theatre, intervention, ICU, or transfer?

### Learning

- [ ] Does the event require documentation?
- [ ] Does it require incident reporting?
- [ ] Is there a system issue that needs addressing?

---

# 28.49 Key Principles

1. **Patient safety comes before blame.**
2. **Errors should be detected as early as possible.**
3. **Near misses are valuable learning opportunities.**
4. **Not every error is caused by individual negligence.**
5. **Systems can make errors more or less likely.**
6. **Closed-loop communication reduces preventable omissions.**
7. **The leader should actively invite challenge.**
8. **Cognitive bias can be reduced by deliberate questioning.**
9. **Every major intervention should be followed by reassessment.**
10. **Unexpected deterioration requires reassessment, not automatic repetition.**
11. **Critical tasks should have clear ownership.**
12. **Escalation should occur early when capability is exceeded.**
13. **A leader should be willing to change their mind.**
14. **Calm correction is safer than public blame.**
15. **Safety is a continuous process throughout resuscitation.**

---

# 28.50 Final Perspective

The safest trauma team is not the team that never makes mistakes.

It is the team that is capable of detecting mistakes early, speaking openly about concerns, correcting problems rapidly, and learning from what happens.

The trauma team leader creates the conditions in which this is possible.

A strong leader therefore asks continuously:

> **What could harm this patient next?**

> **How would we know?**

> **Who is watching for it?**

> **What will we do if it happens?**

The ultimate goal is not perfection.

It is **resilient performance**.

A resilient trauma team can recognise uncertainty, adapt to changing conditions, recover from error, and continue to provide coordinated care under pressure.

The leader's role is to make that resilience possible.

---
# 28.51 Clinical Governance and Accountability

Clinical governance is not a separate administrative activity that occurs after the trauma call.

It is part of clinical leadership.

Every trauma resuscitation is an opportunity to demonstrate whether the organisation can provide safe, effective, timely and accountable care under pressure.

The trauma team leader is therefore not responsible only for what happens during the resuscitation.

The leader is also responsible for recognising when care is becoming unsafe, escalating concerns, ensuring important decisions are communicated, maintaining an appropriate standard of documentation, and helping the team learn from significant events.

A technically successful resuscitation can still contain important governance failures.

Conversely, a difficult case with an adverse outcome may represent high-quality care if the team recognised deterioration, acted appropriately, communicated clearly, escalated early and learned from the event.

Clinical governance is therefore not synonymous with avoiding bad outcomes.

It is the system through which teams continuously improve the quality and safety of care.

---

The trauma team leader should think about governance during the resuscitation rather than after it.

The leader should continuously ask:

- Is the patient receiving appropriate care?
- Are important decisions being made by the appropriate clinician?
- Are concerns being escalated early enough?
- Are we working within our competence?
- Are we following agreed pathways where appropriate?
- Are deviations from normal practice justified?
- Is the team communicating critical information effectively?
- Is the patient's response being reassessed?
- Is there a clear plan for definitive care?
- Will another clinician understand what happened when they review the record later?

These questions transform governance from an administrative concept into a practical component of clinical leadership.

---

# 28.52 The Three Dimensions of Accountability

During major trauma, accountability operates at several levels.

### Individual accountability

Each clinician remains responsible for the actions they personally undertake.

This includes:

- recognising deterioration;
- performing tasks competently;
- identifying limitations;
- seeking assistance;
- communicating important findings;
- documenting significant decisions;
- escalating concerns.

Being part of a trauma team does not remove individual professional responsibility.

---

### Team accountability

The team leader has responsibility for coordinating the overall process.

This includes:

- establishing priorities;
- assigning roles;
- coordinating interventions;
- monitoring progress;
- ensuring reassessment;
- escalating when required;
- ensuring appropriate transfer or definitive management.

The leader should create a team environment in which clinicians can raise concerns.

---

### System accountability

Some failures arise from the environment rather than from an individual clinician.

Examples include:

- inadequate staffing;
- unavailable equipment;
- delayed blood products;
- unclear referral pathways;
- communication failures between departments;
- insufficient training;
- inadequate access to specialist support;
- delays in theatre, interventional radiology or critical care.

A mature governance system distinguishes individual performance problems from system weaknesses.

The trauma leader should recognise both.

---

# 28.53 Accountability Does Not Mean Blame

One of the most important principles of clinical governance is the distinction between accountability and blame.

A clinician may be accountable for an action without being blameworthy.

Similarly, an adverse outcome does not automatically indicate negligence or poor performance.

Trauma care is characterised by:

- incomplete information;
- time pressure;
- rapidly changing physiology;
- competing priorities;
- uncertain diagnoses;
- limited resources;
- complex team interactions.

The appropriate question after a difficult case is not simply:

> "Who made the mistake?"

A better sequence is:

1. What happened?
2. What was known at the time?
3. What decisions were made?
4. Why did those decisions appear reasonable at the time?
5. What went well?
6. What did not go well?
7. What contributed to the problem?
8. Could the problem reasonably have been prevented?
9. What should change as a result?

This approach supports learning rather than concealment.

---

# 28.54 The Trauma Leader as a Governance Role Model

The behaviour of the team leader strongly influences team culture.

A leader who ignores concerns teaches the team to remain silent.

A leader who welcomes challenge teaches the team to speak up.

A leader who hides uncertainty teaches the team to conceal uncertainty.

A leader who openly acknowledges uncertainty teaches the team to manage it safely.

A leader who becomes defensive after an error may create fear.

A leader who responds constructively can turn the same event into a learning opportunity.

The leader should therefore model:

- professionalism;
- transparency;
- respectful communication;
- appropriate escalation;
- situational awareness;
- willingness to reconsider decisions;
- acknowledgement of uncertainty;
- ownership of mistakes;
- commitment to improvement.

---

# 28.55 Speaking Up and Psychological Safety

A safe trauma team must allow clinicians to raise concerns.

Any member of the team may recognise something that the leader has missed.

This is particularly important because the leader may be focused on:

- overall priorities;
- haemorrhage control;
- airway decisions;
- operative planning;
- coordination with specialists;
- transfer arrangements.

Other team members may have better access to specific information.

For example:

The anaesthetist may recognise deteriorating ventilation.

The nurse may notice an unexpected change in vital signs.

The radiographer may identify a concerning imaging finding.

The junior doctor may notice that a medication has not been administered.

The blood bank may identify a problem with product availability.

The team leader should therefore actively invite concerns.

Useful phrases include:

> "If anyone sees a problem, call it out."

> "Tell me if you think our priority needs to change."

> "What am I missing?"

> "Does anyone have a concern about this plan?"

These statements create permission for challenge.

---

# 28.56 Constructive Challenge

Challenge should be directed at the clinical problem rather than at the person.

Poor:

> "You are doing this wrong."

Better:

> "I'm concerned that the patient's blood pressure is continuing to fall despite the current strategy."

Better still:

> "The patient remains unstable. I think we need to reconsider the current plan."

The goal is not to establish who is right.

The goal is to identify the safest course of action.

The team leader should respond to challenge with curiosity rather than defensiveness.

---

# 28.57 When the Leader Is Wrong

Every leader will eventually make an incorrect assessment.

The critical issue is how quickly the error is recognised and corrected.

A dangerous leadership pattern is:

1. make an early decision;
2. become psychologically committed to it;
3. reinterpret new information to support it;
4. ignore contradictory evidence;
5. continue despite deterioration.

A safer pattern is:

1. make the best decision available with current information;
2. state the assumptions behind it;
3. reassess the patient;
4. look for evidence that supports or contradicts the plan;
5. modify the plan when necessary.

A leader should be able to say:

> "The patient is not responding as expected. We need to change direction."

Changing a plan is not weakness.

Persisting with an unsafe plan because it was previously announced is weakness.

---

# 28.58 Clinical Documentation as a Governance Tool

Documentation is not merely a legal record.

It is also a communication and patient-safety tool.

A good trauma record should allow another clinician to reconstruct:

- what happened;
- the patient's condition;
- the important findings;
- major interventions;
- response to treatment;
- significant decisions;
- who was involved;
- why major decisions were made;
- what the next plan was.

Documentation becomes particularly important when:

- the patient is transferred;
- the team changes;
- the patient deteriorates later;
- a procedure is performed;
- a major decision is challenged;
- a complication occurs;
- the case is reviewed.

---

# 28.59 What Should Be Documented?

Documentation requirements vary by institution, but major trauma records should generally capture clinically significant events.

Important elements include:

### Initial condition

Record relevant:

- mechanism;
- time of injury when known;
- initial physiological state;
- major injuries suspected;
- important examination findings.

### Major interventions

Document significant interventions such as:

- airway management;
- ventilation;
- chest decompression;
- haemorrhage control;
- blood product administration;
- major procedures;
- analgesia;
- imaging;
- transfer.

### Response

Record important physiological or clinical responses.

Examples:

- blood pressure improved;
- oxygenation improved;
- persistent shock;
- ongoing external bleeding;
- worsening respiratory distress;
- neurological deterioration.

### Major decisions

Important decisions should be identifiable.

Examples:

- decision to intubate;
- decision to activate massive transfusion;
- decision for emergency surgery;
- decision to transfer;
- decision to stop a particular intervention;
- decision to change the treatment strategy.

---

# 28.60 Documenting Clinical Reasoning

A record that states only:

> "Intubated."

may be insufficient to communicate the clinical reasoning.

Where appropriate, documentation should make clear:

- what prompted the intervention;
- who made the decision;
- what was done;
- what happened afterwards.

Similarly:

> "CT requested."

provides less information than:

> "Given haemodynamic stability after initial resuscitation and concern for intra-abdominal injury, CT trauma imaging requested."

The objective is not to create unnecessary documentation.

The objective is to record information that matters for patient care and subsequent review.

---

# 28.61 The Importance of Time

Trauma care is time-sensitive.

Important events should be documented with accurate times where feasible.

Examples include:

- arrival;
- recognition of deterioration;
- administration of blood products;
- procedures;
- imaging;
- consultant review;
- activation of major protocols;
- transfer.

Accurate chronology can be extremely valuable when reviewing a complex resuscitation.

It can reveal:

- delays;
- parallel processes;
- repeated reassessments;
- treatment-response relationships;
- communication gaps.

---

# 28.62 Verbal Decisions Must Become Shared Decisions

A trauma leader may make numerous decisions verbally.

However, important decisions should not remain isolated inside the leader's mind.

The team should know:

- what the decision is;
- why it has been made;
- what is expected next;
- what would trigger a change.

For example:

> "The patient remains unstable despite blood products. We are treating this as uncontrolled haemorrhage and moving toward definitive haemorrhage control."

This creates a shared mental model.

---

# 28.63 Escalation Is a Governance Skill

Failure to escalate is a common source of risk.

Escalation should occur when:

- the patient's condition exceeds the team's capability;
- specialist expertise is required;
- the diagnosis remains uncertain and the consequences are significant;
- the patient is deteriorating;
- resources are insufficient;
- transfer is required;
- a major intervention may be needed.

Escalation is not an admission of failure.

It is recognition of the limits of the current system.

---

# 28.64 Escalation Should Be Early

A useful principle is:

> Escalate before the situation becomes impossible.

Early escalation creates options.

Late escalation creates emergencies.

For example, contacting:

- the trauma surgeon;
- anaesthesia;
- orthopaedics;
- neurosurgery;
- interventional radiology;
- intensive care;
- blood bank;
- retrieval services;

may be appropriate before the patient reaches a point of physiological collapse.

The exact escalation pathway depends on the local system.

---

# 28.65 Knowing the Limits of the System

Clinical governance requires awareness of organisational capability.

The trauma leader should know:

- what specialist services are immediately available;
- what services require referral;
- whether emergency surgery is available;
- whether interventional radiology is available;
- critical care capacity;
- blood product availability;
- transfer pathways;
- retrieval arrangements;
- imaging capability;
- out-of-hours limitations.

A treatment plan that depends on an unavailable resource is not a safe plan.

---

# 28.66 Deviating From Protocol

Protocols are designed to support safe care.

They are not substitutes for clinical judgement.

Trauma frequently produces circumstances in which the patient's condition does not fit the standard pathway.

A deviation may therefore be appropriate when:

- the patient has an unusual presentation;
- the standard pathway is unavailable;
- the patient is deteriorating;
- multiple injuries create competing priorities;
- specialist advice changes the plan;
- local circumstances require adaptation.

When deviating from a protocol, the leader should be able to explain:

1. What the usual pathway would be.
2. Why it was not appropriate in this situation.
3. What alternative strategy was chosen.
4. Why the alternative was considered safer.

---

# 28.67 Protocols Must Not Replace Reassessment

A protocol may recommend a sequence of actions.

But the patient's physiology determines whether the strategy is working.

The team must continue to reassess.

If the patient deteriorates despite following the protocol, the leader should not simply continue because:

> "That is what the protocol says."

Instead:

> "The patient is not responding. What does this tell us?"

The protocol provides structure.

Clinical reassessment determines direction.

---

# 28.68 Incident Recognition

A significant incident may involve:

- medication error;
- equipment failure;
- delayed treatment;
- communication failure;
- incorrect patient identification;
- procedural complication;
- delayed escalation;
- blood product error;
- failure of monitoring;
- inappropriate transfer;
- unexpected deterioration associated with a system problem.

The leader should recognise when an event may require formal reporting.

Local reporting procedures should be followed.

---

# 28.69 Immediate Response to an Incident

The first priority is always the patient.

When an incident occurs:

### Step 1 — Stabilise the patient

Correct the immediate clinical problem.

### Step 2 — Protect others

Prevent the same error from affecting another patient.

### Step 3 — Inform appropriate senior staff

Escalate according to local policy.

### Step 4 — Preserve relevant information

Ensure important documentation and records are maintained.

### Step 5 — Report through the appropriate system

Follow local incident-reporting procedures.

### Step 6 — Support the team

Recognise that clinicians involved in adverse events may also require support.

---

# 28.70 Do Not Conceal Errors

An error that is concealed cannot be reliably learned from.

Concealment may also allow the same system weakness to harm another patient.

A professional response is:

- recognise;
- stabilise;
- disclose appropriately;
- document accurately;
- escalate;
- report;
- learn.

The exact requirements for disclosure and reporting depend on local policy and professional regulation.

---

# 28.71 Human Factors in Error

Errors rarely occur because of one isolated factor.

They may result from interactions between:

- workload;
- fatigue;
- interruptions;
- unclear communication;
- equipment;
- environment;
- staffing;
- hierarchy;
- cognitive overload;
- poor system design;
- inadequate training.

The team leader should therefore resist simplistic explanations.

Instead of:

> "The clinician made a mistake."

ask:

> "What conditions made this mistake more likely?"

This question is more useful for prevention.

---

# 28.72 Cognitive Error

Trauma leadership is vulnerable to several cognitive traps.

Examples include:

### Anchoring

Becoming fixed on the initial diagnosis.

### Premature closure

Stopping the diagnostic process too early.

### Confirmation bias

Looking primarily for evidence that supports the existing diagnosis.

### Availability bias

Overestimating a diagnosis because it was recently encountered.

### Framing effect

Allowing the way information is presented to influence interpretation.

### Action bias

Feeling compelled to intervene even when the intervention may not help.

### Omission bias

Avoiding an intervention because the clinician fears causing harm.

The leader should recognise these patterns in themselves and the team.

---

# 28.73 Countermeasures Against Cognitive Error

Useful countermeasures include:

- deliberate reassessment;
- structured primary and secondary surveys;
- verbalising uncertainty;
- asking for alternative diagnoses;
- inviting challenge;
- reviewing response to treatment;
- using checklists where appropriate;
- involving senior expertise early;
- pausing before irreversible decisions when time permits.

A useful question is:

> "What else could explain this?"

Another is:

> "What would I expect to see if our current diagnosis were wrong?"

---

# 28.74 Safety Pauses

A safety pause can be useful before a major transition or irreversible intervention.

Examples:

- before emergency transfer;
- before leaving the resuscitation area;
- before major surgery;
- before induction of anaesthesia;
- before transferring a critically ill patient;
- when the diagnosis or plan has changed significantly.

A safety pause does not need to be lengthy.

It should establish:

- current problem;
- current physiology;
- immediate priorities;
- destination;
- responsible clinicians;
- outstanding risks.

---

# 28.75 Handover as a Governance Event

Handover is a high-risk transition.

Information can be lost when responsibility moves between teams.

The trauma leader should ensure that the receiving team understands:

- mechanism;
- injuries;
- physiological trajectory;
- major interventions;
- blood products;
- procedures;
- imaging;
- response;
- unresolved problems;
- current priorities;
- anticipated deterioration;
- outstanding investigations;
- definitive plan.

The handover should be structured and interactive.

The receiving clinician should have an opportunity to ask questions.

---

# 28.76 Closed-Loop Handover

A useful handover structure is:

> "This is what happened."

> "This is what we found."

> "This is what we did."

> "This is how the patient responded."

> "These are the remaining concerns."

> "This is what needs to happen next."

The receiving clinician should confirm understanding of critical issues.

This reduces the risk that responsibility is transferred without shared understanding.

---

# 28.77 Transfer of Responsibility

The leader should explicitly identify when responsibility changes.

For example:

> "Dr Ahmed is now leading the resuscitation."

or:

> "The intensive care team is now responsible for ongoing management."

The transfer should be clear rather than assumed.

Ambiguity about leadership can create dangerous gaps.

---

# 28.78 Continuity of Care

Trauma care often crosses multiple clinical environments:

- ambulance;
- emergency department;
- operating theatre;
- interventional radiology;
- intensive care;
- ward;
- specialist centre;
- rehabilitation.

Each transition introduces risk.

The leader should think beyond the current location.

The question is not:

> "Have we finished our part?"

The question is:

> "What does the patient need next, and have we transferred that information safely?"

---

# 28.79 Clinical Governance and Mortality Review

Major trauma deaths may undergo formal review.

The purpose should be to understand:

- what happened;
- whether care met expected standards;
- what contributed to the outcome;
- whether avoidable factors existed;
- whether system changes are required.

A mortality review should not be reduced to:

> "The patient died."

The clinically useful question is:

> "What can this case teach us about future care?"

---

# 28.80 Avoidability Is Not Binary

A death or complication may contain:

- clearly unavoidable elements;
- potentially modifiable elements;
- system weaknesses;
- areas of uncertainty.

The purpose of review is not necessarily to label the entire case as:

> "avoidable"

or

> "unavoidable."

Instead, identify specific opportunities for improvement.

For example:

- earlier activation of the trauma team;
- faster blood availability;
- clearer escalation;
- improved equipment;
- better handover;
- earlier transfer.

Specific learning points are more actionable than broad conclusions.

---

# 28.81 Morbidity and Mortality Learning

A strong case review should identify:

### What went well?

Recognise effective practice.

### What did not go well?

Identify problems without personal blame.

### Why did it happen?

Explore contributing factors.

### What should change?

Define a practical intervention.

### How will we know it changed?

Identify an outcome or audit measure.

Without the final question, learning may remain theoretical.

---

# 28.82 From Learning to Change

A governance system fails when incidents are repeatedly discussed but nothing changes.

For each significant learning point, consider:

**Problem → Intervention → Owner → Deadline → Measurement**

For example:

**Problem:** Delay obtaining emergency blood.

**Intervention:** Review blood-release process.

**Owner:** Emergency department and blood bank leads.

**Deadline:** Agreed locally.

**Measurement:** Time from activation to first unit.

This converts discussion into quality improvement.

---

# 28.83 Audit and Quality Improvement

Audit asks whether actual practice meets an agreed standard.

Examples of trauma audit measures may include:

- time to trauma team activation;
- time to CT;
- time to blood product administration;
- documentation quality;
- time to definitive haemorrhage control;
- time to specialist review;
- compliance with agreed pathways.

The exact metrics should reflect local priorities.

Not everything needs to be measured.

Measurement should support improvement rather than create unnecessary administrative burden.

---

# 28.84 The Danger of Meaningless Metrics

A metric is useful only if it helps answer a meaningful question.

For example:

> "How many trauma calls occurred?"

may describe activity.

It does not necessarily describe quality.

A more useful question might be:

> "How often was the major haemorrhage pathway activated within the expected timeframe when predefined criteria were present?"

Good governance measures what matters.

---

# 28.85 Training and Competence

Clinical governance includes maintaining competence.

Trauma team members require:

- knowledge;
- technical skills;
- communication skills;
- teamwork;
- decision-making;
- situational awareness;
- familiarity with local systems.

Training should not focus only on procedures.

The team should also rehearse:

- leadership;
- escalation;
- communication;
- role allocation;
- equipment problems;
- unexpected deterioration;
- transfer;
- major haemorrhage;
- difficult airway;
- cardiac arrest.

---

# 28.86 Simulation as a Governance Tool

Simulation can reveal weaknesses that routine clinical care may hide.

A simulation may expose:

- unclear leadership;
- poor role allocation;
- inaccessible equipment;
- communication problems;
- delays in escalation;
- unfamiliarity with protocols;
- poor handover.

The purpose of simulation is not simply to test individuals.

It can test the system.

After simulation, ask:

> "What did the environment make difficult?"

and:

> "What should we change before the next real patient?"

---

# 28.87 Equipment Governance

A trauma team leader should not assume that equipment will always be available and functional.

Important equipment should be:

- accessible;
- checked;
- maintained;
- familiar to staff;
- appropriately stocked.

Examples include:

- airway equipment;
- suction;
- oxygen;
- chest decompression equipment;
- haemorrhage control equipment;
- blood warming systems;
- ultrasound;
- monitoring equipment;
- transfer equipment.

Equipment failure during a resuscitation should trigger both immediate problem-solving and, where appropriate, governance review.

---

# 28.88 The Environment Is Part of the System

The physical environment affects performance.

Potential hazards include:

- poor lighting;
- excessive noise;
- crowding;
- blocked access;
- unclear equipment storage;
- inadequate space;
- interruptions;
- poor visibility of monitors.

A leader who repeatedly encounters the same environmental problem should consider whether it requires system-level action.

---

# 28.89 Staffing and Workload

Understaffing increases risk.

So does inappropriate allocation of experienced staff.

The leader should recognise when the workload exceeds available capacity.

Possible responses include:

- calling additional staff;
- escalating to senior clinicians;
- reallocating tasks;
- reducing non-essential activity;
- requesting specialist assistance;
- arranging transfer.

The correct response to excessive workload is not simply:

> "Work faster."

It is:

> "Increase capacity or reduce competing demands."

---

# 28.90 Fatigue and Performance

Fatigue can impair:

- attention;
- working memory;
- reaction time;
- decision-making;
- communication;
- emotional regulation.

A governance-focused team recognises fatigue as a system risk rather than simply a personal weakness.

During prolonged resuscitations, appropriate delegation and rotation may reduce performance deterioration.

---

# 28.91 The Second Victim Concept

A serious adverse event can affect clinicians emotionally and professionally.

Clinicians may experience:

- guilt;
- anxiety;
- loss of confidence;
- anger;
- shame;
- fear of disciplinary consequences;
- intrusive recollection of the event.

Supporting staff after significant incidents is part of a healthy safety culture.

Support does not mean avoiding accountability.

Both can exist together.

---

# 28.92 Professionalism After an Error

After an error, the leader should avoid:

- humiliation;
- public blame;
- speculation;
- premature conclusions;
- defensive behaviour;
- hiding information.

Instead:

- protect the patient;
- establish facts;
- support staff;
- escalate appropriately;
- document accurately;
- investigate systematically;
- identify learning;
- implement improvement.

---

# 28.93 Just Culture

A just culture attempts to distinguish between:

- human error;
- at-risk behaviour;
- reckless behaviour;
- system failure.

Human error generally requires:

- support;
- redesign;
- education;
- system improvement.

At-risk behaviour may require:

- coaching;
- clarification of expectations;
- removal of incentives for unsafe practice.

Reckless behaviour may require:

- formal investigation;
- appropriate professional action.

The purpose is to respond proportionately.

---

# 28.94 Governance During High-Pressure Resuscitation

Governance principles still apply during extreme emergencies.

The leader should maintain:

- clear leadership;
- role clarity;
- communication;
- reassessment;
- escalation;
- documentation;
- appropriate prioritisation.

However, governance should never become an excuse to delay life-saving treatment.

In a time-critical emergency:

> Treat the patient first.

Then document, communicate and review appropriately.

---

# 28.95 Balancing Speed and Safety

Trauma care frequently involves a tension between:

**speed**

and

**safety.**

The solution is not to choose one universally.

The leader must identify where speed is essential and where a brief safety pause prevents a larger error.

Examples:

- delaying decompression of a tension pneumothorax may be dangerous;
- spending several minutes completing unnecessary documentation during active exsanguination may be inappropriate;
- confirming patient identity before a non-immediate procedure may be essential;
- confirming blood product compatibility according to emergency protocols remains important.

Good leadership recognises the difference.

---

# 28.96 The Governance Mindset

A useful mental model is:

> **Safe care → Reliable systems → Learning → Improvement**

The trauma leader contributes to all four.

### Safe care

Do the right thing for the patient now.

### Reliable systems

Ensure the process supports safe performance.

### Learning

Understand what happened.

### Improvement

Change the system where necessary.

---

# 28.97 Governance Questions for the Team Leader

At the end of a major trauma case, the leader should be able to answer:

### Clinical

- Was the patient appropriately assessed?
- Were immediate threats identified?
- Were priorities appropriate?
- Was deterioration recognised?

### Leadership

- Was there clear leadership?
- Were roles appropriate?
- Was workload distributed effectively?

### Communication

- Were critical instructions understood?
- Were concerns encouraged?
- Was handover effective?

### Escalation

- Was senior help requested appropriately?
- Were specialist services involved early enough?

### Safety

- Were there equipment problems?
- Were there medication or identification issues?
- Were there communication failures?

### Governance

- Does the case require incident reporting?
- Does it require formal review?
- Is there a learning point?
- Does a system need to change?

---

# 28.98 The Five-Minute Governance Review

When time permits after a major case, the leader can conduct a brief review.

### What happened?

One-sentence summary.

### What went well?

Identify effective practice.

### What worried us?

Identify safety concerns.

### What needs follow-up?

Identify unresolved clinical or organisational issues.

### What should change?

Identify one or two actionable improvements.

This can be followed by a more formal review when required.

---

# 28.99 The Governance Trigger List

Consider formal escalation or review when there is:

- unexpected death;
- unexpected deterioration;
- major complication;
- significant delay;
- medication error;
- blood product incident;
- equipment failure;
- communication failure;
- unexpected transfer;
- serious diagnostic error;
- failure of escalation;
- repeated deviation from an agreed pathway;
- significant near miss.

Local policy determines the exact reporting requirements.

When uncertain, seek senior advice.

---

# 28.100 Near Misses Matter

A near miss is an event that could have caused harm but did not.

Near misses are valuable because they reveal system weaknesses without requiring patient harm to occur.

Examples:

- wrong medication identified before administration;
- incorrect blood product identified before transfusion;
- incorrect patient identified before procedure;
- missed injury recognised before transfer;
- equipment failure identified before use.

A team should not wait for harm before improving the system.

---

# 28.101 The Leader Should Ask "Why Did We Get Lucky?"

Sometimes a case ends safely despite a significant vulnerability.

For example:

A wrong medication is prepared but noticed at the last moment.

The patient remains stable despite delayed escalation.

A critical piece of equipment fails but another device is immediately available.

The correct response is not:

> "No harm occurred, so nothing happened."

Instead:

> "What prevented harm this time?"

That question may reveal an important safety barrier.

---

# 28.102 Safety Barriers

Patient safety depends on multiple barriers.

Examples include:

- protocols;
- checklists;
- identification systems;
- trained staff;
- supervision;
- monitoring;
- equipment checks;
- communication;
- pharmacy checks;
- blood bank checks.

When several barriers fail simultaneously, harm becomes more likely.

The leader should therefore consider the system rather than a single point of failure.

---

# 28.103 The Swiss Cheese Concept

Major adverse events may occur when several weaknesses align.

For example:

- unclear handover;
- staffing shortage;
- equipment problem;
- communication failure;
- delayed escalation.

Each weakness may be tolerable individually.

Together they can produce serious harm.

The governance response is to strengthen multiple barriers rather than rely on one person to prevent every error.

---

# 28.104 Governance and Team Culture

A high-performing trauma team develops a culture in which:

- concerns are welcomed;
- uncertainty is acceptable;
- escalation is encouraged;
- errors are reported;
- learning is shared;
- standards are clear;
- feedback is constructive;
- responsibility is understood.

Culture is created by repeated behaviour.

The team leader contributes to this culture every time they lead a resuscitation.

---

# 28.105 The Leader's Language Shapes Culture

Compare:

> "Why did you do that?"

with:

> "Talk me through what you saw."

The second question seeks understanding before judgement.

Compare:

> "You should have known better."

with:

> "What made this difficult to recognise?"

The second question may reveal a system problem.

This does not mean avoiding accountability.

It means obtaining accurate information before reaching conclusions.

---

# 28.106 Governance and Leadership Under Uncertainty

Trauma leadership rarely provides complete information.

A responsible leader should be able to say:

> "We do not know yet."

But uncertainty should be followed by action.

For example:

> "We do not yet know the exact source of bleeding. The patient is unstable, so we will continue haemorrhage control and activate the appropriate pathway while we clarify the source."

This is better than pretending certainty.

---

# 28.107 Transparency

Transparency means communicating important information honestly and appropriately.

This includes:

- acknowledging uncertainty;
- acknowledging complications;
- communicating significant changes;
- reporting safety events;
- documenting important decisions.

Transparency supports trust between:

- clinicians;
- patients;
- families;
- departments;
- organisations.

---

# 28.108 The Patient and Family

Governance ultimately exists to improve patient care.

When appropriate, patients and families should receive clear and honest communication about:

- the clinical situation;
- significant changes;
- important complications;
- ongoing management.

The exact requirements for disclosure following adverse events depend on local policy, professional guidance and applicable law.

The trauma leader should ensure that appropriate senior clinicians and institutional processes are involved when significant adverse events occur.

---

# 28.109 Governance Is Not Paperwork

The greatest mistake is to reduce clinical governance to:

- forms;
- reports;
- meetings;
- policies;
- audits.

These are tools.

The objective is safer care.

A useful test is:

> "Did this governance activity make patient care safer?"

If not, its value should be questioned.

---

# 28.110 The Trauma Team Leader's Governance Checklist

Before closing a major trauma episode, consider:

### Patient

- Is the patient physiologically stable enough for the next transition?
- Are major unresolved problems identified?
- Is there a clear definitive plan?

### Team

- Is responsibility clear?
- Has an appropriate handover occurred?
- Are staff aware of outstanding risks?

### Safety

- Were any incidents or near misses identified?
- Were equipment or system failures encountered?
- Were there medication, blood or identification concerns?

### Documentation

- Are major interventions documented?
- Are significant decisions documented?
- Are important times recorded where feasible?
- Is the current plan clear?

### Escalation

- Has the appropriate senior team been informed?
- Are further specialist services required?

### Governance

- Does the case require reporting?
- Does it require formal review?
- Is there an immediate system problem that should be escalated?

---

# 28.111 A Governance Conversation After the Case

A concise post-case discussion might sound like:

> "The patient is now transferred. Before everyone leaves, let's identify anything that could affect safety."

Then:

> "What went well?"

Pause.

> "What caused difficulty?"

Pause.

> "Did we have any near misses or system problems?"

Pause.

> "Is there anything that needs to be reported or escalated?"

Finally:

> "What is the one thing we should change before the next major trauma?"

This takes little time but can generate significant learning.

---

# 28.112 What Good Governance Looks Like

Good governance is visible in behaviour.

The team:

- recognises deterioration;
- escalates early;
- communicates clearly;
- documents important decisions;
- reports significant incidents;
- reviews difficult cases;
- learns from near misses;
- improves systems;
- supports staff;
- maintains professional accountability.

It is not perfection.

It is a commitment to continuously safer care.

---

# 28.113 The Leader's Responsibility After the Resuscitation

The end of the resuscitation is not necessarily the end of leadership.

The leader should consider:

- Does the patient need further escalation?
- Does the receiving team have all critical information?
- Are there unresolved clinical issues?
- Does the case require documentation or reporting?
- Does the team need a debrief?
- Does equipment need replacement?
- Did anything expose a system weakness?
- Is there a learning point that should be shared?

The leader should close the loop.

---

# 28.114 Closing the Governance Loop

A complete governance cycle is:

**Recognise → Stabilise → Communicate → Document → Report → Review → Learn → Change → Reassess**

The cycle should not stop at:

> "We discussed it."

It should reach:

> "We changed something."

And ideally:

> "We measured whether the change worked."

---

# 28.115 Practical Leadership Principle

> **If the system repeatedly relies on individual heroics to remain safe, the system is not yet safe.**

A strong trauma leader should therefore look beyond individual performance.

If a problem occurs repeatedly, ask:

- Is the process flawed?
- Is the equipment inadequate?
- Is staffing insufficient?
- Is training incomplete?
- Is communication unclear?
- Is the escalation pathway unreliable?

Fixing the system is often more powerful than telling individuals to try harder.

---

# 28.116 Final Mental Model

The trauma team leader should hold five governance principles in mind:

### Protect the patient

Immediate clinical safety comes first.

### Make responsibility clear

Everyone should know who is doing what and who is leading.

### Speak up early

Concerns should be raised before they become crises.

### Learn from what happens

Errors, near misses and difficult cases contain information.

### Change the system

Learning has value only when it improves future care.

---

# 28.117 Final Summary

Clinical governance is not something that happens after the trauma team leaves the resuscitation room.

It is embedded in leadership.

The trauma team leader demonstrates governance by:

- recognising unsafe situations;
- maintaining clear accountability;
- encouraging challenge;
- escalating appropriately;
- documenting significant decisions;
- supporting safe handover;
- recognising incidents and near misses;
- participating in review;
- identifying system weaknesses;
- supporting staff;
- translating learning into improvement.

The strongest leader is not the person who appears never to make an error.

It is the person who creates a system in which errors are detected early, concerns can be voiced, deterioration is recognised, responsibility is clear, and the team learns continuously.

> **Good trauma leadership protects the patient today.
> Good clinical governance makes the next patient safer tomorrow.**

# Key Takeaways

1. Error recognition is a patient-safety responsibility, not an admission of incompetence.
2. The first response to an error is to protect the patient.
3. Closed-loop communication, read-back, cross-checking, and deliberate reassessment reduce preventable error.
4. Cognitive bias must be actively countered during high-pressure decision-making.
5. Speaking up and constructive challenge are safety behaviours.
6. Near misses are valuable sources of learning.
7. Incident reporting should support learning and system improvement rather than concealment.
8. Clinical documentation is a governance tool as well as a clinical record.
9. Accountability should clarify responsibility without automatically becoming blame.
10. Governance includes equipment, staffing, workload, environment, training, simulation, and system design.
11. Protocols support safe care but must never replace clinical reassessment.
12. Mortality review and audit are useful only when learning is translated into measurable change.
13. A just culture distinguishes human error, at-risk behaviour, and reckless behaviour.
14. The trauma leader should model transparency, escalation, challenge, and professional behaviour.
15. Good governance closes the loop between what happened, what was learned, and what changes next.

# Chapter Summary

Error management, patient safety, and clinical governance are inseparable components of trauma leadership.

The trauma team leader must simultaneously manage the patient's immediate clinical risk and the conditions that influence team reliability. Errors must be recognised early, the patient protected, relevant information communicated, and appropriate escalation initiated.

The leader must also ensure that significant events, near misses, system weaknesses, and recurring patterns become sources of structured learning.

Clinical governance extends this responsibility beyond the individual resuscitation. Documentation, accountability, incident reporting, audit, mortality review, training, equipment governance, staffing, and system design all influence the safety of future patients.

The objective is therefore not a trauma service in which nobody ever makes an error.

The objective is a system that detects problems early, limits their consequences, learns from them, and becomes progressively more reliable.

> **Safe trauma leadership protects the patient now, learns from what happened, and changes the system for the next patient.**
