# Chapter 6 — The Trauma Team and Team Activation

## Clinical Scenario

A 34-year-old man is brought to the emergency department after a high-speed road traffic collision.

The pre-hospital team reports:

- high-energy mechanism
- suspected chest and abdominal injuries
- GCS 12
- heart rate 128/min
- systolic blood pressure 88 mmHg
- respiratory rate 30/min
- oxygen saturation 91% despite supplemental oxygen
- pelvic pain and deformity of the right lower limb
- estimated time of arrival: 5 minutes

The trauma team is activated.

Within seconds, multiple clinicians enter the resuscitation room.

Everyone is working, but the team must be organised around a clear leadership structure, defined responsibilities, shared situational awareness, effective communication, anticipation, coordination, and escalation pathways.

The Trauma Team Leader does not need to perform every task.

The leader needs to ensure that the right tasks are performed, in the right order, by the right people, at the right time.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the purpose of a multidisciplinary trauma team.
2. Explain why trauma activation is a system rather than a single notification.
3. Distinguish hospital trauma-team activation from field triage.
4. Describe the principles underlying activation decisions.
5. Recognise physiological, anatomical, mechanism-related, and population-specific activation triggers.
6. Understand tiered trauma activation and specialty-specific mobilisation.
7. Prepare the trauma team and resuscitation environment before patient arrival.
8. Anticipate resources and escalation requirements.
9. Recognise when activation should be escalated or de-escalated.
10. Understand that activation decisions should remain responsive to evolving clinical information.

# 1. The Trauma Team as a Clinical System

A trauma team is a multidisciplinary group of healthcare professionals assembled to assess and treat patients with major or potentially life-threatening trauma.

Major trauma frequently involves:

- multiple injured organ systems
- rapidly changing physiology
- simultaneous diagnostic and therapeutic requirements
- competing priorities
- time-critical interventions
- multiple specialist disciplines
- substantial information uncertainty

No single clinician can reliably perform all required functions simultaneously.

The trauma team therefore distributes work and enables parallel processing.

While one clinician manages the airway:

- another assesses circulation
- another establishes vascular access
- another obtains blood samples
- another prepares blood products
- another performs neurological assessment
- another documents events
- the leader integrates the information

The purpose of the team is not simply to increase the number of people in the room.

Its purpose is to increase the team's ability to:

> **recognise threats, intervene rapidly, reassess continuously and coordinate definitive care.**

A high-performing trauma team should therefore be understood as a structured clinical system rather than a collection of individuals.

---

# 2. Why Trauma Team Activation Exists

Major trauma is time-critical.

Some patients require interventions within minutes of arrival, including:

- haemorrhage control
- airway intervention
- assisted ventilation
- chest decompression
- pelvic stabilisation
- blood product administration
- emergency surgery
- interventional radiology
- neurosurgical intervention
- orthopaedic intervention
- paediatric expertise
- obstetric involvement
- burn management

The required personnel may not normally be physically present in the resuscitation room.

Activation creates a mechanism for mobilising them.

The objective is:

> **The right team, with the right resources, in the right place, before the patient arrives.**

Trauma activation should therefore be understood as a **graded mobilisation system**, not simply a binary event.

---

# 3. Activation Is a System, Not a Telephone Call

A trauma activation pathway usually contains several linked processes:

1. Recognition of a potentially severely injured patient.
2. Transmission of a pre-alert.
3. Selection of the activation level.
4. Notification of the trauma team.
5. Preparation of the resuscitation environment.
6. Mobilisation of additional resources.
7. Assignment of leadership and roles.
8. Reception of the patient.
9. Immediate assessment and resuscitation.
10. Reassessment of the initial activation level.
11. Escalation or de-escalation.
12. Documentation and subsequent performance review.

A weak system may have excellent clinicians but still perform poorly because information or mobilisation is delayed.

A strong system reduces unnecessary delay before the patient reaches the resuscitation room.

---

# 4. Trauma Activation Versus Field Triage

These concepts are related but not identical.

## Field Triage

Field triage helps determine:

- whether the patient is likely to have serious injury
- the appropriate destination
- whether transport to a higher-level trauma centre is appropriate

Field triage should not automatically be treated as the hospital trauma-team activation decision.

## Hospital Trauma-Team Activation

Hospital activation answers a different question:

> **What level of hospital response is required for this patient right now?**

The hospital may receive:

- a patient already identified as major trauma
- an unstable patient without a dramatic mechanism
- a patient with isolated but time-critical injury
- a patient whose severity becomes apparent only after arrival

The two systems should communicate with each other, but they should not be treated as interchangeable.

---

# 5. The Principle of Anticipation

The trauma team leader should ask:

> **What might this patient need in the first five minutes?**

Then:

> **What might this patient need in the next thirty minutes?**

For example, a patient with hypotension and suspected pelvic injury may require:

- major haemorrhage pathway
- blood products
- pelvic binder
- trauma surgery
- anaesthesia
- operating theatre
- interventional radiology

A patient with a penetrating neck injury and airway bleeding may require:

- anaesthesia
- airway equipment
- surgical airway equipment
- trauma or ENT surgery
- operating theatre
- blood products

A patient with severe head injury and GCS 6 may require:

- airway team
- anaesthesia
- neurosurgery
- CT
- critical care

The activation decision should therefore be based on **anticipated clinical needs**, not simply on the mechanism of injury.

---

# 6. Activation Criteria

Every trauma system should have locally agreed activation criteria.

These criteria should be:

- clearly defined
- accessible to staff
- regularly reviewed
- based on evidence and local resources
- linked to a defined response
- understandable to prehospital and hospital clinicians

The exact criteria vary between trauma systems.

There is no single universal list that should simply be copied into every hospital.

## Physiological Compromise

Examples include:

- hypotension
- significant tachycardia
- abnormal respiratory rate
- respiratory distress
- hypoxaemia
- altered mental status
- reduced GCS
- signs of shock
- peri-arrest physiology
- requirement for advanced airway or ventilatory support

Physiological deterioration should generally carry greater weight than mechanism alone.

---

# 7. High-Risk Injury Patterns

Certain injury patterns should prompt a high level of concern.

Examples include:

- penetrating injury to the head, neck, torso, or proximal extremity
- suspected major vascular injury
- suspected pelvic fracture with haemodynamic instability
- flail chest
- major chest injury
- suspected traumatic brain injury with reduced consciousness
- spinal injury with neurological deficit
- mangled or pulseless extremity
- traumatic amputation
- uncontrolled external haemorrhage
- major burns associated with trauma
- multiple proximal long-bone fractures

These examples should be interpreted according to local activation policy.

The presence of one feature should not automatically replace clinical judgment.

---

# 8. Mechanism of Injury

Mechanism remains useful.

However:

> **Mechanism is a risk marker, not a diagnosis.**

Examples of concerning mechanisms include:

- high-speed road traffic collision
- occupant ejection
- significant vehicle intrusion
- death in the same passenger compartment
- pedestrian struck by a vehicle
- motorcycle collision with significant impact
- significant fall
- crush injury
- high-energy penetrating trauma
- blast injury

Mechanism should raise suspicion, but it should not be used in isolation to determine severity.

A patient with a dramatic mechanism may have limited injury.

A patient with a seemingly minor mechanism may have catastrophic injury.

---

# 9. Special Populations

Some patients require a lower threshold for escalation because their physiology or injury pattern may be difficult to interpret.

Examples include:

- children
- older adults
- pregnant patients
- patients taking anticoagulants
- patients with significant comorbidity
- patients with communication difficulties
- patients with cognitive impairment
- patients with intoxication
- patients with burns
- patients with limited physiological reserve

The trauma team leader should ask:

> **Could this patient's apparent stability be misleading?**

---

# 10. Tiered Trauma Activation

A useful trauma system may employ different levels of activation.

## Level 1 — Full Trauma Team Activation

Used when there is a high probability of immediate life-threatening injury or need for multiple simultaneous interventions.

Potential team members may include:

- trauma team leader
- emergency physician
- trauma surgeon
- anaesthetist
- airway clinician
- emergency nurses
- radiographer
- healthcare assistants
- blood bank/transfusion support
- operating theatre team
- other specialists according to injury pattern

The exact composition should follow local policy.

## Level 2 — Modified Trauma Activation

Used when serious injury is suspected but a full multidisciplinary response may not initially be necessary.

Possible examples include:

- stable penetrating injury
- isolated serious limb injury
- moderate traumatic brain injury
- suspected spinal injury without physiological instability
- significant burns
- isolated chest injury

The team can then escalate rapidly if the patient deteriorates.

## Specialty-Specific Activation

Some situations require immediate mobilisation of a particular service.

Examples include:

- neurosurgical activation
- vascular surgery
- cardiothoracic surgery
- orthopaedics
- obstetrics
- paediatrics
- burns service
- interventional radiology

The principle is:

> **Activate the resources required by the patient, not simply the resources traditionally associated with the trauma team.**

---

# 11. Preparation Before Patient Arrival

Once activated, the trauma team leader should not simply wait for the patient.

The leader should establish:

### What is coming?

- age
- sex
- mechanism
- number of patients
- estimated time of arrival

### How sick is the patient?

- airway
- breathing
- circulation
- neurological status
- haemorrhage
- interventions already performed

### What is likely to kill the patient first?

Think:

> **What must we be ready to treat immediately?**

### What resources might be required?

- blood
- airway equipment
- chest drains
- pelvic binder
- ultrasound
- radiography
- CT
- operating theatre
- interventional radiology
- specialist teams

### What is the contingency plan?

Ask:

> **If the patient arrives worse than described, what will we do?**

---

# 12. The Pre-Alert

A useful pre-alert should be concise and structured.

One practical format is:

### ATMIST

**A — Age**

**T — Time**

**M — Mechanism**

**I — Injuries suspected**

**S — Signs**

**T — Treatment given**

The receiving team should listen specifically for:

- physiological instability
- airway compromise
- active haemorrhage
- altered consciousness
- interventions already performed
- estimated arrival time

The leader should identify the information that changes the immediate response rather than allowing the pre-alert to become an undifferentiated stream of information.

---

# 13. Five-Minute Preparation

During the final minutes before arrival, the team should prepare the resuscitation environment according to anticipated threats.

## Airway

Check:

- suction
- oxygen
- bag-mask equipment
- airway devices
- intubation equipment
- difficult-airway equipment
- capnography
- surgical airway equipment

## Breathing

Prepare:

- oxygen delivery
- monitoring
- chest decompression equipment
- chest drains
- ultrasound if available

## Circulation

Prepare:

- vascular access equipment
- rapid infuser
- blood warmers
- haemorrhage control equipment
- pelvic binder
- tourniquets
- haemostatic dressings
- blood products according to local protocol

## Disability

Prepare:

- neurological assessment tools
- glucose measurement
- appropriate sedation and airway medications
- temperature monitoring

## Exposure

Prepare:

- trauma shears
- warming equipment
- blankets
- fluid warming
- temperature control

---

# 14. Mobilising Definitive Resources

Activation may need to extend beyond the emergency department.

A patient with suspected uncontrolled haemorrhage may require:

- emergency surgery
- interventional radiology
- hybrid theatre
- damage-control surgery

The trauma team leader should therefore ask early:

> **If resuscitation fails, where is this patient going?**

The answer should not be discovered after prolonged unsuccessful resuscitation.

It should be anticipated.

---

# 15. Escalation and De-escalation

Activation is not necessarily a fixed commitment.

The initial activation level should be reassessed as new information becomes available.

Escalation may be required when:

- the patient's physiology deteriorates
- additional injuries become apparent
- the response to initial treatment is inadequate
- a previously unrecognised specialist requirement emerges
- definitive intervention is likely to be required

De-escalation may be appropriate when:

- the patient's condition is more stable than anticipated
- the anticipated major threat is excluded
- the required response is substantially less than the initial activation level

The key principle is:

> **Activation should remain responsive to the patient's evolving clinical needs.**

---

# 16. Multiple Casualties and Resource Competition

When multiple casualties arrive, the leadership problem changes.

The team leader must consider:

- available resuscitation capacity
- staffing
- blood products
- imaging
- operating theatre capacity
- specialist availability
- competing patient priorities
- escalation pathways

Individual patient leadership must be coordinated with the wider incident response when demand exceeds available resources.

---

# 17. Communication During Activation

Activation depends on reliable communication.

The trauma team leader should ensure that critical information is communicated clearly to the team and supporting services.

Communication should support:

- role allocation
- resource mobilisation
- escalation
- confirmation of critical tasks
- coordination with theatre, radiology, transfusion and specialist teams

Closed-loop communication should be used for important tasks and decisions.

Detailed communication mechanics belong to the dedicated communication chapter; here, they should be applied specifically to activation.

---

# 18. Activation Checklist

Before patient arrival, the leader should consider:

### Patient

- Who is coming?
- How many patients?
- What is the mechanism?
- What injuries are suspected?

### Physiology

- Is the patient unstable?
- Is there airway or respiratory compromise?
- Is major haemorrhage suspected?
- What interventions have already occurred?

### Injury

- What is most likely to kill the patient first?
- What specialist expertise may be required?

### Resources

- Is blood available?
- Is appropriate equipment ready?
- Are imaging and procedural resources available?
- Is definitive treatment capacity available?

### Environment

- Is the resuscitation room ready?
- Are roles clear?
- Is monitoring ready?

### Contingency

- What if the patient arrives worse?
- What is the escalation pathway?
- Where will the patient go if definitive intervention is required?

---

# 19. Case-Based Application

Activation decisions should be tested against realistic scenarios.

## Case 1 — Obvious Full Activation

A patient arrives after a high-energy collision with hypotension, reduced consciousness and suspected major haemorrhage.

The appropriate response is to mobilise the resources required for simultaneous assessment and immediate resuscitation while anticipating definitive haemorrhage control.

## Case 2 — Dramatic Mechanism, Stable Patient

A patient has a high-energy mechanism but remains physiologically stable with no clear evidence of immediately life-threatening injury.

Mechanism alone should not automatically determine the highest activation level.

## Case 3 — Apparently Minor Mechanism

A patient has a seemingly minor mechanism but presents with physiological instability.

The patient's clinical state should drive escalation.

## Case 4 — Stable Penetrating Injury

A patient has a penetrating injury without initial physiological compromise.

A modified or specialty-specific response may be appropriate, with the ability to escalate rapidly.

## Case 5 — Activation That Must Escalate

A patient initially activated at a lower level deteriorates shortly after arrival.

The activation response should escalate according to the evolving clinical threat.

---

# 20. Common Activation Failures

Common failures include:

- waiting for the patient before preparing
- using mechanism alone
- delaying activation until diagnosis is certain
- treating activation as binary
- failing to escalate
- failing to de-escalate
- forgetting supporting services
- unclear responsibility

The corrective principle is consistent:

> **Activation should anticipate clinical need, mobilise appropriate resources, establish clear responsibility, and remain adaptable as the patient's condition evolves.**

---

# 21. Local Ownership and Performance Improvement

The activation system should be locally owned.

Local policy should define:

- activation criteria
- activation levels
- team composition
- notification pathways
- escalation and de-escalation processes
- supporting-service mobilisation
- documentation requirements
- performance review

Performance improvement should examine:

- under-triage
- over-triage
- delays
- communication failures
- resource mobilisation
- escalation failures
- de-escalation failures

The objective is not simply to activate more patients.

The objective is to create a reliable system that provides the appropriate response to the appropriate patient at the appropriate time.

---

# Key Takeaways

- A trauma team is a structured clinical system, not simply a group of clinicians.
- Activation is a graded mobilisation process.
- Hospital activation and field triage are related but distinct.
- Activation should be driven by anticipated clinical need, not mechanism alone.
- Physiological compromise and high-risk injury patterns should influence activation level.
- Special populations may require a lower threshold for escalation.
- Preparation should occur before the patient arrives.
- The leader should anticipate resources and definitive care early.
- Activation should be reassessed as new information becomes available.
- Escalation and de-escalation are both components of effective activation.
- Local policy should define the exact activation system.
