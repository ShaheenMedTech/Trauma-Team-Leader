# Chapter 11 — Airway Management in Major Trauma

## Clinical Scenario

A 28-year-old man is brought to the resuscitation room following a high-speed motorcycle collision.

The pre-hospital team reports a brief loss of consciousness, facial trauma, vomiting, and progressively increasing work of breathing during transport.

On arrival, the patient is agitated and intermittently confused. He is able to speak, but his voice is increasingly wet and difficult to understand.

Initial observations are:

- respiratory rate 30/min;
- oxygen saturation 91% despite supplemental oxygen;
- heart rate 124/min;
- systolic blood pressure 108 mmHg;
- Glasgow Coma Scale 11/15;
- visible blood in the mouth;
- facial swelling.

There is no obvious catastrophic external haemorrhage.

The Trauma Team Leader recognises that the airway is currently patent but potentially unstable.

The immediate challenge is therefore not simply to determine whether the patient has an airway.

It is to determine whether the patient can maintain and protect that airway, whether oxygenation and ventilation can be sustained, and whether deterioration is sufficiently foreseeable to justify early definitive airway management.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Assess the airway rapidly in the context of major trauma.
2. Recognise actual and impending airway compromise.
3. Integrate airway assessment with breathing, circulation, neurological status, and mechanism of injury.
4. Identify situations in which early definitive airway management should be considered.
5. Coordinate preparation for advanced airway management within the trauma team.
6. Understand the importance of oxygenation, ventilation, aspiration risk, and airway protection.
7. Anticipate difficult and failed airway scenarios.
8. Recognise the importance of confirmation and ongoing monitoring after airway intervention.
9. Reassess the airway after major interventions and whenever the patient's condition changes.
10. Apply a Trauma Team Leader framework to airway decision-making and team coordination.

## Key Concepts

### The Airway Is a Dynamic Problem

A patient who has a patent airway on arrival may not remain so.

Airway compromise may evolve because of:

- facial trauma;
- bleeding;
- secretions or vomit;
- swelling;
- burns;
- reduced consciousness;
- expanding neck injury;
- or progressive fatigue.

The leader should therefore assess both the airway that exists now and the airway that is likely to exist several minutes later.

The important question is:

> **"Can this patient maintain and protect the airway safely, and is that likely to remain true?"**

### Airway Assessment Occurs Within the Primary Survey

Airway assessment should not occur in isolation.

The leader should integrate:

**Airway → Oxygenation → Ventilation → Neurological status → Trajectory**

A patient may have an apparently patent airway but still have inadequate oxygenation or ventilation.

Similarly, a patient with a currently adequate airway may require early intervention because deterioration is foreseeable.

### Treat Immediate Threats Without Unnecessary Delay

If airway obstruction is recognised, it should be treated immediately.

Initial measures may include:

- suction;
- removal of visible obstruction when appropriate;
- appropriate airway manoeuvres;
- airway adjuncts where indicated;
- oxygenation;
- assisted ventilation when required;
- and preparation for definitive airway management.

The team should not delay treatment of an immediately life-threatening airway problem simply to complete the remainder of the primary survey.

### Anticipation Is a Core Airway Skill

The difficulty of airway management may increase rapidly after trauma.

Swelling, bleeding, aspiration, reduced consciousness, and anatomical distortion may make a later airway considerably more difficult than an early airway.

The Trauma Team Leader should therefore ask:

> **"If this airway deteriorates, will it become more difficult to manage?"**

If the answer is yes, the team should prepare early and consider whether definitive airway management should occur before further deterioration.

## Recognising Airway Compromise

### Clinical Indicators

The leader should actively look and listen for evidence of airway compromise.

This may include:

- inability to speak normally;
- altered or obstructed voice;
- snoring;
- gurgling;
- stridor;
- visible obstruction;
- blood or vomit in the airway;
- facial or neck swelling;
- abnormal respiratory effort;
- reduced consciousness;
- and progressive deterioration.

The presence of abnormal airway sounds should be treated as clinically important information rather than simply documented as an observation.

### Ability to Speak

A patient who can speak clearly has evidence that air is currently passing through the airway.

However, speech alone does not guarantee future airway stability.

The leader should consider whether the patient's ability to speak is deteriorating and whether the patient can continue to protect the airway.

### Secretions, Blood, and Vomit

Blood and secretions can rapidly compromise the airway, particularly in patients with facial trauma or reduced consciousness.

Suction should therefore be immediately available.

Repeated contamination of the airway should prompt consideration of whether the patient can reliably maintain airway protection.

### Reduced Consciousness

Reduced consciousness may impair:

- airway tone;
- protective reflexes;
- secretion clearance;
- and the ability to maintain airway patency.

Neurological deterioration should therefore trigger immediate reassessment of the airway as well as the broader primary survey.

## Airway Assessment in Facial and Neck Trauma

### Facial Trauma

Facial trauma may cause:

- bleeding;
- swelling;
- distorted anatomy;
- loose teeth or foreign material;
- and progressive airway obstruction.

The apparent severity of the airway problem may therefore increase with time.

The team should anticipate worsening conditions rather than waiting for complete obstruction.

### Neck Trauma

Neck trauma may be associated with injury to structures surrounding the airway.

The leader should maintain a high level of concern when there is:

- expanding neck swelling;
- penetrating neck trauma;
- significant blunt neck trauma;
- airway symptoms;
- subcutaneous emphysema;
- or progressive respiratory deterioration.

The airway should be reassessed frequently when such findings are present.

### Cervical Spine Considerations

Cervical spine protection should be integrated into airway management according to the mechanism, clinical findings, and local trauma protocols.

However, cervical spine precautions should not prevent treatment of a life-threatening airway problem.

The leader must balance spinal protection with the immediate need to maintain oxygenation and a patent airway.

## Initial Airway Management

### Positioning and Airway Manoeuvres

The team should use appropriate positioning and airway manoeuvres according to the patient's condition and suspected injuries.

The objective is to restore and maintain airway patency while preparing for further management.

### Suction

Effective suction is essential when blood, vomit, or secretions threaten airway patency.

The leader should ensure that suction is:

- immediately available;
- functional;
- appropriately connected;
- and supported by an appropriate team member.

### Airway Adjuncts

Airway adjuncts may be used when appropriate to maintain patency.

Their selection should take into account:

- level of consciousness;
- protective reflexes;
- facial trauma;
- and the anticipated need for definitive airway management.

Airway adjuncts should not create false reassurance when the underlying problem is progressive or when airway protection is inadequate.

### Oxygenation and Ventilation

Oxygenation should be optimised while the airway problem is being addressed.

If the patient cannot maintain adequate ventilation, assisted ventilation may be required.

The leader should continuously reassess whether the current strategy is achieving:

- adequate oxygenation;
- adequate ventilation;
- and effective airway protection.

## When to Consider Definitive Airway Management

### The Decision Is Clinical

The decision to secure the airway should be based on the patient's overall condition rather than a single numerical threshold.

Relevant considerations include:

- inability to maintain airway patency;
- inability to protect the airway;
- inadequate oxygenation;
- inadequate ventilation;
- severe or progressive facial or airway injury;
- reduced consciousness with an unsafe airway;
- anticipated deterioration;
- and the likely clinical trajectory.

### Current Stability Does Not Exclude Early Intervention

A patient may be maintaining the airway at the present moment but still require early definitive management.

Examples include patients with:

- progressive facial swelling;
- ongoing airway contamination;
- deteriorating neurological status;
- severe burns or inhalational injury;
- or an anticipated need for prolonged ventilation.

The leader should consider whether waiting will make the airway more difficult or less safe.

### The Risk of Waiting

The decision to delay definitive airway management should take into account the possibility that deterioration may occur in a less controlled environment.

If the airway becomes significantly more difficult after swelling, bleeding, exhaustion, or neurological deterioration, the options available to the team may become more limited.

Anticipation is therefore preferable to rescue whenever deterioration is foreseeable and preventable.

## Preparation for Advanced Airway Management

### Team Preparation

Before advanced airway management, the Trauma Team Leader should ensure that the team understands:

- who is performing the procedure;
- who is assisting;
- who is monitoring the patient;
- who is administering medications when required;
- who is responsible for equipment;
- and what the backup plan will be if the initial attempt fails.

### Equipment Preparation

The team should prepare appropriate:

- airway equipment;
- suction;
- oxygen delivery;
- ventilation equipment;
- monitoring;
- intravenous or intraosseous access;
- medications where required;
- and rescue airway equipment.

The exact equipment should reflect local protocols and available expertise.

### Plan for Failure Before the First Attempt

A definitive airway plan should include a clear strategy for difficulty or failure.

The leader should ask:

> **"What will we do if the first plan does not work?"**

This should be answered before the airway becomes an emergency.

## Rapid Sequence Intubation in Trauma

### Purpose

When definitive airway management is required, rapid sequence intubation may be used according to local protocols, available expertise, and the patient's clinical condition.

The aim is to achieve a secure airway while minimising:

- aspiration risk;
- hypoxia;
- haemodynamic deterioration;
- and unnecessary delay.

### Physiological Preparation

Trauma patients may have limited physiological reserve.

Before airway intervention, the team should consider:

- current oxygenation;
- ventilation;
- haemodynamic status;
- suspected haemorrhage;
- neurological injury;
- and the likelihood of deterioration during the procedure.

Airway management can itself produce physiological deterioration.

The leader should therefore prepare for this possibility rather than assuming that intubation will improve the patient's condition immediately.

### Preoxygenation

Preoxygenation should be optimised whenever the clinical situation permits.

The objective is to maximise oxygen reserve before airway intervention.

Patients with severe trauma may desaturate rapidly, particularly when there is impaired oxygenation, ventilation, or significant physiological disturbance.

### Haemodynamic Considerations

The leader should recognise that induction and positive-pressure ventilation may worsen haemodynamic instability in selected trauma patients.

This is particularly important when there is:

- significant blood loss;
- obstructive thoracic pathology;
- or limited cardiovascular reserve.

The team should therefore anticipate and prepare for peri-intubation deterioration.

## Difficult Airway Considerations

### Predicting Difficulty

Difficult airway management may result from:

- facial trauma;
- blood or vomit;
- airway swelling;
- distorted anatomy;
- limited access;
- reduced mouth opening;
- cervical spine considerations;
- or rapidly deteriorating physiology.

The leader should identify these factors before the airway becomes critical whenever possible.

### First Attempt Matters

Repeated unsuccessful attempts may worsen:

- hypoxia;
- airway trauma;
- bleeding;
- swelling;
- and overall physiological instability.

The team should therefore avoid an unstructured series of repeated attempts.

Each attempt should be deliberate and supported by an appropriate plan.

### Escalation

If the initial airway strategy is unsuccessful, the team should move promptly to the predefined backup plan.

The exact rescue strategy depends on local protocols, available equipment, and operator expertise.

The key leadership principle is that failure should trigger escalation rather than repetition without modification.

## Confirmation of the Airway

### Clinical Confirmation

Following airway placement, the team should immediately assess whether the airway is functioning as intended.

This includes reassessment of:

- chest movement;
- breath sounds;
- oxygenation;
- ventilation;
- and overall physiological response.

### Objective Confirmation

Where available and appropriate, objective confirmation of tracheal placement should be used according to local practice.

The leader should ensure that tube placement is not simply assumed because the procedure appeared successful.

### Secure the Airway

Once correct placement has been established, the airway should be secured appropriately.

The team should also ensure that the patient continues to receive adequate:

- oxygenation;
- ventilation;
- sedation and analgesia where appropriate;
- monitoring;
- and ongoing reassessment.

## Failed or Difficult Airway

### Recognise Failure Early

A failed airway strategy should be recognised before repeated attempts cause significant physiological deterioration.

Warning signs include:

- worsening oxygenation;
- inability to ventilate effectively;
- repeated unsuccessful attempts;
- worsening airway contamination;
- and rapidly deteriorating physiology.

### Call for Help Early

The Trauma Team Leader should request additional expertise early when difficulty is anticipated or encountered.

Waiting until the patient is profoundly hypoxic or unstable may reduce the available options.

### Maintain Oxygenation

During airway difficulty, maintaining oxygenation and effective ventilation remains the immediate priority.

The team should return to the most effective available oxygenation and ventilation strategy while progressing through the agreed rescue plan.

### Rescue Strategy

The team should follow its predefined difficult-airway and failed-airway pathway.

The precise technical sequence depends on local protocols and available expertise.

The leadership principle is universal:

> **Recognise failure → Maintain oxygenation → Escalate → Reassess → Proceed with the safest available rescue strategy**

## Post-Intubation Management

### Airway Management Does Not End When the Tube Is Placed

Successful intubation is not the endpoint of airway management.

The leader should ensure continued assessment of:

- tube position;
- oxygenation;
- ventilation;
- haemodynamics;
- sedation and analgesia;
- and the patient's overall trajectory.

### Reassess After Major Changes

The airway should be reassessed after:

- transfer;
- changes in position;
- deterioration in oxygenation;
- changes in ventilation;
- or any unexpected physiological change.

A previously confirmed airway can become displaced or obstructed.

### Continue the Primary Survey

After definitive airway management, the team should immediately return to the broader trauma assessment.

Securing the airway does not remove the need to assess:

- breathing;
- circulation;
- neurological status;
- and ongoing haemorrhage.

## Trauma Team Leader Perspective

### Maintain the Overview

The Trauma Team Leader should avoid becoming absorbed in the airway procedure unless personally required to perform it.

The leader's responsibility is to maintain situational awareness and coordinate the wider resuscitation.

### Allocate Roles Clearly

Critical airway tasks should have identifiable owners.

For example:

> **"Airway lead, prepare for definitive airway management. Anaesthesia monitor the patient and prepare medications. Nurse one manage suction and equipment. Nurse two ensure vascular access and prepare for haemodynamic support. I want an airway status update after the intervention."**

Clear delegation reduces ambiguity during high-risk procedures.

### Communicate the Plan

Before an anticipated difficult airway, the leader should communicate:

- the indication;
- the primary plan;
- the backup plan;
- the roles of team members;
- and the trigger for escalation.

The team should know what will happen if the initial strategy fails.

### Keep the Airway in Context

The airway is important, but it should not distract the team from other immediately reversible causes of deterioration.

The leader should continue to consider:

- major haemorrhage;
- tension pneumothorax;
- severe traumatic brain injury;
- and other life-threatening pathology.

The airway plan should therefore remain integrated with the entire resuscitation strategy.

## Common Errors

### Waiting for Complete Airway Obstruction

Waiting until the airway is completely lost may turn a controlled intervention into a rescue emergency.

The team should recognise progressive deterioration and anticipate the need for definitive management.

### Repeated Unstructured Attempts

Multiple attempts without a clear escalation strategy may worsen hypoxia and airway trauma.

A difficult airway should trigger structured escalation.

### Focusing Only on the Tube

Successful tube placement does not mean the patient is safe.

The team must continue to monitor oxygenation, ventilation, haemodynamics, and the underlying trauma.

### Ignoring Haemodynamics

Airway interventions can produce cardiovascular deterioration in physiologically fragile trauma patients.

The team should anticipate this and prepare accordingly.

### Failing to Confirm Placement

An airway should not be considered secure simply because the tube has been inserted.

Placement must be assessed and objectively confirmed where appropriate.

### Forgetting the Backup Plan

A primary airway strategy should always have a defined response to failure.

The leader should establish this before the first attempt whenever possible.

## Reassessment

Airway assessment should be repeated:

- after major interventions;
- after changes in neurological status;
- after deterioration in oxygenation;
- after deterioration in ventilation;
- after transfer or repositioning;
- and whenever the clinical picture changes.

A useful mental model is:

> **Assess → Optimise → Prepare → Secure → Confirm → Reassess**

This cycle should continue throughout the resuscitation.

## Clinical Pearl

> **The safest trauma airway is not simply the airway that can be secured. It is the airway that is recognised early, planned carefully, secured with appropriate expertise, and continuously reassessed.**
