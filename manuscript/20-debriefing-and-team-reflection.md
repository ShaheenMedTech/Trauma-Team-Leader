# 20. Debriefing and Team Reflection

> **A trauma resuscitation does not end when the patient leaves the resuscitation room.**
>
> The team must also learn from what happened.

---

## 20.1 Introduction

Debriefing is a structured opportunity for the trauma team to pause after a resuscitation, reconstruct what happened, identify what worked well, recognise what could have been better, and convert experience into improved future performance.

For the trauma team leader, debriefing is not an optional administrative exercise.

It is part of clinical leadership.

A technically successful resuscitation may still contain:

- communication failures,
- delays in recognition,
- unclear role allocation,
- equipment problems,
- cognitive overload,
- unnecessary duplication of work,
- delayed escalation,
- uncertainty about responsibility,
- problems with patient flow,
- or opportunities to improve team coordination.

Conversely, a difficult outcome does not automatically mean that the team performed poorly.

A patient may die despite excellent clinical care.

The purpose of debriefing is therefore not to determine who was right or wrong.

The purpose is to understand:

> **What happened?**

> **Why did it happen?**

> **What should we continue?**

> **What should we change?**

> **What will we do differently next time?**

The trauma team leader has an important role in creating the conditions in which these questions can be answered honestly and constructively.

---

# 20.2 The Purpose of Debriefing

A useful debrief should achieve several objectives.

### 1. Consolidate learning

The team identifies important clinical and behavioural lessons while the events are still relatively fresh.

### 2. Reinforce effective behaviour

Good performance should be explicitly recognised.

This is important because teams learn not only from errors but also from successful behaviours.

### 3. Identify system problems

Some problems arise from the system rather than from an individual.

Examples include:

- unavailable equipment,
- inadequate staffing,
- unclear escalation pathways,
- delayed imaging,
- poor access to blood products,
- communication-system failures,
- inappropriate room layout,
- or unclear institutional protocols.

### 4. Identify human-factor problems

The team may identify:

- fixation,
- tunnel vision,
- poor situational awareness,
- excessive task saturation,
- ineffective delegation,
- failure to speak up,
- unclear leadership,
- or poor information sharing.

### 5. Improve future performance

The debrief should result in actionable learning.

A debrief that identifies the same problem repeatedly without changing the system is incomplete.

---

# 20.3 Debriefing Is Not Blame

One of the most important principles of trauma-team debriefing is:

> **Debrief the process, not the person.**

A poor outcome should not automatically trigger statements such as:

- "Who made the mistake?"
- "Why didn't you notice this?"
- "Who forgot to call?"
- "Why didn't you do the scan earlier?"

These questions may create defensiveness without identifying the underlying problem.

Instead ask:

- "What happened at that point?"
- "What information did the team have?"
- "What information was missing?"
- "What were we expecting?"
- "What made that difficult?"
- "Was the responsibility clear?"
- "What could make this easier next time?"

The objective is to understand performance in context.

---

# 20.4 Outcome Versus Performance

The trauma team leader must distinguish between:

## Outcome

What happened to the patient.

and:

## Performance

How the team responded to the situation.

These are related but not identical.

A patient may have:

- severe unsurvivable injury,
- prolonged hypoxia before arrival,
- catastrophic haemorrhage,
- major traumatic brain injury,
- or overwhelming physiological derangement.

A poor outcome may therefore occur despite appropriate management.

Similarly, a patient may survive despite major delays or significant system failures.

Therefore:

> **Do not use outcome alone as a measure of team performance.**

The debrief should examine the decisions and actions available to the team at the time.

---

# 20.5 The Timing of Debriefing

There is no single moment at which every trauma debrief should occur.

Different types of debriefing may be useful.

## Immediate or "Hot" Debrief

A hot debrief occurs shortly after the resuscitation.

It is usually brief.

Its purpose is to capture:

- immediate concerns,
- emotional reactions,
- major clinical lessons,
- equipment or system problems,
- and urgent actions.

It may take only a few minutes.

---

## Delayed or "Cold" Debrief

A cold debrief occurs later.

It may involve:

- a more detailed review,
- chart review,
- imaging review,
- protocol review,
- multidisciplinary discussion,
- morbidity and mortality processes,
- or formal quality-improvement work.

The cold debrief allows greater reflection because participants have had time to review the case.

---

# 20.6 The Hot Debrief

A useful hot debrief should be:

- brief,
- structured,
- psychologically safe,
- focused,
- and actionable.

The trauma team leader does not need to conduct a lengthy meeting immediately after every case.

A simple structure is often enough.

### A practical sequence

1. Stabilise the environment.
2. Confirm the patient has been handed over appropriately.
3. Check that immediate team needs are addressed.
4. Thank the team.
5. Ask what went well.
6. Ask what was difficult.
7. Identify immediate safety concerns.
8. Identify one or two changes for the next case.
9. Assign responsibility for urgent actions.
10. Close the debrief.

---

# 20.7 First: Stabilise the Team

The team leader should not begin a debrief while the team is still actively managing unresolved clinical problems.

Before starting:

- ensure the patient has been appropriately handed over,
- confirm ongoing treatment plans,
- ensure required investigations or procedures are complete or delegated,
- address immediate safety issues,
- ensure equipment is restored,
- and ensure staff understand whether another trauma activation is possible.

Only then should the team transition into reflection.

---

# 20.8 Thank the Team

A simple acknowledgement is valuable.

For example:

> "Thank you, everyone. Before we finish, let's take two minutes to review the resuscitation."

This signals that:

- the clinical phase has ended,
- reflection is expected,
- and every member of the team may contribute.

The leader should avoid beginning with criticism.

---

# 20.9 What Went Well?

Begin by identifying effective performance.

Ask:

> "What went well?"

Potential answers may include:

- early recognition of haemorrhage,
- rapid activation of the massive transfusion pathway,
- effective airway preparation,
- clear role allocation,
- timely surgical involvement,
- good communication,
- successful escalation,
- efficient transfer to theatre,
- or appropriate use of imaging.

This question serves two purposes.

First, it identifies behaviours worth repeating.

Second, it prevents the debrief from becoming an exercise focused exclusively on failure.

---

# 20.10 What Was Difficult?

The next question should explore difficulty.

Ask:

> "What was difficult?"

Examples include:

- unclear information from the ambulance team,
- difficulty obtaining intravenous access,
- conflicting priorities,
- limited personnel,
- equipment failure,
- unclear responsibility,
- delayed blood products,
- difficult airway,
- unexpected deterioration,
- or uncertainty about the next destination.

The word **difficult** is useful.

It invites discussion without immediately assigning blame.

---

# 20.11 What Should We Change?

The final key question is:

> "What should we do differently next time?"

The answer should be specific.

Weak conclusion:

> "Communication needs to improve."

Better conclusion:

> "During the next major trauma activation, the team leader will ask for a verbal confirmation of the blood-product plan before moving the patient."

The second statement can be implemented and evaluated.

---

# 20.12 The Three-Question Debrief

When time is extremely limited, use three questions:

### 1. What went well?

### 2. What did not go well?

### 3. What should we change next time?

This simple structure can be completed rapidly.

It is preferable to having no debrief at all.

---

# 20.13 The Five-Question Debrief

For more complex cases:

### 1. What happened?

Establish a shared understanding of events.

### 2. What went well?

Identify strengths.

### 3. What did not go as expected?

Identify problems without prematurely assigning blame.

### 4. Why did this happen?

Explore clinical, human-factor, communication, and system contributors.

### 5. What will we change?

Convert learning into action.

---

# 20.14 Psychological Safety

Effective debriefing requires psychological safety.

Team members should feel able to say:

- "I was not sure what was happening."
- "I did not understand the instruction."
- "I thought someone else had done it."
- "I was worried about the airway."
- "I did not know who was leading."
- "I wanted to speak but could not get the leader's attention."
- "The equipment was not available."
- "I think we missed something."

These statements can reveal important problems.

If team members fear humiliation or punishment, they are less likely to disclose them.

Therefore:

> **A debrief must be challenging without being threatening.**

---

# 20.15 The Leader Sets the Tone

The behaviour of the trauma team leader strongly influences the debrief.

If the leader says:

> "Everything was fine. We just need to be faster."

the discussion may stop immediately.

If the leader says:

> "There were several difficult moments. I would like to hear what each of you noticed."

the team is more likely to engage.

The leader should demonstrate:

- openness,
- humility,
- curiosity,
- respect,
- and willingness to hear criticism.

---

# 20.16 Leader Vulnerability

A powerful leadership behaviour is acknowledging one's own uncertainty or error.

For example:

> "I think I became focused on the airway and may have missed the change in the circulation. Did anyone else notice that?"

This communicates that speaking honestly is acceptable.

It also demonstrates that leadership does not require pretending to be infallible.

---

# 20.17 Avoiding the Blame Trap

A debrief can deteriorate rapidly if it becomes a search for an individual to blame.

For example:

> "Why didn't you give the blood?"

may produce defensiveness.

A better question is:

> "We expected blood to be running earlier. What prevented that from happening?"

The second question explores the process.

Possible explanations may include:

- blood was not immediately available,
- the request was unclear,
- responsibility was ambiguous,
- the blood bank was not informed,
- the team member was managing another critical task,
- or the instruction was never closed-loop confirmed.

The goal is to identify the barrier.

---

# 20.18 Human Factors

Many trauma-team problems are not caused by lack of knowledge.

They arise from the interaction between:

- people,
- environment,
- workload,
- equipment,
- communication,
- and decision-making.

A debrief should therefore consider human factors.

Ask:

- Was the workload excessive?
- Were too many tasks assigned to one person?
- Was the team leader overloaded?
- Were roles clear?
- Did information reach the right person?
- Was the environment noisy?
- Was the patient position problematic?
- Did interruptions occur?
- Did the team become fixated on one problem?

---

# 20.19 Cognitive Overload

Trauma resuscitation can generate a large amount of information.

The team may be simultaneously dealing with:

- airway management,
- ventilation,
- circulation,
- haemorrhage control,
- monitoring,
- blood products,
- imaging,
- procedures,
- medications,
- laboratory results,
- family information,
- and transfer decisions.

A debrief should explore whether cognitive overload contributed to problems.

For example:

> "At what point did the workload become difficult to manage?"

This may reveal that:

- another senior clinician should have been called earlier,
- a task should have been delegated,
- or the leader needed to step back and reassess.

---

# 20.20 Fixation

Fixation occurs when attention becomes excessively focused on one problem.

Examples include:

- focusing on the airway while haemorrhage continues,
- repeatedly attempting intravenous access while other routes are available,
- focusing on an abnormal imaging finding while the patient deteriorates,
- or continuing a procedure despite changing priorities.

A debrief should ask:

> "Did we become too focused on one problem?"

If so:

> "What could have helped us step back earlier?"

---

# 20.21 Loss of Situational Awareness

A team can lose awareness of the overall situation even when individual tasks are being completed correctly.

The debrief should consider:

- Did everyone know the current priority?
- Did the team know the patient's physiological trend?
- Did the leader know what tasks were outstanding?
- Did anyone recognise deterioration before it was communicated?
- Was there a shared understanding of the next step?

---

# 20.22 Communication Problems

Communication failures are common contributors to trauma-team difficulties.

Explore:

- unclear instructions,
- incomplete information,
- assumptions,
- interruptions,
- failure to confirm,
- inappropriate communication channels,
- or excessive background noise.

Ask:

> "Was there any point where someone did not know what was happening?"

This can identify hidden communication failures.

---

# 20.23 Closed-Loop Communication During Debrief

The same communication principles used during resuscitation should be applied to the debrief.

If the leader assigns an action:

> "Sarah, please review whether the blood-warmer was functioning."

Sarah should confirm:

> "I'll check it."

Later:

> "The blood-warmer was functioning, but the tubing was not available."

The team has now completed a communication loop.

---

# 20.24 The Importance of Speaking Up

Every team member should be able to raise a concern.

A debrief should ask:

> "Did anyone have a concern that they felt unable to raise?"

This is particularly important if the team encountered:

- rapidly changing physiology,
- airway difficulty,
- unexpected bleeding,
- medication errors,
- equipment problems,
- or uncertainty about the plan.

The leader should reinforce:

> **Speaking up is a clinical responsibility, not a challenge to authority.**

---

# 20.25 The Two-Challenge Principle

If a team member identifies a significant safety concern and it is not acknowledged, escalation should be possible.

A practical approach is:

### First challenge

Clearly state the concern.

> "I'm concerned the patient is becoming unstable."

### Second challenge

If the concern is not addressed:

> "I need the team to stop for a moment. I think we need to reassess the patient now."

The precise institutional escalation process may vary.

The underlying principle remains:

> **Important safety concerns should not disappear because the hierarchy is strong.**

---

# 20.26 Role Clarity

Debriefing should examine whether roles were clear.

Ask:

- Who was leading?
- Who was managing the airway?
- Who was responsible for circulation?
- Who was monitoring?
- Who was documenting?
- Who was communicating with radiology?
- Who was coordinating blood products?
- Who was preparing for transfer?

If people were unsure:

> "What could we change during the briefing to make this clearer?"

---

# 20.27 Task Ownership

A recurring problem is the difference between:

> "Someone should do this."

and:

> "Ahmed will do this."

The first creates ambiguity.

The second creates ownership.

The debrief should identify tasks that were delayed because ownership was unclear.

---

# 20.28 The Handover Interface

The transition between teams is a high-risk point.

Debrief:

- Was the receiving team ready?
- Was the patient stable enough for transfer?
- Was the diagnosis communicated?
- Were outstanding concerns communicated?
- Were interventions and medications documented?
- Was the destination clear?
- Was responsibility transferred explicitly?

A successful resuscitation can still be followed by a poor handover.

---

# 20.29 Debriefing Equipment Problems

Equipment problems should be captured systematically.

Examples:

- missing equipment,
- incorrect equipment,
- equipment not functioning,
- equipment difficult to locate,
- insufficient stock,
- unfamiliar equipment,
- or unclear responsibility for restocking.

Do not simply say:

> "The equipment was missing."

Instead determine:

- What was missing?
- Why was it missing?
- Where should it have been?
- Who is responsible for replacement?
- How will the problem be prevented?

---

# 20.30 Debriefing Blood-Product Problems

If blood-product delivery was delayed, determine the pathway.

Ask:

- Was the need recognised early?
- Was the blood bank contacted?
- Was the correct protocol activated?
- Was the request understood?
- Were products available?
- Was the transport pathway clear?
- Was the blood warmer available?
- Was administration delayed by equipment or personnel?

This converts a vague complaint into a process problem that can be investigated.

---

# 20.31 Debriefing Imaging Problems

For patients requiring urgent imaging, review:

- decision-making,
- timing,
- physiological stability,
- scanner availability,
- communication with radiology,
- transport,
- monitoring,
- and interpretation.

The question should not simply be:

> "Why was CT delayed?"

Ask:

> "What happened between the decision to obtain CT and the patient entering the scanner?"

This reveals the actual process.

---

# 20.32 Debriefing Theatre Transfer

For patients requiring operative management, consider:

- Was the decision made early enough?
- Was the surgical team informed?
- Was anaesthesia involved?
- Was theatre ready?
- Was blood available?
- Was the patient appropriately monitored?
- Were essential interventions completed?
- Was the receiving team aware of the clinical trajectory?

The objective is to identify delays and prevent recurrence.

---

# 20.33 Debriefing the Primary Survey

Review the primary survey systematically.

### Airway

- Was airway risk recognised?
- Was the plan clear?
- Was equipment available?
- Was there difficulty with intubation?
- Was escalation timely?

### Breathing

- Were life-threatening thoracic injuries recognised?
- Was oxygenation monitored?
- Were interventions performed promptly?

### Circulation

- Was haemorrhage recognised?
- Was vascular access adequate?
- Was blood-product resuscitation appropriate?
- Was haemorrhage control prioritised?

### Disability

- Was neurological deterioration recognised?
- Was glucose checked when appropriate?
- Was neurological status communicated?

### Exposure

- Was the patient adequately exposed?
- Was heat loss minimised?
- Was the patient systematically reassessed?

The review should remain focused on team performance rather than becoming an exhaustive retrospective clinical critique.

---

# 20.34 Reassessment

A trauma patient is dynamic.

A debrief should ask:

> "Did our assessment change when the patient's physiology changed?"

If not, explore why.

Potential contributors include:

- fixation,
- incomplete reassessment,
- poor monitoring,
- communication failure,
- competing priorities,
- or unclear leadership.

---

# 20.35 The Importance of Time

Time perception during resuscitation can be unreliable.

Team members may remember:

> "We did it quickly."

while documentation shows a significant delay.

Where relevant, objective timelines can help.

Review:

- arrival,
- first assessment,
- airway intervention,
- blood-product activation,
- imaging,
- procedures,
- consultant arrival,
- theatre decision,
- transfer,
- and definitive treatment.

The purpose is not to create a punitive stopwatch.

It is to understand the sequence.

---
