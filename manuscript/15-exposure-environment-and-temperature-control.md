# Chapter 15 — Exposure, Environment and Temperature Control

## Clinical Scenario

A 42-year-old man is brought to the resuscitation room following a high-speed road traffic collision.

He has obvious deformity of the left thigh and multiple areas of bruising across the chest and abdomen.

His initial airway is patent and his oxygenation is acceptable. He is tachycardic but initially maintains his blood pressure.

The team begins the primary survey.

As the assessment progresses, the Trauma Team Leader recognises that the patient cannot be adequately assessed while fully clothed. At the same time, the resuscitation room is cold, the patient's clothing is wet, and several interventions are already exposing large areas of skin.

The leader therefore asks the team to expose the patient systematically while actively preventing further heat loss.

The patient is covered with warm blankets, wet clothing is removed, warmed fluids are prepared, and temperature monitoring is established.

Twenty minutes later, the patient's temperature is falling and his physiological state is worsening.

The leader recognises that temperature is no longer simply an environmental issue. It has become part of the patient's resuscitation problem.

The key question is therefore:

> **How can the team obtain the information it needs from exposure without allowing exposure itself to worsen the patient's physiology?**

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain the purpose of exposure during trauma resuscitation.
2. Perform systematic exposure while preserving dignity and preventing unnecessary heat loss.
3. Identify injuries that may remain hidden beneath clothing or equipment.
4. Assess the posterior surface when clinically indicated.
5. Recognise environmental factors that contribute to heat loss.
6. Establish and monitor the patient's temperature.
7. Understand the relationship between hypothermia, haemorrhage and physiological deterioration.
8. Initiate active measures to prevent heat loss early.
9. Recognise patients at increased risk of hypothermia.
10. Integrate temperature management with the wider trauma resuscitation.
11. Reassess temperature and physiological response after intervention.
12. Coordinate the team so that exposure and warming occur simultaneously.

## Key Concepts

### Exposure Is an Assessment, Not Simply Undressing

The purpose of exposure is to identify injuries that may otherwise remain hidden.

The patient should therefore be exposed sufficiently to permit an effective assessment of the entire body while avoiding unnecessary exposure.

The leader should ask:

- What areas have not yet been examined?
- Could an important injury be hidden by clothing?
- Does the patient need further exposure?
- How will heat loss be prevented?

Exposure should have a clinical purpose.

### Exposure Must Be Controlled

Complete exposure may be necessary in major trauma, but prolonged unrestricted exposure increases heat loss.

The team should therefore expose, assess, and cover in a controlled sequence.

The principle is:

> **Expose to assess → identify injuries → cover to protect.**

The team should avoid leaving large areas of the body uncovered while attention is directed elsewhere.

### Temperature Is a Physiological Variable

Temperature should not be treated as an afterthought.

A falling temperature may accompany severe trauma, haemorrhage, environmental exposure, prolonged pre-hospital time, or inadequate warming during resuscitation.

The patient's temperature should therefore be incorporated into the overall physiological assessment.

The leader should consider temperature alongside heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory status, mental state and evidence of ongoing blood loss.

### Hypothermia Can Worsen Trauma Physiology

Hypothermia may contribute to impaired coagulation and worsening physiological instability.

In a bleeding patient, allowing temperature to fall unnecessarily may contribute to progression of the physiological spiral.

The leader should therefore treat prevention of heat loss as part of resuscitation.

### The Primary Survey Remains the Priority

Exposure must not become an isolated examination that distracts the team from immediately life-threatening problems.

If the patient develops airway obstruction, respiratory failure or major haemorrhage during exposure, those problems take priority.

The leader should maintain the primary survey framework while coordinating exposure and environmental control.

## The Exposure Assessment

### Remove Clothing When Necessary

Clothing should be removed when it prevents adequate assessment or treatment.

Cutting clothing may be faster and safer than attempting to remove it conventionally.

The team should avoid unnecessary manipulation of injured limbs.

Clothing should not be removed simply because complete undressing is possible. It should be removed when doing so improves assessment, treatment or access.

### Look for Hidden Injury

Exposure should allow assessment for:

- penetrating wounds;
- bleeding;
- deformity;
- soft-tissue injury;
- swelling;
- bruising;
- burns;
- wounds concealed beneath clothing;
- and injuries to areas not visible during the initial assessment.

The absence of an obvious injury on initial inspection does not exclude significant trauma.

The leader should deliberately consider whether the mechanism makes a hidden injury likely.

### Examine the Entire Patient

The leader should maintain awareness of areas that are easily missed.

These include:

- the back;
- axillae;
- perineum;
- buttocks;
- posterior limbs;
- and areas concealed by clothing or equipment.

The extent of examination should be guided by the mechanism and clinical condition.

### Preserve Dignity and Privacy

Exposure should remain clinically justified.

The patient should be protected from unnecessary embarrassment, particularly when multiple staff members are present.

The leader can achieve this without compromising assessment by ensuring that areas not being examined are covered.

Professional handling of exposure is part of good trauma-team practice.

## Posterior Assessment

### The Back Must Not Be Forgotten

Important injuries may be present on the posterior surface.

When a posterior examination is required, it should be coordinated with spinal precautions and the wider resuscitation.

The leader should avoid unnecessary repeated movement.

A posterior assessment may identify wounds, bleeding, deformity, bruising, burns or other injuries that are not visible from the front.

### Log-Rolling Requires Coordination

If a log-roll is required, responsibilities should be assigned before movement.

The team should understand:

- who controls the head and spine;
- who coordinates the movement;
- who examines the back;
- who manages lines and tubes;
- and who returns the patient safely to the original position.

The leader should confirm readiness before the movement begins.

The movement should be purposeful and coordinated rather than repeated simply because the team has not decided who is responsible for examining the posterior surface.

### The Timing of Posterior Examination Matters

A posterior examination should occur when clinically indicated and when it can be performed safely.

The leader should consider whether the patient is sufficiently stable for the planned movement.

If immediate life-saving treatment is required, posterior examination should not create avoidable delay.

## Environmental Heat Loss

### The Resuscitation Environment Matters

Heat loss can be increased by:

- cold ambient temperature;
- wet clothing;
- prolonged exposure;
- uncovered skin;
- cold intravenous fluids;
- cold irrigation fluids;
- and prolonged procedures.

These factors may appear individually minor but become clinically important when combined.

### Wet Clothing Should Be Removed

Wet clothing increases heat loss.

When clinically appropriate, wet clothing should be removed and replaced with measures that reduce further heat loss.

The team should balance removal of wet clothing against the need to maintain access, privacy and safe handling of injured areas.

### Minimise Unnecessary Exposure

The team should avoid leaving the patient uncovered after an area has been assessed.

The leader can reinforce this by assigning responsibility for ongoing warming and covering.

The patient should remain covered between assessments whenever this is compatible with ongoing treatment.

### The Room Itself Can Contribute to Heat Loss

A cold resuscitation environment can undermine otherwise appropriate warming measures.

The team should consider the ambient environment early, particularly during prolonged procedures or when large areas of the patient's body remain exposed.

## Prevention of Hypothermia

### Start Early

Warming should not wait until significant hypothermia has developed.

Patients at risk should be protected from heat loss from the beginning of resuscitation.

This is particularly important when the patient is already cold, wet, shocked, bleeding, or expected to undergo prolonged treatment.

### Use Active and Passive Measures

Depending on the clinical environment and available resources, measures may include:

- warm blankets;
- forced-air warming;
- warmed intravenous fluids;
- warmed blood products where available;
- increasing ambient temperature;
- removal of wet clothing;
- and minimising unnecessary exposure.

The exact approach should follow local protocols and available equipment.

### Warm Fluids and Blood Products Appropriately

Large-volume administration of cold fluids can contribute to heat loss.

Where warming systems are available and indicated, intravenous fluids and blood products should be managed according to local major-trauma and warming protocols.

Temperature management should therefore be considered when planning resuscitation rather than after large volumes have already been administered.

### Warming Is a Team Responsibility

Temperature control should not be delegated to nobody.

The leader should assign ownership for:

- temperature measurement;
- warming equipment;
- fluid and blood warming;
- covering the patient;
- and reassessment.

## Recognising Hypothermia

### Do Not Rely Only on Appearance

A patient may become hypothermic during prolonged trauma care without dramatic initial clinical findings.

Temperature measurement is therefore important when the risk is significant.

Clinical appearance should be interpreted alongside objective temperature measurement and the wider physiological picture.

### Falling Temperature Is a Trend

A single measurement provides less information than the direction of change.

The leader should consider:

> **What was the temperature, what is it now, and why is it changing?**

A falling temperature in a patient with ongoing haemorrhage should increase concern about worsening physiology.

### Consider the Context

Temperature should be interpreted in relation to:

- duration of exposure;
- pre-hospital conditions;
- haemorrhage;
- shock;
- fluid administration;
- operative or procedural time;
- burns;
- and the patient's ability to generate or retain heat.

## Hypothermia and Haemorrhage

### The Problems Can Reinforce Each Other

Major trauma may produce haemorrhage, impaired perfusion, and heat loss simultaneously.

As physiological instability develops, hypothermia may become part of a broader deterioration.

The leader should therefore manage temperature alongside haemorrhage control rather than treating them as separate problems.

### Prevention Is Easier Than Rescue

Once significant hypothermia has developed, correction may be difficult during ongoing resuscitation.

Early prevention is therefore preferable.

The team should anticipate heat loss before it becomes clinically significant.

### Rewarming Does Not Replace Haemorrhage Control

Warming is supportive treatment.

It does not stop traumatic bleeding.

A patient with ongoing haemorrhage requires simultaneous attention to haemorrhage control, resuscitation, temperature and definitive care.

The leader should avoid allowing temperature management to become a distraction from the source of physiological deterioration.

## Temperature Monitoring

### Establish a Baseline

When clinically relevant, obtain an early temperature measurement.

Document the value and interpret it alongside the rest of the patient's physiology.

The measurement should be obtained using an appropriate method for the clinical situation and local equipment.

### Reassess After Major Changes

Temperature should be reassessed when:

- resuscitation is prolonged;
- major haemorrhage continues;
- large volumes of fluid or blood are administered;
- the patient has been exposed for a prolonged period;
- warming interventions are started;
- or the clinical condition deteriorates.

### Monitor the Response to Warming

The presence of warming equipment does not prove that warming is effective.

The leader should ask whether:

- the warming method is functioning;
- the patient remains exposed;
- additional heat loss is occurring;
- and the temperature trend is improving.

Failure to improve should trigger reassessment rather than simply adding more equipment.

## Special Situations

### Prolonged Pre-Hospital Exposure

Patients exposed to cold environments before arrival may already have significant heat loss.

The resuscitation team should assume that additional exposure can worsen the problem.

The pre-hospital history should therefore include environmental conditions and duration of exposure when relevant.

### Wet or Contaminated Patients

Wet clothing should be removed when feasible, while maintaining appropriate infection-control and environmental precautions.

The team should continue to protect the patient from heat loss during decontamination or cleaning.

### Major Burns

Burn patients require particular attention to heat loss because large areas of skin may be exposed.

Assessment and treatment should therefore be coordinated with aggressive prevention of unnecessary heat loss.

### Paediatric and Vulnerable Patients

Children and other vulnerable patients may lose heat rapidly.

The threshold for active warming should therefore be appropriately low.

### Prolonged Procedures

Patients undergoing prolonged imaging, operative preparation, procedures or transfer may remain exposed for extended periods.

The leader should anticipate this and ensure that temperature protection continues beyond the initial assessment.

## Reassessment

### Reassess After Exposure

Exposure is not complete when the patient has been undressed.

The leader should confirm:

- what injuries were found;
- what remains unassessed;
- whether the patient is covered;
- whether temperature has been measured;
- and whether warming measures are effective.

### Reassess the Whole Patient

Temperature deterioration should trigger consideration of the entire clinical picture.

The leader should ask whether the patient is:

- bleeding;
- inadequately perfused;
- exposed to a cold environment;
- receiving unwarmed fluids;
- undergoing prolonged procedures;
- or developing another cause of physiological deterioration.

### Reassessment Must Be Continuous

The exposure assessment should not be regarded as a single event.

As treatment progresses, clothing, dressings, equipment and procedures may conceal or reveal new areas.

The leader should therefore maintain awareness of what has been examined and what remains potentially hidden.

## The Trauma Team Leader's Role

### Assign Clear Ownership

The leader should ensure that someone is explicitly responsible for temperature control.

This prevents warming from becoming an assumed task that nobody performs.

### Maintain the Shared Mental Model

The team should understand why the patient is being exposed and why warming is occurring simultaneously.

A clear instruction might be:

> **“Expose for assessment, but keep the patient covered everywhere we are not actively examining.”**

### Coordinate Competing Priorities

Exposure competes with other demands for staff attention.

The leader should ensure that:

- airway and breathing remain protected;
- haemorrhage control continues;
- lines and monitoring remain secure;
- the patient is not left unnecessarily uncovered;
- and warming continues during other procedures.

### Anticipate the Next Problem

The leader should consider the likely duration of resuscitation.

If the patient is bleeding, requires surgery, needs prolonged imaging, or is likely to remain exposed, warming measures should be initiated early.

The leader should also consider whether transfer to another area may expose the patient to further heat loss.

## Practical Approach

### First 30 Seconds

The leader should establish:

1. What needs to be exposed?
2. What areas remain unseen?
3. Is the patient wet or cold?
4. Is temperature measurement required?
5. How will heat loss be prevented?
6. Who is responsible for warming and covering?

### Immediate Priorities

The team should:

- expose systematically;
- identify hidden injuries;
- control obvious bleeding;
- remove wet clothing when appropriate;
- minimise unnecessary exposure;
- initiate warming;
- monitor temperature;
- and continue the wider primary survey.

### During Posterior Assessment

If posterior examination is required:

1. announce the plan;
2. assign movement and examination roles;
3. secure lines and tubes;
4. perform the examination efficiently;
5. identify and communicate findings;
6. return the patient safely;
7. cover the patient promptly;
8. and reassess physiology.

### Reassess

After exposure and warming:

> **Expose → Assess → Cover → Warm → Reassess**

This cycle should continue throughout prolonged trauma resuscitation.

## Common Leadership Errors

### Leaving the Patient Uncovered

Unnecessary exposure can increase heat loss.

**Better approach:**

Expose only what needs to be assessed and cover the patient promptly.

### Forgetting Temperature Measurement

Temperature may be overlooked when attention is focused on airway, breathing and circulation.

**Better approach:**

Include temperature in the physiological assessment when clinically relevant.

### Treating Warming as a Later Intervention

Waiting until significant hypothermia develops may make correction more difficult.

**Better approach:**

Prevent heat loss from the beginning.

### Failing to Assign Responsibility

If nobody owns temperature management, important measures may be missed.

**Better approach:**

Assign a specific team member to monitor temperature and warming.

### Performing Unnecessary Movement

Repeated movement increases risk and consumes team attention.

**Better approach:**

Coordinate posterior assessment and movement only when clinically indicated.

### Focusing on Exposure Without Considering the Environment

The patient may be appropriately exposed but inadequately protected from heat loss.

**Better approach:**

Treat exposure and environmental control as one combined task.

### Assuming Warming Has Worked

Starting a warming intervention does not prove that heat loss has stopped.

**Better approach:**

Monitor the patient's temperature trend and inspect whether additional sources of heat loss remain.

### Allowing Temperature Management to Distract From Haemorrhage Control

Temperature control is important but does not replace treatment of life-threatening bleeding.

**Better approach:**

Manage exposure, warming and haemorrhage simultaneously according to priority.

## Clinical Pearl

> **Exposure should reveal what matters without creating another physiological problem.**

## Leadership Pearl

> **The best Trauma Team Leader exposes the patient enough to find the injuries, while protecting the patient from the consequences of being exposed.**

## Final Takeaway

Exposure completes the physical assessment of the trauma patient, but it must be performed deliberately.

The patient should be exposed sufficiently to identify important injuries while simultaneously protecting against environmental heat loss.

Temperature should be treated as part of the patient's physiological trajectory, particularly in major haemorrhage, prolonged resuscitation, cold environments, burns, and other situations associated with significant heat loss.

The Trauma Team Leader should continuously connect:

> **Exposure + Environment + Temperature + Overall Physiology**

The goal is not simply to expose the patient.

It is to:

> **Expose → Identify → Protect → Warm → Reassess**

## Summary

The key principles of exposure and temperature control in trauma are:

- expose the patient sufficiently for a complete assessment;
- look deliberately for hidden injuries;
- assess the posterior surface when indicated;
- coordinate movement carefully;
- remove wet clothing when appropriate;
- minimise unnecessary exposure;
- preserve patient dignity and privacy;
- measure temperature when clinically relevant;
- recognise falling temperature as a physiological trend;
- prevent heat loss early;
- use appropriate active and passive warming measures;
- integrate temperature management with haemorrhage and resuscitation;
- assign clear responsibility for warming;
- monitor the response to warming;
- reassess temperature and the whole patient;
- and anticipate deterioration before significant hypothermia develops.

> **Exposure is part of assessment, but temperature control is part of resuscitation.**
