# Chapter 3 — Primary Survey: <C>ABCDE

## Clinical Scenario

A 32-year-old man is brought to the resuscitation room following a high-speed road traffic collision.

The pre-hospital team reports significant vehicle intrusion, a brief period of reduced consciousness, increasing respiratory effort, and persistent tachycardia.

On arrival, the patient is pale and distressed. He is speaking, but only in short sentences.

Initial observations are:

- respiratory rate 32/min;
- oxygen saturation 93% despite supplemental oxygen;
- heart rate 132/min;
- systolic blood pressure 94 mmHg;
- Glasgow Coma Scale 13/15;
- cool peripheries.

There is no obvious catastrophic external haemorrhage.

The Trauma Team Leader immediately asks the team to begin the primary survey.

However, the leader must also recognise that the primary survey is not simply a sequence of examinations.

It is a framework for identifying and treating immediately life-threatening problems while continuously reassessing the patient's response.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain the purpose and structure of the <C>ABCDE primary survey.
2. Recognise catastrophic haemorrhage before progressing through the assessment.
3. Assess and manage immediate threats to the airway while maintaining appropriate cervical spine precautions.
4. Identify and treat life-threatening chest injuries during the breathing assessment.
5. Assess circulation and recognise major haemorrhage and evolving circulatory compromise.
6. Perform a focused neurological assessment and recognise deterioration.
7. Complete exposure while minimising preventable heat loss.
8. Coordinate parallel assessment and intervention during the primary survey.
9. Reassess the patient after major interventions and when physiology changes.
10. Recognise when the primary survey should trigger escalation towards definitive management.

## Key Concepts

### The Primary Survey Is a Treatment Framework

The primary survey is not simply a diagnostic checklist.

Its purpose is to identify conditions that can kill the patient rapidly and to ensure that appropriate treatment begins without unnecessary delay.

Assessment and treatment occur together.

A life-threatening problem that is recognised during the primary survey should be treated immediately rather than waiting for completion of the entire sequence.

### Why <C>ABCDE?

The initial priority is catastrophic haemorrhage because uncontrolled major bleeding can lead to death within minutes.

The sequence then proceeds through:

- **A — Airway and cervical spine**
- **B — Breathing**
- **C — Circulation**
- **D — Disability**
- **E — Exposure and environmental control**

The sequence provides structure, but it should not create rigid thinking.

If a life-threatening problem is identified at any stage, the team should intervene immediately.

### The Primary Survey Is Dynamic

The findings obtained during the first assessment represent the patient's condition at that moment.

They may change rapidly.

The leader should therefore repeatedly integrate:

**Mechanism → Injury pattern → Physiology → Response to treatment → Trajectory**

A patient who initially has an adequate airway may deteriorate.

A patient with apparently acceptable blood pressure may develop haemorrhagic shock.

A patient with an initially modest respiratory abnormality may develop a rapidly expanding chest threat.

The primary survey must therefore be repeated whenever the patient's condition changes and after major interventions.

### Parallel Processing

The primary survey should be systematic without being unnecessarily sequential.

Different team members may perform different components simultaneously.

For example:

- airway assessment may occur while another clinician assesses breathing;
- vascular access may be established while the chest is examined;
- blood samples may be obtained while neurological status is assessed;
- blood products may be prepared while the team identifies the likely source of haemorrhage.

The Trauma Team Leader coordinates these activities and maintains the overall priority order.

### Treat as You Find

The central principle is:

> **Identify → Treat → Reassess**

The team should not continue mechanically through the sequence when an immediately life-threatening problem has already been identified.

After intervention, the leader should ask:

> **"Did the patient respond as expected?"**

If not, the working diagnosis and overall strategy should be reconsidered.

## <C> Catastrophic Haemorrhage

### Why It Comes First

Major external or immediately recognisable haemorrhage can cause rapid cardiovascular collapse.

The first task is therefore to identify and control catastrophic bleeding.

This may include:

- direct pressure;
- appropriate haemostatic measures;
- tourniquet application when indicated;
- pelvic stabilisation when appropriate;
- activation of major haemorrhage pathways;
- and rapid progression towards definitive haemorrhage control.

The precise intervention depends on the source and severity of bleeding.

### Look Beyond External Bleeding

The absence of obvious external haemorrhage does not exclude major blood loss.

The leader should consider major internal bleeding according to:

- mechanism;
- injury pattern;
- physiological findings;
- examination;
- response to treatment;
- and trajectory.

Potential sources include the:

- chest;
- abdomen;
- pelvis;
- retroperitoneum;
- and long bones or other injured tissues.

### The Leader's Role

The Trauma Team Leader should ensure that suspected major haemorrhage is communicated clearly and that the required resources are activated early.

The leader should also consider whether the patient is moving towards a need for definitive haemorrhage control rather than allowing diagnostic processes to create avoidable delay.

## A — Airway and Cervical Spine

### Immediate Airway Assessment

The first question is whether the patient can maintain a patent airway and protect it adequately.

The leader should look for:

- ability to speak;
- abnormal airway sounds;
- facial or neck trauma;
- blood or secretions;
- airway obstruction;
- reduced consciousness;
- and progressive deterioration.

A patient who is speaking clearly currently has an apparently patent airway, but this does not guarantee that it will remain so.

### Anticipate Airway Deterioration

Airway compromise may develop because of:

- facial trauma;
- bleeding;
- swelling;
- burns;
- reduced consciousness;
- or evolving soft-tissue injury.

The team should therefore prepare appropriate airway equipment and expertise when deterioration is foreseeable.

### Cervical Spine Considerations

Cervical spine protection should be integrated into the airway assessment according to the mechanism, clinical findings, and local trauma protocols.

The presence of suspected cervical spine injury should not prevent the team from treating a life-threatening airway problem.

The leader must balance protection of the cervical spine with the immediate need to maintain oxygenation and a patent airway.

### When Airway Intervention Is Required

The decision to secure the airway should be based on the patient's clinical condition, anticipated trajectory, and ability to maintain airway patency and adequate ventilation and oxygenation.

The leader should ensure that appropriate expertise, equipment, monitoring, and a backup plan are available when advanced airway management is required.

## B — Breathing

### Immediate Assessment

The breathing assessment should rapidly identify immediately life-threatening thoracic conditions.

The leader should integrate:

- respiratory rate;
- work of breathing;
- oxygenation;
- chest movement;
- breath sounds;
- chest wall injury;
- tracheal position where relevant;
- and overall clinical trajectory.

### Life-Threatening Chest Problems

The team should actively consider conditions such as:

- tension pneumothorax;
- massive haemothorax;
- open pneumothorax;
- and other major thoracic injuries causing respiratory or circulatory compromise.

When a life-threatening chest problem is sufficiently recognised clinically, treatment should not be unnecessarily delayed by investigations.

### Response to Intervention

Following an intervention, the leader should reassess:

- respiratory effort;
- oxygenation;
- chest findings;
- haemodynamic status;
- and overall clinical response.

Failure to improve should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis, adequacy of treatment, and presence of another concurrent problem.

## C — Circulation

### Assess Perfusion, Not Just Blood Pressure

Circulatory assessment should include more than a single blood pressure measurement.

The leader should integrate:

- heart rate;
- blood pressure;
- peripheral perfusion;
- mental status;
- skin temperature;
- pulse characteristics;
- ongoing blood loss;
- and trends over time.

A preserved blood pressure does not exclude significant haemorrhage.

### Search for the Source

When haemorrhage is suspected, the team should actively consider the likely source rather than simply treating the physiological consequences.

The mechanism and injury pattern should guide the search.

### Reassessment

The response to resuscitation provides important information.

If the patient's physiology fails to improve as expected, the leader should ask:

- Is the presumed source correct?
- Is bleeding continuing?
- Has the intervention been effective?
- Is another life-threatening problem present?
- Does the patient require definitive haemorrhage control?

## D — Disability

### Focused Neurological Assessment

The disability assessment should rapidly establish the patient's neurological status and identify deterioration.

This includes assessment of:

- level of consciousness;
- pupil findings where relevant;
- limb movement;
- glucose when indicated;
- and changes from the previous neurological state.

### Trend Matters

A single neurological assessment is less informative than a trend.

A patient whose consciousness deteriorates during resuscitation requires immediate reassessment of the airway, breathing, circulation, and neurological status.

The leader should not assume that neurological deterioration is necessarily an isolated intracranial problem.

## E — Exposure and Environmental Control

### Complete the Assessment

The patient should be adequately exposed to identify injuries that may otherwise remain hidden.

This may include examination of:

- the anterior and posterior body;
- axillae;
- perineum;
- limbs;
- and other areas that may be concealed by clothing.

### Prevent Heat Loss

Exposure must be balanced against the risk of hypothermia.

The team should actively minimise unnecessary heat loss through:

- appropriate environmental control;
- warming measures;
- warmed fluids or blood products where appropriate;
- and prompt re-covering once examination is complete.

Hypothermia is particularly important in patients with major haemorrhage and severe trauma.

## Clinical Pearl

> **The primary survey is not a race through A to E. It is a structured method for identifying the problems most likely to kill the patient and treating them before they become irreversible.**


## Role of the Trauma Team Leader During <C>ABCDE

The Trauma Team Leader is responsible for maintaining the overall clinical picture while individual team members perform focused assessments and interventions.

The leader should:

- establish the immediate priorities;
- allocate critical tasks to identifiable team members;
- ensure that life-threatening problems are treated without unnecessary delay;
- monitor the patient's response to intervention;
- maintain situational awareness;
- anticipate deterioration;
- reprioritise when the patient's condition changes;
- and begin planning definitive management early.

The leader should avoid becoming absorbed in a single procedure or diagnostic problem.

The primary survey is a team process, and the leader's value lies in coordinating the process rather than personally performing every component.

### Maintain a Shared Mental Model

As the assessment progresses, the leader should periodically summarise the situation for the team.

A useful update may include:

- the major suspected threats;
- current physiological status;
- interventions already performed;
- response to treatment;
- unresolved problems;
- and the next intended action.

For example:

> **"We have ongoing respiratory and circulatory compromise. The airway is currently maintained. We are treating the chest problem, reassessing the response, and preparing for possible definitive haemorrhage control."**

A shared mental model reduces ambiguity and helps the team understand both the current priorities and the anticipated next steps.

### Reassess After Major Interventions

Every major intervention should be followed by an assessment of its effect.

The leader should ask:

> **"What did we expect to happen, and did it happen?"**

If the response differs from expectation, the team should reconsider:

- whether the original diagnosis was correct;
- whether the intervention was adequate;
- whether the intervention has introduced a new problem;
- and whether another life-threatening condition is present.

## Reprioritisation During the Primary Survey

The order of priorities may change during resuscitation.

For example, a patient initially requiring urgent attention to breathing may subsequently develop evidence of major haemorrhage.

Similarly, a patient with an apparently adequate airway may deteriorate as consciousness falls or facial swelling progresses.

The leader should therefore continuously integrate:

**Current findings → Intervention → Response → New trajectory**

A change in trajectory should trigger reassessment rather than continuation of the original plan.

## Common Errors During the Primary Survey

### Treating the Primary Survey as a Rigid Checklist

A structured approach is valuable, but rigid progression through A to E can become dangerous if the patient's condition changes.

The primary survey should remain dynamic.

### Completing Assessment Before Treating

A life-threatening problem should not be left untreated simply because the team has not yet completed the entire primary survey.

Assessment and treatment should occur together.

### Focusing on a Single Abnormality

One dramatic injury or abnormal observation should not obscure other potentially fatal problems.

The leader should maintain a broad view of the patient.

### Over-reliance on a Single Physiological Measurement

A single blood pressure, oxygen saturation, or neurological assessment may provide an incomplete picture.

Trends and response to intervention are often more informative.

### Failure to Reassess

An intervention is not the endpoint.

Failure to reassess may result in continued treatment of a problem that has not actually been corrected.

### Failure to Anticipate Deterioration

The team should prepare for foreseeable deterioration rather than waiting for physiological collapse.

### Poor Task Ownership

Important tasks should have identifiable owners.

A general instruction to the room may result in nobody assuming responsibility.

### Allowing Diagnostic Processes to Delay Definitive Care

Investigations should support clinical decision-making rather than become an endpoint.

When the patient requires definitive intervention, the team should move towards it without unnecessary delay.

## Case-Based Discussion

### Scenario

A 41-year-old man is brought to the resuscitation room after a high-speed collision.

He has significant vehicle intrusion and required prolonged extrication.

The pre-hospital team reports:

- increasing respiratory effort;
- persistent tachycardia;
- transient hypotension;
- and concern for major torso trauma.

On arrival:

- respiratory rate is 34/min;
- oxygen saturation is 91% despite supplemental oxygen;
- heart rate is 138/min;
- systolic blood pressure is 88 mmHg;
- Glasgow Coma Scale is 14/15.

The patient is speaking in short sentences.

The team begins the primary survey.

### Question 1 — What should the Trauma Team Leader do first?

The leader should establish whether there is catastrophic haemorrhage or another immediately life-threatening problem while coordinating the <C>ABCDE assessment.

The leader should avoid waiting for the entire primary survey to be completed before treating an identified threat.

### Question 2 — Does the patient's blood pressure confirm or exclude major haemorrhage?

No.

The blood pressure must be interpreted in the context of the mechanism, heart rate, peripheral perfusion, suspected injury pattern, and trajectory.

A relatively preserved or moderately reduced blood pressure does not by itself exclude major haemorrhage.

### Question 3 — What should happen if the patient deteriorates during the assessment?

The team should pause long enough to establish what has changed.

The leader should reassess the patient, reconsider the priority order, and determine whether a previously occult life-threatening problem has become clinically apparent.

The team should not continue mechanically through the original sequence while the patient's physiology is deteriorating.

### Question 4 — What should happen after a major intervention?

The response should be reassessed.

The leader should determine whether the patient improved as expected and whether the working diagnosis remains appropriate.

Failure to improve should trigger reconsideration of the diagnosis, the intervention, and the possibility of another concurrent life-threatening problem.

### Leadership Point

The primary survey demonstrates that trauma leadership is not simply about knowing the sequence of clinical assessments.

The leader must continuously coordinate:

**Recognise → Prioritise → Delegate → Intervene → Reassess → Reprioritise → Anticipate**

The sequence provides structure, but the patient's physiology determines what matters most at any given moment.

## Key Takeaways

1. **The primary survey is a structured framework for identifying and treating immediately life-threatening problems.**
2. **Catastrophic haemorrhage should be considered before progressing through the traditional ABC sequence.**
3. **Assessment and treatment should occur simultaneously when a life-threatening problem is identified.**
4. **The primary survey is dynamic and should be repeated when the patient's condition changes.**
5. **A single physiological measurement should never be interpreted in isolation.**
6. **Response to treatment is important diagnostic information.**
7. **Parallel processing should increase the speed of care without creating loss of task ownership or situational awareness.**
8. **The Trauma Team Leader should coordinate rather than personally perform every task.**
9. **Major interventions should be followed by reassessment of their effect.**
10. **Failure to improve should trigger reconsideration of the diagnosis and overall strategy.**
11. **The leader should anticipate deterioration and prepare resources before they become urgently required.**
12. **The primary survey should lead efficiently towards definitive treatment when life-threatening pathology requires it.**
13. **The practical mental model is: Recognise → Prioritise → Delegate → Intervene → Reassess → Reprioritise → Anticipate → Definitive Care.**
