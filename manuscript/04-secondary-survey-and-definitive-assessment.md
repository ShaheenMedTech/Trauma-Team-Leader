# Chapter 4 — Secondary Survey and Definitive Assessment

## Clinical Scenario

A 36-year-old patient has completed the primary survey following a high-speed road traffic collision.

The airway is currently maintained. Breathing has improved following treatment, and circulation is responding to initial resuscitation.

The patient's immediate life-threatening problems have been identified and addressed, but the mechanism and initial findings suggest significant multisystem trauma.

The Trauma Team Leader must now decide whether the patient is stable enough for a secondary survey and further investigation, while ensuring that ongoing threats are not overlooked.

The secondary survey should therefore not be regarded as a routine checklist performed simply because the primary survey is complete.

It is a structured search for additional injuries performed within the limits imposed by the patient's physiology and the need for definitive treatment.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain the purpose of the secondary survey.
2. Recognise when the patient's physiology permits a more detailed assessment.
3. Perform a structured head-to-toe assessment without losing situational awareness.
4. Integrate history, mechanism, examination, and investigations.
5. Recognise injuries that may remain occult during the primary survey.
6. Avoid allowing secondary assessment or investigation to delay definitive treatment.
7. Reassess the patient when new information or physiological deterioration occurs.
8. Determine when the patient should proceed to CT, theatre, interventional radiology, critical care, or transfer.

## Key Concepts

### Secondary Survey Is Not a Substitute for Resuscitation

The secondary survey begins only after immediate life-threatening problems have been identified and addressed sufficiently to allow further assessment.

If the patient's physiology deteriorates, the team should return immediately to the primary survey and reassess for life-threatening pathology.

### Stability Determines What Can Be Done Next

The leader should continually assess whether the patient is stable enough to proceed with further examination or investigation.

The decision should consider:

- current physiology;
- response to resuscitation;
- suspected injury pattern;
- ongoing haemorrhage;
- airway and respiratory stability;
- neurological status;
- and the likely benefit of further investigation.

The important question is:

> **"Is this patient stable enough for the next step?"**

### Structured Head-to-Toe Assessment

The secondary survey should provide a systematic assessment for injuries that may not have been identified during the primary survey.

This includes consideration of:

- head and face;
- neck;
- chest;
- abdomen;
- pelvis;
- perineum;
- back;
- limbs;
- and neurological status.

The examination should remain focused on findings that may alter immediate management.

### AMPLE History

When information is available, the team should obtain a focused trauma history, including:

- Allergies;
- Medications;
- Past medical history and pregnancy where relevant;
- Last meal;
- Events and environment surrounding the injury.

Information from the patient, relatives, pre-hospital clinicians, witnesses, and available records may all contribute to the assessment.

### Mechanism Matters

The mechanism of injury should continue to influence the search for occult injury.

The leader should integrate:

**Mechanism → Injury pattern → Examination → Physiology → Investigation**

The absence of an obvious injury does not necessarily exclude significant underlying pathology.

### Investigations Should Answer Clinical Questions

Investigations should be selected according to the patient's clinical condition and the questions that need to be answered.

The leader should avoid ordering investigations simply because they are routinely available.

The key question is:

> **"What decision will this investigation help us make?"**

### Do Not Let Investigation Become the Endpoint

A patient may require CT, operative management, interventional radiology, critical care, or transfer.

The investigation should support that pathway rather than delay it.

If the patient requires immediate definitive intervention, the team should not allow completion of a diagnostic sequence to become a substitute for treatment.

## Clinical Pearl

> **The secondary survey expands the search for injury; it does not replace repeated assessment of the patient's immediate threats or delay definitive treatment.**


## Practical Approach

### Reassess Before Starting

The completion of the primary survey does not automatically mean that the patient is ready for a secondary survey.

Before proceeding, the Trauma Team Leader should establish that the immediate life-threatening problems have been identified and addressed sufficiently to permit further assessment.

The leader should briefly reassess:

- airway patency and ability to maintain it;
- respiratory effort and oxygenation;
- haemodynamic status and perfusion;
- neurological status;
- ongoing or suspected haemorrhage;
- response to interventions already performed;
- and the overall trajectory of the patient.

If the patient's physiology has deteriorated, the team should return immediately to the primary survey.

The secondary survey should never become a reason to continue with a planned sequence when the patient's clinical condition has changed.

### Establish Whether the Patient Is Stable Enough

The decision to proceed with detailed examination and investigation is a clinical decision rather than a procedural milestone.

The leader should consider whether the patient is:

- physiologically stable;
- responding appropriately to resuscitation;
- at risk of ongoing haemorrhage;
- maintaining adequate airway and ventilation;
- neurologically stable;
- and likely to tolerate the next stage of assessment.

A patient who remains unstable may require further resuscitation and definitive treatment rather than a prolonged secondary survey.

A patient who is sufficiently stable can undergo a more detailed assessment while the team continues to monitor for deterioration.

The practical question is:

> **"Is the patient stable enough for the next step?"**

### Perform a Structured Head-to-Toe Assessment

The secondary survey should follow a consistent structure so that important injuries are less likely to be overlooked.

The examination should generally progress through:

1. head and face;
2. neck;
3. chest;
4. abdomen;
5. pelvis and perineum;
6. back;
7. limbs;
8. neurological reassessment.

The examination should be systematic but clinically focused.

The leader should avoid allowing the team to become distracted by minor injuries while potentially significant injuries remain unresolved.

Findings should be interpreted in the context of:

**Mechanism → Injury pattern → Examination → Physiology**

An apparently minor examination finding may become important when considered alongside the mechanism or physiological trajectory.

### Obtain a Focused Trauma History

The secondary survey provides an opportunity to obtain additional information that may change the diagnostic and management pathway.

The team should obtain an AMPLE history when possible:

- **A — Allergies**
- **M — Medications**
- **P — Past medical history and pregnancy where relevant**
- **L — Last meal**
- **E — Events and environment surrounding the injury**

The history may come from several sources.

These may include:

- the patient;
- relatives;
- witnesses;
- pre-hospital clinicians;
- police or other first responders where relevant;
- and available medical records.

The Trauma Team Leader should recognise that information obtained later may change the interpretation of findings already identified.

### Review Pre-Hospital Information

The pre-hospital phase can provide important information about the patient's trajectory before arrival.

The leader should establish, where available:

- mechanism and energy of injury;
- initial physiological observations;
- changes in consciousness;
- suspected injuries;
- interventions performed;
- medications administered;
- fluids or blood products given;
- response to treatment;
- and any deterioration or improvement during transport.

The patient's condition on arrival should therefore be interpreted as part of a trajectory rather than as an isolated set of observations.

A patient whose vital signs appear acceptable after significant pre-hospital intervention may still have serious ongoing pathology.

### Examine for Occult Injury

The purpose of the secondary survey is not simply to identify visible injuries.

The team should actively consider injuries that may initially be clinically subtle.

This is particularly important when there is:

- a high-energy mechanism;
- distracting injury;
- altered consciousness;
- intoxication or impaired communication;
- multiple injuries;
- significant pain;
- or an unreliable examination.

The absence of an obvious external injury should not be interpreted as evidence that significant internal injury is absent.

The leader should continually compare the examination with the mechanism and physiological findings.

Investigations should be selected according to the clinical question they are intended to answer.

The investigation should then be interpreted in the context of the patient's physiology, examination, mechanism, and response to treatment.

Investigations may include, according to the clinical situation and local trauma pathway:

- blood tests;
- blood gas analysis;
- ECG;
- focused ultrasound;
- plain radiography;
- CT;
- and other targeted investigations.

The precise investigation strategy should reflect the patient's condition and the resources available within the trauma system.

### Decide Whether Imaging Can Be Performed Safely

The need for imaging does not override the patient's physiological needs.

Before transferring a patient to imaging, the leader should consider:

- airway security;
- respiratory stability;
- haemodynamic stability;
- ongoing haemorrhage;
- monitoring requirements;
- availability of appropriately skilled personnel;
- and the ability to manage deterioration outside the resuscitation room.

A patient who is too unstable to tolerate transfer should not be treated as an imaging problem.

The leader should instead consider whether further resuscitation or immediate definitive intervention is required.

### Reassess After New Information

The secondary survey is an iterative process.

New examination findings, investigation results, or changes in physiology may alter the working diagnosis and priority order.

The leader should therefore repeatedly ask:

- What have we learned?
- Does this change the working diagnosis?
- Is the patient still stable?
- Has a new life-threatening problem been identified?
- Does the patient still need further investigation?
- Should definitive treatment now take priority?

The team should return to the primary survey whenever the patient's condition deteriorates.

### Transition Towards Definitive Management

The secondary survey should ultimately lead to a management decision.

Depending on the patient's injuries and physiology, the next destination may be:

- operating theatre;
- interventional radiology;
- critical care;
- specialist treatment;
- continued observation and reassessment;
- or transfer to another trauma facility.

The leader should avoid allowing the secondary survey or diagnostic process to continue indefinitely.

Once sufficient information is available to determine the next appropriate pathway, the team should act on that information.

The key transition is:

> **Assessment → Diagnosis → Decision → Definitive Management**

The purpose of the secondary survey is therefore not to achieve diagnostic completeness at all costs.

Its purpose is to identify additional clinically important injuries and obtain the information necessary to make the safest next decision.

### Maintain Situational Awareness

During the secondary survey, the Trauma Team Leader must continue to monitor the patient rather than becoming absorbed in the examination.

The leader should maintain awareness of:

- current physiology;
- interventions being performed;
- response to treatment;
- pending investigations;
- unresolved injuries;
- available resources;
- and the anticipated definitive destination.

A useful mental model is:

> **"What do we know, what remains uncertain, and what decision do we need to make next?"**

This helps prevent the secondary survey from becoming a task-completion exercise.

### Practical Mental Model

The secondary survey can be summarised as:

> **Reassess → Confirm Stability → Examine → Obtain History → Investigate → Interpret → Reassess → Decide → Definitive Care**

This is not a rigid sequence.

The process should be interrupted whenever the patient's physiology deteriorates or a new immediate threat is identified.

### Head and Face

The team should assess for:

- scalp injury;
- facial fractures;
- bleeding;
- facial swelling;
- ocular injury;
- oral injury;
- and signs of significant head trauma.

The leader should consider whether facial injuries may affect the airway now or later.

### Neck

The neck should be assessed for:

- tenderness;
- deformity;
- penetrating injury;
- swelling;
- vascular injury;
- tracheal abnormalities;
- and other findings suggesting significant cervical trauma.

Cervical spine precautions should remain appropriate to the patient's mechanism, examination, and local trauma pathway.

### Chest

The chest should be reassessed after the initial breathing assessment.

The team should look for:

- chest wall tenderness;
- deformity;
- bruising;
- penetrating injury;
- subcutaneous emphysema;
- and other evidence of thoracic injury.

The leader should remain alert to deterioration despite apparently reassuring initial findings.

### Abdomen and Pelvis

The abdomen should be assessed for:

- tenderness;
- guarding;
- distension;
- bruising;
- penetrating injury;
- and other evidence of abdominal trauma.

The pelvis should be assessed in accordance with the local trauma protocol.

The absence of marked abdominal tenderness does not reliably exclude significant intra-abdominal injury.

The leader should therefore integrate examination findings with mechanism, physiology, and investigation.

### Perineum and Back

The perineum should be inspected when clinically appropriate for:

- bruising;
- bleeding;
- soft-tissue injury;
- and other evidence of pelvic or genitourinary trauma.

The back should be examined systematically while maintaining appropriate spinal precautions.

The team should look for:

- wounds;
- bruising;
- tenderness;
- deformity;
- and other injuries that may not be visible from the front.

### Limbs

Each limb should be assessed for:

- deformity;
- wounds;
- swelling;
- tenderness;
- abnormal movement;
- distal perfusion;
- and neurological function.

The assessment should identify injuries that require urgent intervention while avoiding unnecessary delay to definitive management of more serious injuries.

### Neurological Reassessment

Neurological status should be reassessed during the secondary survey.

The leader should consider:

- level of consciousness;
- pupils where relevant;
- limb movement;
- sensation where appropriate;
- and changes from the previous assessment.

A deterioration in neurological status should trigger immediate reassessment rather than simply being documented as another secondary-survey finding.

### Review the Response to Resuscitation

The response to treatment is an important part of the secondary assessment.

The leader should review:

- haemodynamic trends;
- respiratory status;
- neurological status;
- urine output where relevant;
- temperature;
- blood results;
- and response to blood products or other major interventions.

Failure to improve should prompt reconsideration of the working diagnosis and the possibility of ongoing occult injury.

### Integrate Examination With Investigation

Investigations should be selected to answer specific clinical questions.

Depending on the patient's condition and suspected injuries, this may include:

- laboratory investigations;
- focused bedside imaging;
- radiography;
- CT;
- or other targeted investigations.

The investigation should be interpreted within the clinical context rather than in isolation.

A normal or reassuring investigation does not necessarily eliminate concern when the clinical picture remains inconsistent.

### Decide the Next Destination

The secondary survey should help the team determine the next stage of management.

Possible destinations include:

- CT;
- operating theatre;
- interventional radiology;
- critical care;
- specialist management;
- or transfer to another trauma facility.

The decision should be based on the patient's physiology, injury pattern, response to treatment, investigation findings, available resources, and local trauma pathways.

The key question remains:

> **"What does this patient need next, and is it safe to take them there?"**

### Reassess Before Leaving the Resuscitation Room

Before transfer to another area, the leader should confirm that the patient's current condition is adequately understood and that the receiving team and destination are prepared.

The team should communicate:

- major injuries identified;
- current physiological status;
- interventions performed;
- treatment still in progress;
- unresolved concerns;
- and the anticipated next steps.

Transfer should not create a period in which responsibility becomes unclear.
