# Chapter 20 — Pregnancy and Trauma

## Clinical Scenario

A 29-year-old woman at approximately 30 weeks' gestation is brought to the trauma centre following a high-speed motor vehicle collision.

She was a restrained passenger and sustained significant blunt abdominal and thoracic trauma.

On arrival, she is conscious but distressed.

Her airway is patent.

She is tachycardic and mildly hypotensive.

There is abdominal tenderness and bruising across the lower abdomen consistent with a seatbelt injury.

The fetal status is not yet known.

The Trauma Team Leader must coordinate simultaneous maternal resuscitation, injury assessment, obstetric evaluation, and fetal assessment.

The central principle is straightforward:

> **Treat the pregnant patient first, while recognising that there are two patients whose outcomes are closely linked.**

The fetus depends almost entirely on maternal oxygenation, ventilation, circulation, and perfusion.

Therefore, deterioration in maternal physiology is an immediate threat to fetal survival.

Pregnancy does not change the fundamental priorities of trauma care.

It changes the context in which those priorities must be applied.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Recognise the physiological and anatomical changes that affect trauma assessment during pregnancy.
2. Apply standard trauma principles to the pregnant patient.
3. Prioritise maternal resuscitation while maintaining awareness of fetal wellbeing.
4. Recognise mechanisms and patterns of injury that may threaten both mother and fetus.
5. Identify maternal shock despite apparently modest external findings.
6. Understand the implications of uterine enlargement for abdominal and thoracic trauma.
7. Coordinate maternal and fetal assessment without delaying life-saving treatment.
8. Select appropriate investigations when pregnancy is present.
9. Recognise placental abruption and other pregnancy-specific complications after trauma.
10. Understand the role of fetal monitoring after significant trauma.
11. Recognise when obstetric, neonatal, anaesthetic, surgical, radiology, and critical care input is required.
12. Understand the principles of resuscitative hysterotomy in maternal traumatic cardiac arrest.
13. Avoid common errors caused by overestimating the risks of diagnostic imaging or underestimating maternal injury.
14. Lead the trauma team when maternal and fetal priorities appear to compete.

---

## Key Concept

> **There is one resuscitation with two patients.**

The pregnant patient should not be managed as though maternal and fetal care are two independent pathways.

Maternal oxygenation, ventilation, perfusion, haemorrhage control, and definitive treatment remain the immediate priorities.

At the same time, the team must recognise pregnancy-specific threats to the fetus, including:

- maternal hypoxia;
- maternal hypotension;
- placental abruption;
- uterine injury;
- fetomaternal haemorrhage;
- premature labour;
- premature rupture of membranes;
- direct fetal injury;
- and maternal cardiac arrest.

The fetal condition therefore provides additional information about the mother's physiological state, but fetal assessment must not distract the team from maternal resuscitation.

---

# Physiological Changes Relevant to Trauma

Pregnancy produces major physiological adaptations.

These changes may be protective in some circumstances but can also make serious maternal deterioration difficult to recognise.

The Trauma Team Leader should understand these changes rather than relying exclusively on conventional normal ranges.

---

## Blood Volume

Maternal plasma volume increases substantially during pregnancy.

Red-cell mass also increases, although proportionally less than plasma volume.

This produces a physiological reduction in haemoglobin concentration.

The increased circulating volume provides some reserve against blood loss.

However, this reserve can create a dangerous misconception.

A pregnant patient may lose a significant amount of blood before obvious hypotension develops.

Therefore:

> **A normal blood pressure does not exclude significant haemorrhage in pregnancy.**

The team should interpret:

- heart rate;
- blood pressure;
- mental status;
- peripheral perfusion;
- urine output;
- lactate;
- acid-base status;
- haemoglobin trend;
- and response to resuscitation

as a combined picture rather than relying on a single parameter.

---

## Cardiovascular Changes

Cardiac output increases during pregnancy.

Heart rate is normally elevated compared with the non-pregnant state.

Systemic vascular resistance decreases.

The enlarged uterus can also affect venous return, particularly when the patient is supine later in pregnancy.

These changes have several implications.

A pregnant patient may tolerate some blood loss without immediate hypotension.

However, once compensatory mechanisms fail, deterioration may be rapid.

The Trauma Team Leader should therefore treat unexplained tachycardia seriously, particularly when accompanied by:

- abdominal pain;
- vaginal bleeding;
- uterine tenderness;
- altered mental status;
- cool peripheries;
- delayed capillary refill;
- metabolic acidosis;
- or evidence of traumatic injury.

---

## Respiratory Changes

Pregnancy increases oxygen consumption.

Functional residual capacity decreases.

Oxygen reserve is therefore reduced.

A pregnant patient may desaturate rapidly during:

- apnoea;
- airway obstruction;
- hypoventilation;
- difficult intubation;
- or prolonged airway manipulation.

This reinforces the importance of early oxygenation and efficient airway management.

The airway chapter should therefore be applied without unnecessary delay.

Pregnancy is not a reason to postpone definitive airway management when it is clinically indicated.

---

## Gastrointestinal and Anatomical Changes

The enlarging uterus displaces abdominal organs.

The diaphragm is elevated.

The stomach is displaced and intra-abdominal pressure is increased.

These changes may affect:

- examination findings;
- gastric emptying;
- aspiration risk;
- diaphragmatic position;
- abdominal imaging;
- and the anatomical pattern of injury.

The uterus becomes increasingly vulnerable to direct trauma as pregnancy progresses.

---

# Positioning and Aortocaval Compression

In later pregnancy, the enlarged uterus can compress the inferior vena cava and reduce venous return when the patient is supine.

This can worsen maternal hypotension.

The team should therefore avoid unnecessary prolonged flat supine positioning in a significantly pregnant patient when this compromises circulation.

Possible strategies include:

- manual left uterine displacement;
- appropriate leftward tilt when compatible with trauma management;
- or other positioning strategies that maintain spinal precautions and facilitate resuscitation.

The important principle is not a particular angle.

The principle is:

> **Relieve aortocaval compression without compromising trauma resuscitation.**

If spinal precautions or other injuries require the patient to remain supine, manual uterine displacement may be preferable.

The Trauma Team Leader should explicitly assign this task.

---

# Maternal Resuscitation Comes First

The first priority remains the same:

> **Identify and treat immediate threats to the mother.**

The primary survey should follow the established trauma sequence.

### Airway

Assess and secure the airway when indicated.

Anticipate:

- rapid desaturation;
- aspiration risk;
- airway oedema;
- and difficult laryngoscopy in some patients.

Do not delay a necessary airway intervention because of concern about the fetus.

---

### Breathing

Assess:

- oxygenation;
- ventilation;
- chest movement;
- breath sounds;
- thoracic injuries;
- and evidence of tension pneumothorax or other immediately life-threatening pathology.

Treat life-threatening chest injury according to standard trauma principles.

---

### Circulation

Assume significant haemorrhage when the mechanism and findings support it.

Look for bleeding from:

- external wounds;
- chest;
- abdomen;
- pelvis;
- long bones;
- retroperitoneum;
- and the uterus or genital tract.

Pregnancy does not protect against major traumatic haemorrhage.

In some situations, the source of blood loss may not be immediately obvious.

---

### Disability

Assess:

- GCS;
- pupils;
- glucose;
- neurological deficits;
- seizures;
- and evidence of traumatic brain injury.

Do not attribute altered mental status to pregnancy.

---

### Exposure

Fully expose the patient while actively preventing hypothermia.

Inspect for:

- abdominal trauma;
- seatbelt marks;
- vaginal bleeding;
- perineal injury;
- pelvic injury;
- penetrating trauma;
- and other external signs.

Maintain dignity and minimise unnecessary exposure.

---

# The Seatbelt Sign

A visible abdominal seatbelt mark after significant blunt trauma should increase suspicion for internal injury.

Potential associated injuries include:

- abdominal wall injury;
- bowel injury;
- mesenteric injury;
- uterine injury;
- placental injury;
- and spinal injury.

The absence of severe abdominal pain does not reliably exclude significant injury.

The Trauma Team Leader should therefore consider the mechanism, physical findings, physiological status, and imaging together.

---

# Shock in Pregnancy

Shock should be treated aggressively.

The apparent blood pressure may underestimate the severity of blood loss.

Important warning signs include:

- persistent tachycardia;
- altered mental status;
- cool extremities;
- prolonged capillary refill;
- oliguria;
- rising lactate;
- metabolic acidosis;
- and poor response to initial resuscitation.

The team should not wait for profound hypotension before activating a major haemorrhage response when the clinical picture indicates significant bleeding.

---

# Haemorrhage Control

The principles of haemorrhage control remain unchanged.

The team should rapidly identify and control bleeding.

Potential interventions include:

- direct pressure;
- tourniquet where indicated;
- pelvic stabilisation when appropriate;
- blood product resuscitation;
- operative haemorrhage control;
- interventional radiology;
- and definitive management of solid-organ or vascular injury.

The presence of pregnancy should not delay life-saving haemorrhage control.

---

# Blood Products

Significant traumatic haemorrhage should be managed using an institutional major haemorrhage protocol.

The exact composition of blood component therapy should follow local trauma and transfusion protocols.

The team should consider:

- red blood cells;
- plasma;
- platelets;
- fibrinogen replacement where indicated;
- calcium;
- temperature;
- coagulation status;
- and correction of metabolic derangements.

The goal is restoration of effective maternal circulation and haemostasis.

---

# Pelvic Trauma

Pelvic trauma in pregnancy presents a particular challenge.

The enlarged uterus occupies a large portion of the abdominal and pelvic cavity.

Pelvic fractures can cause major haemorrhage.

The team should consider:

- pelvic instability;
- retroperitoneal haemorrhage;
- uterine injury;
- placental injury;
- bladder injury;
- urethral injury;
- and associated abdominal trauma.

Pelvic examination should be clinically appropriate and should not be repeated unnecessarily.

A pelvic binder may be appropriate for suspected unstable pelvic haemorrhage according to local trauma protocols.

Its application should account for the pregnancy and uterine position while maintaining effective pelvic stabilisation.

---

# Abdominal Trauma

Abdominal trauma may cause:

- solid-organ injury;
- bowel injury;
- mesenteric injury;
- retroperitoneal haemorrhage;
- uterine injury;
- placental injury;
- and fetomaternal haemorrhage.

The physical examination can be difficult to interpret.

The enlarged uterus changes abdominal anatomy.

Pain and tenderness may also be attributed incorrectly to pregnancy.

The leader should therefore maintain a low threshold for appropriate imaging when significant injury is suspected.

---

# Placental Abruption

Placental abruption is one of the most important pregnancy-specific complications following trauma.

Traumatic shearing forces can separate the placenta from the uterine wall.

This may compromise fetal oxygenation and can cause maternal haemorrhage.

The classic presentation may include:

- vaginal bleeding;
- abdominal pain;
- uterine tenderness;
- uterine contractions;
- and fetal heart-rate abnormalities.

However:

> **The absence of vaginal bleeding does not exclude placental abruption.**

Bleeding may be concealed behind the placenta.

Therefore, the Trauma Team Leader should consider abruption whenever significant trauma occurs during pregnancy, particularly with:

- abdominal trauma;
- uterine tenderness;
- contractions;
- vaginal bleeding;
- fetal distress;
- or unexplained maternal deterioration.

---

# Uterine Rupture

Uterine rupture is uncommon but potentially catastrophic.

Risk may be influenced by:

- gestational age;
- previous uterine surgery;
- direct abdominal trauma;
- and the magnitude and direction of force.

Possible findings include:

- severe abdominal pain;
- haemodynamic instability;
- abnormal fetal heart rate;
- loss of uterine contour;
- vaginal bleeding;
- or evidence of intra-abdominal haemorrhage.

The diagnosis may be difficult in the severely injured patient.

When suspected, urgent multidisciplinary management is required.

---

# Fetomaternal Haemorrhage

Trauma can disrupt the maternal-fetal interface and allow fetal red cells to enter the maternal circulation.

This is clinically important because fetomaternal haemorrhage can contribute to fetal compromise and may cause RhD alloimmunisation in an RhD-negative patient.

The Trauma Team Leader should ensure that the appropriate laboratory and obstetric pathways are activated.

Current trauma guidance recommends consideration of Kleihauer-Betke testing in Rh-negative patients with suspected abdominal trauma to help guide RhD immunoglobulin dosing according to local obstetric protocols.

---

# Fetal Assessment

Fetal assessment should occur early once a rapid maternal assessment and initial stabilisation have been achieved.

The 2025 EAST systematic review and guideline suggests fetal assessment at the end of the primary survey after rapid maternal evaluation and stabilisation in trauma patients with viable pregnancies. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

The exact approach depends on:

- gestational age;
- maternal condition;
- fetal viability;
- mechanism;
- suspected injury;
- local resources;
- and obstetric expertise.

Fetal assessment may include:

- fetal heart-rate assessment;
- cardiotocography when appropriate;
- ultrasound;
- assessment of uterine activity;
- and obstetric examination.

The key principle is:

> **Fetal assessment complements maternal resuscitation; it does not replace it.**

---

# Fetal Heart-Rate Monitoring

In a viable pregnancy following significant trauma, continuous or prolonged cardiotocographic monitoring may be appropriate.

The 2025 EAST guideline conditionally recommends a formal observation period of at least 4–6 hours in trauma patients with viable pregnancies. Monitoring should be extended when concerning clinical or fetal findings are present. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

Concerning findings include:

- abnormal fetal heart-rate pattern;
- uterine contractions;
- vaginal bleeding;
- uterine tenderness;
- membrane rupture;
- significant maternal injury;
- or maternal physiological instability.

The exact duration should follow current local obstetric and trauma protocols.

---

# Ultrasound

Ultrasound can provide useful information about:

- fetal cardiac activity;
- fetal movement;
- fetal position;
- placental location;
- amniotic fluid;
- and selected maternal injuries.

However:

> **A normal ultrasound does not exclude placental abruption or significant maternal injury.**

Ultrasound should therefore be interpreted within the complete clinical picture.

It should not be used as a reason to avoid definitive imaging when maternal injury is suspected.

---

# Diagnostic Imaging

Pregnancy should not automatically prevent clinically indicated imaging.

This is a critical leadership principle.

If the mother may have a serious injury, failure to diagnose that injury can pose a greater risk to both mother and fetus than the radiation exposure associated with an appropriately performed diagnostic study.

ACOG states that medically indicated radiography and CT should not be withheld solely because of pregnancy when these examinations are necessary for diagnosis and management. :contentReference[oaicite:3]{index=3}

Therefore:

> **Do not allow fear of radiation to create a diagnostic delay that places the mother and fetus at greater risk.**

---

# Choosing Imaging

Imaging should answer a clinical question.

Consider:

- ultrasound;
- radiography;
- CT;
- MRI;
- angiography;
- and other modalities

according to the suspected injury.

The choice should be based on:

- diagnostic performance;
- availability;
- urgency;
- maternal physiology;
- mechanism;
- and the consequence of missing the diagnosis.

Ultrasound and MRI do not use ionising radiation, but they are not substitutes for CT when CT is the appropriate test for a potentially life-threatening traumatic injury.

---

# CT in Pregnancy

CT may be necessary in significant trauma.

The Trauma Team Leader should not delay indicated CT solely because the patient is pregnant.

The team should:

- define the clinical question;
- avoid unnecessary duplicated imaging;
- use appropriate protocols;
- minimise exposure consistent with diagnostic quality;
- and involve radiology when appropriate.

The decision should be framed as:

> **What is the safest way to diagnose the suspected injury?**

rather than:

> **Can we avoid CT because the patient is pregnant?**

---

# MRI

MRI may be useful when it can answer a relevant clinical question without delaying urgent treatment.

However, MRI may be impractical in unstable trauma patients because of:

- time;
- patient monitoring requirements;
- limited availability;
- and difficulty managing an unstable patient in the scanner environment.

The patient's physiology and the urgency of diagnosis remain decisive.

---

# Diagnostic Imaging: Leadership Principle

The Trauma Team Leader should actively prevent two opposite errors.

### Error 1: Over-imaging

Ordering investigations without a clinical question creates delay, radiation exposure, transport risk, and resource use.

### Error 2: Under-imaging

Avoiding clinically necessary imaging because of pregnancy may result in missed maternal injury.

The correct approach is:

> **Use the investigation that provides the information required to make the next important clinical decision.**

---

# Obstetric Consultation

Significant trauma during pregnancy should trigger early communication with obstetric services.

Depending on the patient, additional involvement may include:

- maternal-fetal medicine;
- neonatology;
- anaesthesia;
- general surgery;
- trauma surgery;
- orthopaedics;
- interventional radiology;
- intensive care;
- and blood bank services.

The Trauma Team Leader should identify these requirements early.

The presence of multiple specialties should not result in diffusion of responsibility.

One clinician must remain clearly responsible for overall trauma leadership.

---

# The Trauma Team Leader's Role

The Trauma Team Leader should establish:

1. Who is leading maternal resuscitation.
2. Who is responsible for airway management.
3. Who is responsible for circulation and haemorrhage control.
4. Who is obtaining fetal assessment.
5. Who is coordinating obstetric input.
6. What imaging is required.
7. What definitive treatment may be needed.
8. Where the patient should go next.

Possible destinations include:

- CT;
- operating theatre;
- interventional radiology;
- obstetric theatre;
- intensive care;
- high-dependency care;
- or transfer to a higher-level trauma centre.

---

# Maternal and Fetal Priorities

At times, maternal and fetal priorities may appear to conflict.

The leader should remember that maternal physiology remains the foundation of fetal survival.

Examples include:

- maternal airway compromise;
- maternal hypoxia;
- maternal haemorrhage;
- maternal shock;
- maternal cardiac arrest;
- and injuries requiring immediate operative management.

In each situation, stabilisation of the mother is the primary intervention for both patients.

---

# When Maternal Condition Is Unstable

If the patient remains unstable:

- continue primary survey;
- control haemorrhage;
- treat chest injury;
- secure the airway when required;
- provide blood products;
- correct reversible causes;
- and activate definitive intervention.

Do not delay life-saving treatment for:

- prolonged ultrasound;
- complete obstetric history;
- non-essential laboratory testing;
- or routine fetal assessment.

---

# Maternal Cardiac Arrest

Maternal cardiac arrest is a special circumstance.

The team should initiate standard high-quality adult resuscitation immediately.

Pregnancy-specific considerations include:

- relief of aortocaval compression;
- early recognition of reversible causes;
- rapid escalation to definitive resuscitation;
- and preparation for resuscitative hysterotomy when indicated.

The key concept is:

> **Resuscitative hysterotomy is primarily a maternal resuscitation procedure that may also provide fetal benefit.**

---

# Resuscitative Hysterotomy

Resuscitative hysterotomy should be considered in a pregnant patient with traumatic cardiac arrest when the gestational age is sufficient for the gravid uterus to contribute significantly to aortocaval compression.

The current EAST guideline conditionally recommends resuscitative hysterotomy as soon as possible in traumatic arrest with a viable pregnancy at approximately 20 weeks' gestation or greater. :contentReference[oaicite:4]{index=4}

The procedure should not be delayed while waiting for:

- fetal monitoring;
- ultrasound confirmation;
- transfer to another department;
- laboratory results;
- or a prolonged discussion.

The trauma team should recognise the need early and activate the appropriate personnel and equipment.

---

# The Four-Minute Concept

Historically, resuscitative hysterotomy has been taught using the concept of initiating the procedure within approximately 4 minutes of maternal arrest when indicated.

The purpose is not to create a rigid stopwatch-based rule.

The important principle is:

> **Once indicated, do not delay resuscitative hysterotomy.**

The 2025 EAST guideline emphasises performing the procedure as soon as possible in the appropriate clinical context. :contentReference[oaicite:5]{index=5}

Local protocols should define:

- gestational-age thresholds;
- equipment;
- personnel;
- incision technique;
- neonatal response;
- and location of emergency equipment.

---

# Maternal Cardiac Arrest: Team Organisation

The Trauma Team Leader should immediately assign roles.

For example:

### Team Leader

Coordinates the resuscitation and decision-making.

### Airway Lead

Manages oxygenation and airway.

### CPR Lead

Coordinates high-quality chest compressions.

### Circulation Lead

Manages vascular access, blood products, and reversible causes.

### Obstetric Lead

Prepares for and performs resuscitative hysterotomy when indicated.

### Neonatal Lead

Prepares for immediate neonatal resuscitation.

### Recorder

Documents:

- arrest time;
- interventions;
- medications;
- defibrillation;
- blood products;
- and timing of hysterotomy.

Clear role allocation prevents delay.

---

# Trauma in Pregnancy: Special Mechanisms

The Trauma Team Leader should consider:

- road traffic collisions;
- falls;
- assaults;
- penetrating trauma;
- burns;
- blast injury;
- domestic violence;
- occupational trauma;
- and iatrogenic injury.

Mechanism should guide the search for injury.

Pregnancy does not protect against standard trauma pathology.

---

# Road Traffic Collision

Important considerations include:

- seatbelt injury;
- airbag deployment;
- vehicle intrusion;
- ejection;
- rollover;
- occupant position;
- speed;
- direct abdominal impact;
- and associated fatalities at the scene.

The leader should obtain pre-hospital information whenever possible.

A apparently minor collision does not necessarily imply minor injury.

---

# Penetrating Trauma

Penetrating trauma requires standard trauma assessment with consideration of the altered anatomy of pregnancy.

Potential injuries include:

- maternal vascular injury;
- bowel injury;
- uterine injury;
- placental injury;
- fetal injury;
- and retroperitoneal injury.

The trajectory should be considered carefully.

The enlarged uterus may alter the organs at risk.

Management should be based on maternal physiology and suspected injury rather than pregnancy alone.

---

# Burns and Pregnancy

Burns in pregnancy require simultaneous maternal resuscitation and pregnancy-specific assessment.

Important threats include:

- airway injury;
- inhalational injury;
- hypovolaemia;
- carbon monoxide exposure;
- thermal injury;
- and fetal hypoxia.

The maternal airway and oxygenation remain the immediate priorities.

Carbon monoxide exposure is particularly important because fetal clearance may lag behind maternal clearance.

Appropriate oxygen therapy and definitive burn management should not be delayed.

---

# Trauma-Induced Preterm Labour

Trauma may precipitate uterine contractions and premature labour.

The patient should be assessed for:

- contractions;
- cervical change;
- vaginal bleeding;
- membrane rupture;
- and fetal compromise.

Management should be coordinated with obstetric services.

The trauma team should continue to focus on maternal injury while obstetric management proceeds in parallel.

---

# Vaginal Bleeding

Vaginal bleeding after trauma should never be dismissed.

Possible causes include:

- placental abruption;
- cervical injury;
- vaginal injury;
- uterine injury;
- labour;
- or other obstetric causes.

The amount of visible blood does not necessarily represent total blood loss.

Concealed bleeding remains possible.

---

# Abdominal Examination

The abdominal examination should assess:

- tenderness;
- guarding;
- rigidity;
- distension;
- uterine tenderness;
- seatbelt marks;
- penetrating wounds;
- and signs of internal haemorrhage.

Repeated examination may be useful because findings can evolve.

However, repeated examinations should be purposeful.

The team should not use serial examination as a reason to delay appropriate imaging or definitive treatment.

---

# Reassessment

Pregnant trauma patients require repeated assessment.

Reassessment should include:

- airway;
- breathing;
- circulation;
- neurological status;
- pain;
- abdominal findings;
- uterine activity;
- vaginal bleeding;
- fetal status;
- and response to treatment.

A previously reassuring assessment does not guarantee continued stability.

---

# Disposition

The destination should reflect the patient's actual clinical needs.

Potential destinations include:

- trauma operating theatre;
- obstetric operating theatre;
- interventional radiology;
- intensive care;
- obstetric unit;
- high-dependency care;
- ward observation;
- or transfer.

The decision should be made early.

The leader should avoid allowing the patient to remain in the emergency department simply because no single service has assumed responsibility.

---

# Transfer

Transfer may be required when the current facility lacks:

- trauma surgery;
- obstetric surgery;
- neonatal care;
- interventional radiology;
- intensive care;
- blood products;
- or appropriate imaging.

Transfer should occur after appropriate stabilisation when feasible.

However:

> **Do not delay necessary definitive treatment merely to achieve a more convenient transfer.**

The sending team should communicate:

- gestational age;
- maternal injuries;
- physiological status;
- blood products administered;
- fetal status;
- investigations;
- procedures performed;
- and anticipated interventions.

---

# Discharge After Minor Trauma

A pregnant patient who appears well after trauma may still require observation depending on:

- gestational age;
- mechanism;
- symptoms;
- uterine activity;
- fetal assessment;
- maternal injuries;
- and local protocol.

Before discharge, the team should ensure:

- maternal stability;
- appropriate fetal assessment;
- no concerning obstetric findings;
- appropriate observation where indicated;
- clear return precautions;
- and appropriate obstetric follow-up.

The threshold for reassessment should remain low if symptoms develop later.

---

# Common Cognitive Errors

## Error 1: Treating the Fetus Instead of the Mother

Fetal distress may trigger intense attention to the fetal monitor.

However, maternal hypoxia or hypotension may be the underlying cause.

**Correction:**

Return to maternal physiology.

---

## Error 2: Assuming Normal Blood Pressure Means No Major Haemorrhage

Pregnancy provides increased circulating volume.

**Correction:**

Interpret the entire physiological picture.

---

## Error 3: Avoiding CT Because of Pregnancy

This may lead to missed maternal injury.

**Correction:**

Use clinically indicated imaging.

---

## Error 4: Assuming No Vaginal Bleeding Means No Placental Abruption

Abruption can be concealed.

**Correction:**

Consider uterine tenderness, contractions, fetal abnormalities, and maternal physiology.

---

## Error 5: Allowing Obstetric Assessment to Delay Trauma Resuscitation

**Correction:**

Run maternal resuscitation and obstetric assessment in parallel.

---

## Error 6: Assuming the Fetal Heart Rate Is a Maternal Vital Sign

The fetal heart rate provides important information but does not replace maternal assessment.

**Correction:**

Treat maternal physiology independently.

---

## Error 7: Delaying Resuscitative Hysterotomy

Waiting for additional confirmation can cost valuable time.

**Correction:**

Recognise maternal arrest early and activate the appropriate pathway immediately.

---

## Error 8: Forgetting Aortocaval Compression

Prolonged supine positioning can worsen venous return in later pregnancy.

**Correction:**

Use appropriate uterine displacement while maintaining trauma priorities.

---

## Error 9: Focusing Only on the Abdomen

Pregnancy does not protect the patient from:

- traumatic brain injury;
- chest trauma;
- pelvic injury;
- limb trauma;
- vascular injury;
- or spinal injury.

**Correction:**

Complete the trauma assessment.

---

# Trauma Team Leader Checklist

When a pregnant trauma patient arrives, the leader should ask:

### Maternal

- Is the airway secure?
- Is oxygenation adequate?
- Is ventilation adequate?
- Is there major haemorrhage?
- Is the patient perfusing adequately?
- Is there a life-threatening chest injury?
- Is there traumatic brain injury?
- Is pelvic injury suspected?
- Is the patient deteriorating?

### Pregnancy

- What is the gestational age?
- Is the pregnancy viable?
- Is there vaginal bleeding?
- Is there abdominal or uterine tenderness?
- Are there contractions?
- Has the membrane ruptured?
- What is the fetal heart rate?
- Does the patient require cardiotocographic monitoring?
- Is obstetric consultation required?

### Investigation

- What injury am I trying to diagnose?
- Will the investigation change management?
- Is CT required?
- Could ultrasound or MRI answer the question?
- Am I delaying definitive treatment?

### Definitive pathway

- Does the patient require theatre?
- Interventional radiology?
- Intensive care?
- Obstetric theatre?
- Neonatal support?
- Transfer?

---

# Leadership Framework

The Trauma Team Leader should think in five simultaneous domains:

**1. Maternal physiology**

Is the mother oxygenated and perfused?

**2. Injury pattern**

What injuries could explain the mechanism and physiological abnormalities?

**3. Pregnancy-specific complications**

Could there be:

- abruption;
- uterine injury;
- fetomaternal haemorrhage;
- premature labour;
- or fetal compromise?

**4. Diagnostic strategy**

What investigation will answer the next important clinical question?

**5. Definitive treatment**

Where does the patient need to go next?

This can be summarised as:

> **Mother → Injury → Pregnancy → Investigation → Definitive treatment**

# Clinical Scenario: Revisited

The 29-year-old woman remains tachycardic with borderline blood pressure.

She has a prominent seatbelt mark and abdominal tenderness.

The Trauma Team Leader recognises that the mechanism and examination are concerning for significant intra-abdominal injury.

The patient is managed according to the trauma primary survey.

Large-bore intravenous access is established.

Blood is sent for appropriate investigations and crossmatching.

The major haemorrhage pathway is considered.

The patient receives appropriate resuscitation.

Obstetric services are alerted.

After rapid maternal assessment and initial stabilisation, fetal assessment is initiated.

The fetal heart rate is abnormal.

The team now faces two simultaneous questions:

> **What is causing the maternal physiological abnormality?**

and

> **Is the fetus being compromised by maternal injury or a pregnancy-specific complication?**

The leader does not allow the fetal monitor to become the centre of the resuscitation.

Maternal haemorrhage control remains the priority.

Appropriate imaging is obtained because the suspected maternal injury cannot be reliably excluded clinically.

The patient is subsequently transferred to definitive management.

The key leadership decision was not simply identifying that the patient was pregnant.

It was recognising that pregnancy changes the context of trauma without changing the fundamental hierarchy of resuscitation.

---

# Clinical Pearl

> **In the injured pregnant patient, the best initial treatment for the fetus is effective resuscitation of the mother.**

Pregnancy should trigger additional vigilance, not diagnostic paralysis.

The Trauma Team Leader must simultaneously protect:

- maternal oxygenation;
- maternal circulation;
- haemorrhage control;
- fetal wellbeing;
- and the pathway to definitive treatment.

The correct approach is not to choose between mother and fetus.

It is to optimise maternal survival while identifying and treating pregnancy-specific threats as early as possible.

---

# Key Takeaways

1. Pregnancy does not change the fundamental priorities of trauma resuscitation.
2. Maternal resuscitation is the first priority.
3. The fetus is highly dependent on maternal oxygenation and perfusion.
4. Physiological changes in pregnancy can mask early shock.
5. A normal blood pressure does not exclude major haemorrhage.
6. Later pregnancy increases the importance of avoiding aortocaval compression.
7. Significant abdominal trauma may cause placental abruption even without vaginal bleeding.
8. Fetal assessment should occur early after rapid maternal assessment and initial stabilisation.
9. Viable pregnancies following significant trauma may require formal maternal and fetal observation.
10. A normal ultrasound does not exclude placental abruption or significant maternal injury.
11. Clinically indicated CT should not be withheld solely because of pregnancy.
12. Imaging should answer a clinical question and should not delay definitive treatment.
13. Obstetric involvement should occur early in significant trauma.
14. Maternal haemorrhage should be managed using established trauma and transfusion principles.
15. RhD-negative patients with suspected abdominal trauma require appropriate assessment and RhD immunoglobulin management according to local obstetric protocols.
16. Maternal cardiac arrest requires immediate high-quality resuscitation and consideration of resuscitative hysterotomy when indicated.
17. Resuscitative hysterotomy should not be delayed once the appropriate clinical threshold is reached.
18. The Trauma Team Leader must maintain overall situational awareness.
19. Fetal monitoring should never replace maternal reassessment.
20. The endpoint of trauma assessment is definitive treatment, not completion of a checklist.

---

# Final Leadership Principle

> **Pregnancy creates two interdependent patients, but one immediate resuscitation priority: restore and maintain the mother's oxygenation, circulation, and survival.**

The Trauma Team Leader must recognise pregnancy-specific threats without allowing them to distract from standard trauma priorities.

The sequence remains:

**Recognise → Resuscitate → Assess → Investigate → Reassess → Definitively Treat**

with maternal and fetal assessment integrated throughout.

The successful trauma team does not manage the mother and fetus as competing priorities.

It recognises that, in most traumatic emergencies:

> **Saving the mother is the most immediate and effective way to save the fetus.**
