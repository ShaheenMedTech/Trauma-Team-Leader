# Chapter 22 — Communication and Closed-Loop Communication

## Clinical Scenario

A 34-year-old man arrives in the resuscitation room following a high-speed road traffic collision.

The pre-hospital team reports:

- Significant frontal vehicle damage
- Prolonged extrication
- Suspected chest injury
- Ongoing external haemorrhage from the right thigh
- GCS 12 at scene
- Tachycardia and hypotension despite initial fluid administration

The trauma team is assembled.

The team leader says:

> "Let's start."

The airway clinician begins assessing the patient.

The circulation clinician is attempting intravenous access.

A nurse is preparing blood products.

The trauma leader is looking at the monitor and asks:

> "What's the blood pressure?"

The nurse replies:

> "It's 78 systolic."

The leader turns to another clinician:

> "Can you give some blood?"

The clinician responds:

> "Yes."

Thirty seconds later, nobody is certain whether blood has actually been requested from the blood bank, whether the massive haemorrhage protocol has been activated, or who is responsible for controlling the thigh haemorrhage.

The patient's condition deteriorates.

This is not primarily a knowledge failure.

It is a **communication failure**.

In major trauma, communication is a clinical intervention.

The quality of communication determines whether the team understands:

- What is happening
- What matters most
- What has already been done
- What needs to happen next
- Who is responsible
- Whether an intervention has actually occurred
- Whether the patient is improving or deteriorating

A trauma team can contain highly skilled individuals and still perform poorly if information does not move reliably through the team.

The trauma team leader therefore has a responsibility not merely to communicate, but to **design communication**.

# Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain why communication is a core clinical skill in major trauma.
2. Describe the characteristics of effective trauma-team communication.
3. Use clear, concise and directed communication.
4. Apply closed-loop communication reliably.
5. Distinguish between giving an instruction and confirming completion.
6. Use call-outs to maintain shared situational awareness.
7. Use check-backs when information is uncertain or high-risk.
8. Structure important information using standardised communication techniques.
9. Recognise communication failures during trauma resuscitation.
10. Manage communication during periods of high cognitive load.
11. Escalate concerns when the team leader is wrong or unaware of deterioration.
12. Maintain psychological safety while preserving decisive leadership.
13. Integrate communication with prioritisation and delegation.
14. Use communication to maintain a shared mental model.
15. Conduct effective communication during transitions and handover.

# 1. Why Communication Matters in Trauma

Major trauma is a high-risk communication environment.

The patient may deteriorate rapidly.

Multiple interventions occur simultaneously.

Different clinicians possess different pieces of information.

The team may include:

- Emergency physicians
- Trauma surgeons
- Anaesthetists
- Orthopaedic surgeons
- Radiologists
- Nurses
- Operating department practitioners
- Paramedics
- Blood-bank personnel
- Radiographers
- Porters
- Other specialist teams

No single person sees everything.

The trauma team leader therefore operates within a distributed information system.

The leader must ensure that critical information is:

> **heard → understood → acted upon → confirmed → integrated into the next decision**

Failure at any point can produce error.

---

# 2. Communication Is a Clinical Intervention

Communication should not be considered an administrative activity occurring around clinical care.

It is part of clinical care.

Consider the difference between:

> "Someone get blood."

and:

> "Ahmed, activate the major haemorrhage protocol and request emergency blood now. Tell me when the blood bank confirms activation."

The second instruction contains:

- A named recipient
- A specific action
- A clear urgency
- A defined destination
- A requirement for confirmation

This is more than good manners.

It is an error-reduction strategy.

---

# 3. Communication Under Pressure

Trauma resuscitation creates conditions that make communication difficult.

These include:

- Noise
- Alarms
- Multiple conversations
- Time pressure
- Emotional stress
- Uncertainty
- Fatigue
- Hierarchy
- Cognitive overload
- Interruptions
- Conflicting priorities
- Incomplete information

Under pressure, communication tends to become shorter.

That can be helpful.

But excessive compression can make communication ambiguous.

For example:

> "Tube him."

may be understood as:

- Prepare for intubation
- Intubate immediately
- Prepare airway equipment
- Call anaesthesia
- Give induction drugs
- Perform RSI

The team leader should therefore aim for:

> **brevity without ambiguity**

---

# 4. The Principles of Effective Trauma Communication

Effective communication in the resuscitation room should be:

## 4.1 Clear

Use language that is immediately understandable.

Avoid unnecessary terminology, indirect language and vague instructions.

Instead of:

> "Maybe we should think about getting some blood."

say:

> "Start the massive haemorrhage protocol now."

---

## 4.2 Concise

The trauma leader should communicate the information required for action without unnecessary explanation during the critical phase.

Long explanations can be deferred until the immediate threat is controlled.

---

## 4.3 Directed

Instructions should be directed to a specific person whenever possible.

Instead of:

> "Can someone get another IV?"

say:

> "Sarah, insert a large-bore IV in the left arm."

Directed communication reduces ambiguity about responsibility.

---

## 4.4 Timely

Information must be delivered when it can influence management.

An important piece of information delivered too late may have little clinical value.

---

## 4.5 Confirmed

Critical instructions should not disappear into the background noise.

The leader should know:

- What was requested
- Who accepted responsibility
- Whether it was completed
- What the result was

---

## 4.6 Prioritised

Not every piece of information deserves equal attention.

The leader should emphasise information that changes immediate management.

For example:

> "Pressure is 65 systolic despite blood."

is more immediately actionable than a detailed discussion of a minor wound.

---

# 5. The Trauma Team Leader as Communication Coordinator

The leader should continuously ask:

> "Does everyone know what matters right now?"

The leader is not required to personally transmit every piece of information.

Instead, the leader coordinates information flow.

This involves:

1. Requesting information.
2. Receiving information.
3. Interpreting information.
4. Sharing important information.
5. Assigning actions.
6. Confirming completion.
7. Updating the team.
8. Reassessing the shared plan.

This creates a communication loop around the patient.

---

# 6. Closed-Loop Communication

Closed-loop communication is one of the most important communication techniques in high-risk environments.

The basic sequence is:

> **Instruction → acknowledgement → action → confirmation**

For example:

### Leader

> "Ali, apply direct pressure to the right thigh haemorrhage."

### Team member

> "Applying direct pressure to the right thigh."

### Team member after intervention

> "Direct pressure applied. Bleeding is significantly reduced."

### Leader

> "Good."

The loop is closed.

---

# 7. Why Closed-Loop Communication Matters

Without closed-loop communication, the leader may assume that an instruction was understood and completed.

Consider:

> "Give tranexamic acid."

Silence follows.

The leader moves to another problem.

Later, the leader assumes the drug has been administered.

But perhaps:

- Nobody heard the instruction.
- Two people heard it and assumed the other would act.
- The drug was prepared but not administered.
- The drug was unavailable.
- The dose was unclear.
- The clinician was interrupted.

The instruction existed.

The intervention did not.

Closed-loop communication reduces this gap.

---

# 8. The Three Essential Components

A practical closed-loop model contains three critical elements.

## Step 1 — Directed instruction

Identify the person.

> "Nurse Ahmed..."

Then state the action.

> "...give 1 g tranexamic acid IV now."

---

## Step 2 — Acknowledgement

The recipient confirms the instruction.

> "Giving 1 g tranexamic acid IV now."

This confirms that the message was heard and understood.

---

## Step 3 — Completion confirmation

The recipient reports completion.

> "Tranexamic acid administered."

The leader now knows the intervention occurred.

---

# 9. Closed Loop Does Not Mean Repeating Everything

Not every sentence requires a formal response.

The technique is most important for:

- Time-critical interventions
- High-risk interventions
- Medication administration
- Blood-product requests
- Procedures
- Transfers of responsibility
- Critical investigations
- Escalation
- Major changes in management

The leader should avoid creating unnecessary communication overhead.

The goal is:

> **reliable communication where reliability matters most**

---

# 10. Call-Outs

A call-out is the deliberate verbal communication of important information to the entire team.

Examples include:

> "Systolic pressure is 70."

> "There is severe external haemorrhage from the left leg."

> "The patient is becoming less responsive."

> "Airway is currently patent."

> "Chest movement is absent on the left."

Call-outs create a shared information environment.

---

# 11. Why Call-Outs Matter

Without call-outs, information can remain trapped within individual team members.

The anaesthetist may know:

> "The patient is becoming difficult to ventilate."

The nurse may know:

> "The oxygen saturation has fallen."

The surgeon may know:

> "The chest drain has produced no output."

If these observations remain isolated, the team may fail to recognise a developing tension pneumothorax or airway problem.

Call-outs combine distributed observations into a shared mental model.

---

# 12. Effective Call-Outs

An effective call-out should be:

- Brief
- Relevant
- Audible
- Specific
- Action-oriented when necessary

Poor:

> "Something isn't right."

Better:

> "Systolic pressure has fallen from 90 to 60."

Better still when action is required:

> "Systolic pressure has fallen from 90 to 60 despite blood. We need to reassess haemorrhage control now."

---

# 13. Check-Backs

A check-back is used when information needs to be verified.

For example:

> "What is the potassium?"

> "6.8."

> "Confirm potassium 6.8?"

> "Confirmed. 6.8."

Check-backs are particularly useful for:

- Drug doses
- Blood products
- Critical laboratory values
- Patient identification
- Procedure instructions
- Transfer destinations
- Changes in treatment

---

# 14. Read-Back of Critical Information

When an instruction or result has high potential for harm if misunderstood, read-back can provide an additional safety barrier.

Example:

> "Send this patient directly to theatre."

Recipient:

> "Confirmed: direct transfer to theatre."

This is especially important during transitions where assumptions can otherwise develop.

---

# 15. SBAR and Structured Communication

Structured communication frameworks can help organise information.

One commonly used structure is:

### S — Situation

What is happening now?

### B — Background

What relevant information explains the situation?

### A — Assessment

What do we think is happening?

### R — Recommendation

What do we need to do?

Example:

> **Situation:** "This is a hypotensive trauma patient with ongoing pelvic bleeding."
>
> **Background:** "High-speed collision, pelvic fracture identified on imaging, four units of blood already given."
>
> **Assessment:** "He remains unstable despite resuscitation."
>
> **Recommendation:** "We need immediate haemorrhage-control intervention and senior surgical review."

Structured communication reduces omission of important information.

---

# 16. Communication During the Primary Survey

Communication should follow the structure of the resuscitation.

The leader should prevent the team from becoming fragmented.

A useful sequence is:

> **Observe → verbalise → interpret → act → reassess**

For example:

> "Airway is compromised."

> "We need definitive airway control."

> "Anaesthesia, prepare for RSI."

> "Confirm when ready."

> "RSI underway."

> "Tube secured."

> "Ventilation confirmed."

> "Move to breathing assessment."

This creates a visible progression through the resuscitation.

---

# 17. Communication During Airway Management

Airway management is a high-risk communication environment.

The leader should clarify:

- Who owns the airway
- Who is preparing equipment
- Who is administering medications
- Who is monitoring
- What the backup plan is
- When the team is ready
- What the endpoint is

Example:

> "Anaesthesia will lead the airway. Sarah, prepare RSI drugs. Ahmed, prepare suction and backup airway equipment. Nurse, confirm monitoring and IV access. Tell me when the team is ready."

Before induction:

> "Everyone ready for RSI?"

After intubation:

> "Tube in. Confirm capnography."

This reduces ambiguity during a high-risk procedure.

---

# 18. Communication During Haemorrhage

Major haemorrhage requires particularly disciplined communication.

The leader should communicate:

- Recognition of haemorrhage
- Severity
- Source if known
- Blood-product requirement
- Major haemorrhage activation
- Mechanical haemorrhage control
- Response to resuscitation
- Need for definitive haemorrhage control

Example:

> "This patient remains in haemorrhagic shock with suspected pelvic bleeding."

> "Activate the massive haemorrhage protocol."

> "Apply pelvic binder now."

> "Tell me when blood is available."

> "Pressure remains 60 systolic after two units. We need definitive haemorrhage control."

---

# 19. Communication During Chest Trauma

The leader should ensure that findings and interventions are verbalised.

For example:

> "Absent left-sided breath sounds with severe respiratory distress."

Then:

> "This is clinically significant thoracic compromise."

Then:

> "Treat immediately according to the clinical picture."

After intervention:

> "Chest decompression completed."

Then:

> "Respiratory status reassessed."

The communication should reflect the clinical cycle:

> **finding → interpretation → intervention → response**

---

# 20. Communication During Neurological Deterioration

Changes in neurological status should be called out.

For example:

> "GCS has fallen from 12 to 8."

The leader should then make the implication explicit:

> "This is a significant neurological deterioration."

Then:

> "Prepare for definitive airway management."

This prevents an important change from being treated as merely another observation.

---

# 21. Communication About Uncertainty

Trauma care often occurs without diagnostic certainty.

The leader should communicate uncertainty honestly.

Useful phrases include:

> "The source of bleeding is not yet clear."

> "We have not excluded abdominal haemorrhage."

> "This is most likely chest haemorrhage, but we need to reassess."

> "I am concerned about occult ongoing bleeding."

This is preferable to false certainty.

---

# 22. Communicating a Change in Plan

The team should be told when the plan changes.

For example:

> "The original plan was CT, but the patient is now unstable. We are no longer going to CT. We are proceeding directly to haemorrhage control."

This prevents team members from continuing with an outdated plan.

---

# 23. The Shared Mental Model

A high-performing trauma team should have a shared understanding of:

- The patient's current condition
- The major threats
- What has already been done
- What is being done now
- What needs to happen next
- What could go wrong
- What the destination is

The leader creates and maintains this shared mental model.

A useful periodic summary is:

> "Current situation: ongoing haemorrhagic shock. Airway secured. Chest currently stable. Pelvic haemorrhage remains the leading concern. Blood is running. We are preparing for definitive haemorrhage control."

This takes seconds but can significantly improve team alignment.

---

# 24. The Leader's Periodic Update

During prolonged resuscitation, the leader should periodically pause and summarise.

A practical structure is:

### What do we know?

### What have we done?

### What has changed?

### What remains unresolved?

### What are we doing next?

Example:

> "We have controlled the external haemorrhage and secured the airway. He remains hypotensive despite blood. The chest is currently stable. The major unresolved problem is ongoing internal haemorrhage. Next priority is definitive haemorrhage control."

---

# 25. Communication and Delegation

Delegation without communication is incomplete.

The leader should communicate:

1. Who
2. What
3. When
4. Expected endpoint
5. Confirmation requirement

Example:

> "Ahmed, insert a large-bore IV now. Tell me when access is secured."

This is stronger than:

> "We need another IV."

---

# 26. Communication and Prioritisation

The leader should communicate the priority explicitly.

For example:

> "Our immediate priority is haemorrhage control."

This helps prevent the team from becoming distracted by less important tasks.

When several problems exist:

> "Airway is currently controlled. The immediate threat is haemorrhage."

The statement provides direction.

---

# 27. Avoiding Ambiguous Pronouns

Avoid:

> "He needs that."

> "Give it."

> "Put this there."

Instead use specific language:

> "Give 1 g tranexamic acid IV."

> "Apply the pelvic binder."

> "Send the blood sample to the laboratory."

Specific language reduces misunderstanding.

---

# 28. Avoiding Vague Time References

Avoid:

> "Soon."

> "Later."

> "When you can."

Instead use:

> "Now."

> "Within the next minute."

> "After the airway is secured."

> "Before leaving the resuscitation room."

Time-critical instructions should have a clear temporal meaning.

---

# 29. Communication and Noise

Resuscitation rooms are noisy.

The leader should not assume that everyone heard an important instruction.

For critical information:

- Use the person's name.
- Face the recipient if possible.
- Speak clearly.
- Avoid speaking simultaneously with another speaker.
- Request acknowledgement.
- Confirm completion.

If the message is critical, repeat it.

---

# 30. Managing Multiple Conversations

Multiple simultaneous conversations are common.

The leader should monitor whether important information is being lost.

If necessary:

> "Pause. One conversation."

or:

> "I need everyone's attention for a moment."

Then provide the critical update.

This is particularly useful when the patient's condition changes suddenly.

---

# 31. Communication During Crisis

When the situation deteriorates rapidly, communication should become more structured rather than less structured.

The leader should:

1. Announce the deterioration.
2. State the immediate threat.
3. Assign actions.
4. Confirm completion.
5. Reassess.

Example:

> "Pressure is now 50 systolic."

> "We have uncontrolled haemorrhagic shock."

> "Massive haemorrhage protocol continues."

> "Surgical team, prepare immediate haemorrhage control."

> "Anaesthesia, maintain airway and support circulation."

> "Nurse, confirm blood availability."

> "Tell me when each action is complete."

---

# 32. Speaking Up

Effective communication must work in both directions.

Team members should be able to communicate concerns to the leader.

A nurse who notices:

> "The blood pressure has fallen."

must feel able to say so immediately.

A junior doctor who notices:

> "The chest drain has stopped swinging and the patient is deteriorating."

must be able to raise the concern.

The leader should actively encourage this.

Useful language:

> "If anyone sees something I am missing, say it immediately."

---

# 33. The Leader Must Listen

Communication is not simply speaking.

The leader must actively listen.

Poor leadership may involve:

- Interrupting repeatedly
- Ignoring concerns
- Looking only at the monitor
- Failing to acknowledge information
- Rejecting contradictory observations
- Becoming fixated on one diagnosis

Good leadership includes:

> **listening → processing → responding**

---

# 34. Psychological Safety

Team members must feel able to speak up about:

- Deterioration
- Medication errors
- Equipment problems
- Incorrect assumptions
- Procedural difficulties
- Safety concerns

A psychologically safe team does not mean an unstructured team.

The leader can remain decisive while encouraging challenge.

For example:

> "If you disagree with the plan, tell me. We will resolve it quickly."

---

# 35. Graded Assertiveness

When a team member identifies a safety concern that is not being addressed, graded assertiveness can help escalate the message.

A progression may be:

> "I am concerned."

Then:

> "I am seriously concerned."

Then:

> "I think this is unsafe."

The exact wording may vary by local practice.

The important principle is that the team member should be able to escalate when the risk persists.

---

# 36. Responding to Challenge

The trauma leader should avoid responding defensively.

If a team member says:

> "I am concerned that we are missing abdominal bleeding."

A poor response is:

> "I know what I am doing."

A better response is:

> "What are you seeing that makes you concerned?"

This converts challenge into useful information.

---

# 37. Communication Failure: The Unnamed Task

### Example

Leader:

> "Someone get another IV."

No one responds.

### Failure

Responsibility is undefined.

### Correction

> "Ahmed, insert a second large-bore IV."

### Closed loop

> "Second IV in."

---

# 38. Communication Failure: No Confirmation

Leader:

> "Activate the massive haemorrhage protocol."

Silence.

The leader assumes it happened.

### Correction

> "Sarah, activate the massive haemorrhage protocol and confirm when the blood bank has accepted the request."

---

# 39. Communication Failure: Information Not Shared

The anaesthetist notices rapidly increasing airway pressures but does not immediately communicate it.

The leader remains focused on pelvic bleeding.

### Correction

The anaesthetist should call out:

> "Airway pressures are rising significantly."

The leader should acknowledge:

> "Received. We have a new ventilation problem. Stop and reassess the airway and chest."

---

# 40. Communication Failure: Outdated Plan

The team was preparing for CT.

The patient then becomes profoundly hypotensive.

A radiographer asks:

> "Are we going to CT?"

The team has not discussed the change.

### Correction

The leader announces:

> "No. The patient is now unstable. CT is cancelled. We are proceeding to immediate haemorrhage control."

---

# 41. Communication Failure: Overcommunication

Not all communication is useful.

Continuous unnecessary discussion can:

- Increase noise
- Distract clinicians
- Delay decisions
- Hide important information
- Increase cognitive load

The leader should distinguish:

> **information required for action**

from:

> **information that can wait**

---

# 42. Communication Failure: Undercommunication

The opposite problem is silence.

Silence may lead to:

- Assumptions
- Duplication
- Omission
- Unclear responsibility
- Loss of situational awareness

The leader should therefore communicate enough to keep the team aligned.

---

# 43. Communication Failure: Premature Discussion

During an immediate life-threatening problem, prolonged discussion can delay intervention.

For example:

> "Could this be a pelvic bleed, or perhaps an abdominal source, or maybe..."

The team needs an immediate action.

A better approach:

> "He is in haemorrhagic shock. Start haemorrhage-control measures now. We can refine the source as we proceed."

---

# 44. Communication Failure: Hierarchy

Hierarchy can prevent junior team members from speaking.

A junior doctor may recognise:

> "The patient is becoming more unstable."

but hesitate to interrupt the consultant.

The leader should actively reduce this barrier.

For example:

> "If you see deterioration, interrupt me."

---

# 45. Communication During Procedures

Before a high-risk procedure, the leader should establish:

- Indication
- Operator
- Assistants
- Equipment
- Monitoring
- Expected endpoint
- Potential complications
- Backup plan

For example:

> "We are performing emergency thoracic intervention for suspected life-threatening chest injury. Surgeon will perform the procedure. Anaesthesia will maintain airway and circulation. Nurse will prepare equipment and blood. Tell me immediately if physiology deteriorates."

---

# 46. Communication Before Transfer

Transfer is a high-risk communication point.

Before leaving the resuscitation room, the team should know:

- Destination
- Clinical status
- Airway status
- Circulatory status
- Current infusions
- Blood products
- Drains
- Lines
- Pending investigations
- Outstanding interventions
- Anticipated deterioration

The leader should explicitly communicate:

> "We are transferring to theatre for definitive haemorrhage control."

---

# 47. Handover as a Communication Event

Handover should not be treated as a simple recitation of facts.

The receiving team needs to understand:

> **What happened → what has been done → what is still wrong → what needs to happen next**

The leader should ensure that critical information is transferred accurately.

---

# 48. Communication and Documentation

Important communication may need to be reflected in documentation.

Examples include:

- Major decisions
- Treatment escalation
- Specialist advice
- Transfer decisions
- Changes in diagnosis
- Family communication
- Consent discussions where applicable
- Time-critical interventions

The documentation should reflect the clinical reality without becoming a substitute for direct communication.

---

# 49. Communication and Technology

Technology can support communication but can also interfere with it.

Examples include:

- Monitors
- Electronic prescribing
- Digital imaging
- Telephone communication
- Radios
- Electronic records

The leader should not allow attention to technology to replace direct team communication.

A monitor may show:

> "BP 60/30."

The team still needs someone to say:

> "The patient is profoundly hypotensive."

The clinical meaning must be communicated.

---

# 50. Communication and Cognitive Load

Under cognitive load, people process less information.

Therefore, the leader should:

- Reduce unnecessary conversation
- Use short statements
- Use names
- State priorities
- Repeat critical information
- Confirm completion
- Summarise periodically

A simple communication architecture is:

> **Priority → instruction → confirmation → result**

---

# 51. The Communication Pause

At important transition points, the leader can briefly pause the team.

For example:

> "Pause for a ten-second update."

Then:

> "Airway secured. Ventilation adequate. Still hypotensive. External bleeding controlled. Blood running. Main concern remains internal haemorrhage. Next step is definitive haemorrhage control."

This can reset the team's shared mental model.

---

# 52. The One-Minute Team Update

A useful structure during prolonged resuscitation is:

### 1. Current state

> "Patient remains unstable."

### 2. Main threats

> "Ongoing haemorrhage is the leading concern."

### 3. Completed interventions

> "Airway secured, pelvic binder applied, blood products running."

### 4. Outstanding actions

> "Definitive haemorrhage control is pending."

### 5. Next decision

> "We are proceeding to theatre."

---

# 53. Communication When the Leader Is Wrong

The trauma leader may make an incorrect assessment.

The communication system should allow correction.

A team member might say:

> "I think the source may be different."

The leader should consider the information rather than defending the original decision.

The correct response may be:

> "Explain."

Then:

> "Thank you. Let's reassess."

Leadership is not demonstrated by never changing the plan.

It is demonstrated by changing the plan appropriately when new information appears.

---

# 54. Communication and Situational Awareness

Communication is one of the main mechanisms through which situational awareness is maintained.

The leader must know:

### What is happening?

### Why is it happening?

### What is likely to happen next?

### What are we doing about it?

Communication helps the entire team answer these questions.

---

# 55. Communication and Team Trust

Trust develops when team members experience:

- Clear instructions
- Predictable leadership
- Respectful interaction
- Accurate information
- Appropriate delegation
- Acknowledgement of concerns
- Consistent follow-through

A leader who repeatedly gives instructions without checking completion creates uncertainty.

A leader who listens and closes loops creates reliability.

---

# 56. Practical Communication Algorithm

A simple trauma-team communication algorithm is:

> **Name → Action → Time → Confirmation**

Example:

> "Ahmed → apply direct pressure → now → tell me when bleeding is controlled."

For critical interventions:

> **Name → Action → Time → Acknowledgement → Completion → Result**

Example:

> "Sarah, activate the massive haemorrhage protocol now."

> "Activating now."

> "Protocol activated."

> "Good. Tell me when blood is available."

> "Blood is available."

> "Start transfusion."

---

# 57. Communication Checklist for the Trauma Leader

During resuscitation, ask:

### Direction

- Did I name the person?
- Did I give a clear instruction?
- Did I specify the expected action?

### Timing

- Did I state when the action should occur?
- Is the urgency understood?

### Confirmation

- Did the recipient acknowledge?
- Did I receive confirmation of completion?

### Shared mental model

- Does the team know the current priorities?
- Has the plan changed?
- Did I communicate the change?

### Information flow

- Are important observations being called out?
- Are concerns being heard?
- Is anyone missing critical information?

### Safety

- Can team members challenge the plan?
- Have I actively invited concerns?

# 58. Case-Based Discussion

## Case

A 67-year-old man presents following a high-speed collision.

Initial findings:

- GCS 13
- Respiratory distress
- Systolic BP 75 mmHg
- Heart rate 135/min
- Obvious pelvic instability
- Cool peripheries
- No major external haemorrhage

The team begins resuscitation.

The trauma leader says:

> "Let's get an ultrasound."

The nurse begins preparing the machine.

The surgeon starts examining the abdomen.

The anaesthetist focuses on the airway.

Nobody explicitly states the overall priority.

### Questions

1. What communication problem is present?
2. What is the immediate leadership issue?
3. How could the leader create a shared mental model?
4. How could the instruction be made clearer?
5. Where should closed-loop communication be used?

### Suggested discussion

The leader should first communicate the immediate clinical priority.

For example:

> "This patient is in profound shock. Our immediate concern is major haemorrhage."

Then delegate:

> "Anaesthesia, manage airway and ventilation."

> "Surgery, assess and control haemorrhage."

> "Nurse, activate the major haemorrhage pathway and prepare blood."

> "Tell me when blood is available."

The team now has:

- A shared problem
- Defined roles
- Explicit priorities
- Clear communication loops

---

# 59. Case-Based Discussion — Communication Breakdown

## Case

A patient with penetrating chest trauma suddenly becomes hypotensive.

The nurse says:

> "Pressure is falling."

The leader does not respond.

The anaesthetist says:

> "We are losing the airway."

The surgeon continues discussing the abdominal wound.

### Questions

What has gone wrong?

The problem is not merely that information was spoken.

The problem is that information was not integrated into the team's decision-making.

The leader should acknowledge the information:

> "Received. The patient is deteriorating."

Then establish priority:

> "Immediate threat is airway and circulation."

Then assign actions:

> "Anaesthesia, secure the airway."

> "Surgery, prepare immediate chest intervention."

> "Nurse, continue blood pressure and blood-product monitoring."

The leader should then reassess.

---

# 60. Case-Based Discussion — Closed Loop Failure

## Case

The leader says:

> "Give calcium."

A nurse asks:

> "How much?"

The leader does not hear the question.

The nurse waits.

The resuscitation continues.

### Lesson

An instruction is not complete simply because it was spoken.

The leader should specify:

- Drug
- Dose
- Route
- Timing

and ensure acknowledgement.

Example:

> "Give the indicated calcium dose IV now. Confirm when administered."

If the dose is uncertain, the leader should verify it rather than rely on assumption.

---

# 61. Case-Based Discussion — Speaking Up

## Case

The leader decides to transfer a patient to CT.

The patient remains tachycardic and hypotensive.

A junior doctor says:

> "I am concerned that the patient is still unstable for CT."

The leader replies:

> "We need the scan."

### Questions

What should happen next?

The concern should be explored.

A safer response is:

> "What are you seeing?"

If the concern is justified:

> "Agreed. We are not going to CT. We will continue resuscitation and obtain definitive haemorrhage control."

The communication system has therefore prevented a potentially dangerous plan from continuing unchallenged.

# 62. Common Pitfalls

## Pitfall 1 — Giving instructions to the room

> "Someone get blood."

### Correction

Name the person.

---

## Pitfall 2 — Assuming completion

> "Give the medication."

Then moving on.

### Correction

Require confirmation.

---

## Pitfall 3 — Speaking too quickly

Rapid speech may reduce comprehension.

### Correction

Use short, clear statements.

---

## Pitfall 4 — Excessive discussion

Long conversations can obscure urgent information.

### Correction

Prioritise actionable information.

---

## Pitfall 5 — Failure to announce deterioration

The team may continue following an outdated plan.

### Correction

Call out major physiological changes.

---

## Pitfall 6 — Ignoring challenge

This suppresses useful information.

### Correction

Ask:

> "What are you seeing?"

---

## Pitfall 7 — No periodic summary

The team gradually develops different understandings of the situation.

### Correction

Use brief communication pauses.

---

## Pitfall 8 — Communication dominated by hierarchy

Junior staff may stop speaking.

### Correction

Explicitly invite concerns.

---

## Pitfall 9 — Vague language

Words such as:

> "Soon."

> "Maybe."

> "Someone."

create ambiguity.

### Correction

Use specific actions and responsibilities.

# 63. The Trauma Leader's Communication Philosophy

The leader should aim for:

> **Clear enough to act.  
> Brief enough to hear.  
> Specific enough to understand.  
> Structured enough to remember.  
> Confirmed enough to trust.**

---

# 64. Practical Resuscitation-Room Communication Model

A highly practical model is:

### 1. See

Observe the patient.

### 2. Say

Communicate the important finding.

### 3. Decide

Identify the priority.

### 4. Direct

Assign the action.

### 5. Confirm

Close the communication loop.

### 6. Reassess

Determine the response.

### 7. Update

Communicate the new situation.

This creates a continuous cycle:

> **Observe → communicate → act → confirm → reassess → communicate again**

---

# 65. Final Practical Checklist

Before moving on from a major problem, the trauma leader should ask:

- What did we find?
- Has the team heard it?
- What does it mean?
- What is the immediate priority?
- Who is doing what?
- Has the instruction been acknowledged?
- Has the intervention been completed?
- What was the response?
- Does the plan need to change?
- Does the whole team know the new plan?

# 66. Key Takeaways

1. Communication is a clinical intervention in major trauma.
2. High-quality communication reduces ambiguity and omission.
3. Critical instructions should be directed to named individuals.
4. Closed-loop communication confirms that important tasks are understood and completed.
5. Call-outs create shared situational awareness.
6. Check-backs and read-backs can reduce high-risk communication errors.
7. The leader should communicate priorities explicitly.
8. The leader should periodically update the team's shared mental model.
9. Communication should become more structured during deterioration, not less.
10. Team members must be able to speak up when they identify danger.
11. The leader should listen actively and respond to challenge.
12. Vague instructions create avoidable uncertainty.
13. Communication should be concise but never ambiguous.
14. Major changes in the clinical plan should be announced clearly.
15. Effective communication links observation, decision, action and reassessment.

> **A trauma team cannot reliably execute a plan that it does not clearly understand.**

The trauma leader's job is therefore not simply to speak.

It is to ensure that the right information reaches the right person, at the right time, in a form that produces the right action—and that the result comes back to the team.
