# Chapter 11 — Burns and Thermal Trauma

## Clinical Scenario

A 29-year-old man is brought to the resuscitation room following a house fire.

He was found inside a smoke-filled room and was removed by firefighters.

The pre-hospital team reports that he was initially confused and coughing extensively.

On arrival, he is anxious and tachypnoeic.

There are burns involving the face, anterior chest, and both upper limbs.

His voice is hoarse.

Initial observations are:

- respiratory rate 30/min;
- oxygen saturation 94% on high-concentration oxygen;
- heart rate 128/min;
- systolic blood pressure 102 mmHg;
- Glasgow Coma Scale 14/15;
- cool peripheries.

There is soot around the mouth and nose.

The Trauma Team Leader recognises that the visible burns are only part of the immediate problem.

The major priorities are airway protection, oxygenation, recognition of inhalation injury, assessment of burn severity, prevention of ongoing thermal injury, and identification of associated trauma or toxic exposure.

The leader must coordinate these priorities while continuing the principles of the primary survey.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Recognise burns as a major trauma problem rather than an isolated skin injury.
2. Apply the primary survey to the burned or thermally injured patient.
3. Recognise clinical features suggesting impending airway compromise.
4. Identify the importance of inhalation injury and toxic gas exposure.
5. Understand the significance of carbon monoxide and other combustion-related toxic exposures.
6. Estimate burn extent using an appropriate method.
7. Recognise burns that require urgent specialist assessment.
8. Initiate appropriate fluid resuscitation when indicated.
9. Prevent hypothermia while assessing and treating extensive burns.
10. Recognise circumferential burns that may compromise ventilation or limb perfusion.
11. Identify associated traumatic injuries that may coexist with burns.
12. Coordinate transfer to an appropriate burns or trauma service.
13. Reassess the patient after major interventions and when physiology changes.

---

## Key Concepts

### Burns Are a Trauma Problem

A major burn should be approached using the same fundamental principles that govern other forms of major trauma.

The team must first identify and treat immediate threats to:

- airway;
- breathing;
- circulation;
- neurological function;
- and environmental control.

The burn itself should not distract the team from potentially fatal associated injuries.

A patient rescued from a fire may have:

- inhalation injury;
- carbon monoxide exposure;
- other toxic inhalation;
- trauma from falls or explosions;
- blast injury;
- fractures;
- head injury;
- or other occult injuries.

The leader must therefore maintain a broad diagnostic approach.

---

### Mechanism Matters

The mechanism should immediately influence the assessment.

Important mechanisms include:

- flame injury;
- scald;
- contact burn;
- chemical exposure;
- electrical injury;
- explosion;
- smoke exposure;
- and enclosed-space fire.

An enclosed-space fire is particularly important because it increases concern for inhalation injury and toxic exposure.

The leader should ask:

> **What happened, where did it happen, and what was the patient exposed to?**

This information may be as important as the visible appearance of the burn.

---

### The Burn Is Dynamic

The appearance of a burn at the time of arrival may underestimate the eventual severity.

Tissue injury can evolve.

Oedema may also increase significantly during the early resuscitation period.

This is particularly important when burns involve:

- the face;
- neck;
- chest;
- hands;
- or circumferential areas of the limbs.

The leader should therefore treat the patient according to both current findings and anticipated trajectory.

---

## A — Airway

### Airway Assessment

The airway should be assessed immediately.

The leader should look for:

- ability to speak;
- voice changes;
- hoarseness;
- stridor;
- facial burns;
- oral burns;
- soot in the mouth or nose;
- carbonaceous sputum;
- swelling;
- reduced consciousness;
- and signs of airway obstruction.

A patient who is initially speaking may still develop progressive airway compromise.

---

### Inhalation Injury

Inhalation injury should be suspected particularly when there has been exposure to:

- smoke;
- fire in an enclosed space;
- hot gases;
- combustion products;
- or chemical fumes.

Clinical features may include:

- facial burns;
- singed facial or nasal hair;
- soot around the mouth or nose;
- hoarseness;
- cough;
- carbonaceous sputum;
- wheeze;
- stridor;
- respiratory distress;
- or altered mental status.

No single external finding reliably excludes inhalation injury.

The leader should therefore integrate the mechanism, clinical findings, and trajectory.

---

### Anticipate Airway Oedema

Airway oedema can progress after the initial assessment.

Fluid resuscitation may further increase tissue swelling.

The critical question is not simply:

> **"Is the airway patent now?"**

It is:

> **"Will the patient be able to maintain a patent airway as the injury evolves?"**

If significant deterioration is anticipated, early airway planning is essential.

Delayed airway intervention may become more difficult as oedema progresses.

---

### Airway Intervention

When airway protection is required, the team should ensure:

- appropriate expertise;
- appropriate equipment;
- continuous monitoring;
- adequate oxygenation;
- a backup airway strategy;
- and preparation for a potentially difficult airway.

The decision should be based on:

- current airway findings;
- neurological status;
- inhalation injury;
- facial or neck burns;
- anticipated oedema;
- and the patient's trajectory.

The Trauma Team Leader should ensure that the airway plan is communicated clearly to the entire team.

---

## B — Breathing

### Immediate Assessment

Breathing should be assessed using the standard trauma approach.

The leader should assess:

- respiratory rate;
- work of breathing;
- oxygenation;
- chest movement;
- breath sounds;
- chest wall injury;
- and overall respiratory trajectory.

The presence of a burn does not explain every respiratory abnormality.

Other thoracic injuries must remain in the differential diagnosis.

---

### Smoke and Toxic Gas Exposure

Respiratory distress after a fire may result from:

- direct airway injury;
- bronchospasm;
- pulmonary injury;
- carbon monoxide exposure;
- other toxic gases;
- or associated trauma.

The team should avoid attributing all abnormalities simply to the visible burn.

---

### Carbon Monoxide Exposure

Carbon monoxide exposure should be considered following significant smoke exposure, particularly in an enclosed-space fire.

Clinical features may include:

- headache;
- confusion;
- dizziness;
- altered consciousness;
- weakness;
- nausea;
- or cardiovascular instability.

Neurological symptoms may occur despite apparently acceptable oxygen saturation.

Standard pulse oximetry cannot reliably determine the severity of carbon monoxide exposure because it may not distinguish oxyhaemoglobin from carboxyhaemoglobin.

The clinical context should therefore remain central.

---

### Respiratory Monitoring

The patient should undergo appropriate monitoring according to the severity of injury.

The leader should reassess:

- respiratory effort;
- oxygenation;
- mental status;
- chest examination;
- and response to treatment.

A deteriorating patient should trigger immediate reassessment rather than continuation of a predetermined pathway.

---

### Circumferential Chest Burns

A deep circumferential burn involving the chest may restrict chest wall movement.

The leader should consider this when:

- ventilation becomes increasingly difficult;
- chest excursion is restricted;
- airway and pulmonary causes do not adequately explain deterioration;
- or the burn pattern suggests significant circumferential restriction.

If chest restriction becomes clinically significant, urgent specialist intervention may be required.

---

## C — Circulation

### Assess Perfusion

The circulation assessment follows the same trauma principles used in other major trauma patients.

The leader should assess:

- heart rate;
- blood pressure;
- peripheral perfusion;
- mental status;
- urine output where appropriate;
- and overall response to resuscitation.

Hypotension in a burned patient should not automatically be attributed to the burn itself.

Other causes must be considered, including:

- haemorrhage;
- associated trauma;
- dehydration;
- toxic exposure;
- cardiac injury;
- and evolving shock.

---

### Intravenous Access

Patients with significant burns may require reliable vascular access.

The team should obtain appropriate access without allowing access attempts to create unnecessary delay.

If peripheral access is difficult, alternative appropriate access strategies may be required according to the clinical situation and local protocol.

---

### Fluid Resuscitation

Patients with significant burns may develop substantial fluid shifts and require formal fluid resuscitation.

The amount required depends on factors including:

- burn size;
- patient characteristics;
- associated injuries;
- and clinical response.

The leader should ensure that fluid administration is guided by an appropriate burns resuscitation protocol rather than by an arbitrary fixed volume.

---

### Do Not Over-Resuscitate

Excessive fluid administration may worsen:

- tissue oedema;
- airway swelling;
- limb compartment pressure;
- pulmonary complications;
- and overall morbidity.

The goal is therefore not simply to administer large volumes.

The goal is:

> **Adequate perfusion with the minimum necessary resuscitation.**

The patient must be reassessed repeatedly.

---

## Estimating Burn Size

### Why Burn Size Matters

Estimating the extent of the burn helps determine:

- severity;
- need for fluid resuscitation;
- need for specialist referral;
- transfer requirements;
- and prognosis.

The estimate should be performed systematically.

---

### Total Body Surface Area

Burn extent is commonly expressed as a percentage of total body surface area.

Only areas of significant partial-thickness and full-thickness burn are generally included in formal burn-size calculations.

Simple erythema without significant tissue injury should not be treated as equivalent to a deeper burn when calculating burn size.

---

### Rule of Nines

The Rule of Nines can provide a rapid adult estimate.

It divides the body into approximate regions representing multiples of 9%.

However, it is an estimation tool rather than a precise measurement.

The leader should use an appropriate method according to patient age, body habitus, and local practice.

---

### Lund and Browder Method

The Lund and Browder chart provides a more accurate method of estimating burn extent, particularly in children.

Body proportions vary with age.

The method therefore adjusts the relative contribution of different body regions.

When available, an appropriate age-adjusted burn chart should be used for formal assessment.

---

### Patient's Palm

The patient's palm can provide a convenient bedside reference for estimating small burn areas.

This is particularly useful when the burn involves relatively limited or irregular areas.

The estimate should still be interpreted within the overall clinical context.

---

## Burn Depth

### Superficial Injury

Superficial burns primarily involve the epidermis.

Typical features include:

- erythema;
- pain;
- and absence of blistering associated with deeper tissue injury.

These injuries generally do not form the major component of burn resuscitation calculations.

---

### Partial-Thickness Burns

Partial-thickness burns involve deeper layers of the skin.

They may be:

- superficial partial thickness;
- or deeper partial thickness.

Clinical appearance can vary.

Features may include:

- blistering;
- pain;
- moist appearance;
- and variable colour.

The depth may evolve over time.

---

### Full-Thickness Burns

Full-thickness injury extends through the full thickness of the skin.

The affected area may appear:

- pale;
- white;
- brown;
- or charred.

Sensation may be reduced because of destruction of cutaneous nerve endings.

The visual appearance alone should not be used to determine the complete extent of tissue injury.

---

## Special Burn Mechanisms

### Chemical Burns

Chemical burns require immediate attention to ongoing exposure.

The priorities include:

1. protect the team from contamination;
2. remove contaminated clothing;
3. remove the chemical from the patient;
4. irrigate appropriately;
5. identify the substance if possible;
6. assess for systemic toxicity;
7. and obtain specialist advice.

The specific management may depend on the chemical involved.

The leader should ensure that the team does not become exposed during decontamination.

---

### Electrical Burns

Electrical injury may cause significant internal damage despite limited external skin findings.

Potential complications include:

- cardiac arrhythmia;
- myocardial injury;
- muscle damage;
- neurological injury;
- compartment syndrome;
- and renal complications associated with severe muscle injury.

The mechanism should therefore be carefully established.

Important information includes:

- voltage;
- type of current;
- duration of exposure;
- pathway through the body;
- and whether the patient was thrown or fell.

---

### Lightning Injury

Lightning injury has a distinct mechanism and may cause:

- cardiac arrest;
- respiratory arrest;
- neurological injury;
- burns;
- and trauma from falls or blast-like effects.

The patient should therefore be assessed as a trauma patient rather than focusing exclusively on skin injury.

---

### Explosion-Related Burns

Burns following an explosion may coexist with:

- blast injury;
- penetrating injury;
- blunt trauma;
- tympanic membrane injury;
- pulmonary injury;
- and traumatic brain injury.

The mechanism should therefore trigger a broad trauma assessment.

---

## Circumferential Burns

### Why They Matter

Circumferential burns may produce a constricting eschar.

This can impair:

- chest expansion;
- limb perfusion;
- venous drainage;
- or tissue pressure.

The risk depends on the depth and location of the burn.

---

### Circumferential Limb Burns

The team should repeatedly assess:

- distal pulses;
- capillary refill;
- skin temperature;
- sensation;
- motor function;
- pain;
- and swelling.

Changes may indicate evolving vascular compromise.

A normal pulse at one point in time does not eliminate the need for continued monitoring.

---

### Circumferential Chest Burns

A circumferential chest burn may impair ventilation by restricting chest wall expansion.

The leader should consider this when respiratory mechanics deteriorate despite appropriate management of other causes.

Urgent specialist assessment may be required.

---

## D — Disability

Neurological assessment remains essential.

The leader should assess:

- Glasgow Coma Scale;
- pupils;
- limb movement;
- glucose where indicated;
- and overall neurological trajectory.

Altered consciousness following a fire may result from:

- hypoxia;
- carbon monoxide exposure;
- other toxic exposure;
- traumatic brain injury;
- shock;
- or another medical or traumatic cause.

The finding should therefore not automatically be attributed to smoke inhalation.

---

## E — Exposure and Environmental Control

### Complete Exposure

The patient should be adequately exposed to identify:

- the full extent of burns;
- associated trauma;
- hidden injuries;
- and circumferential burns.

Clothing and other materials that may retain heat or chemicals should be removed appropriately.

---

### Prevent Hypothermia

Burned patients are particularly vulnerable to heat loss.

This creates an important balance:

> **Expose enough to assess the patient, but do not leave the patient unnecessarily exposed.**

The team should:

- use a warm environment;
- minimise unnecessary exposure time;
- use appropriate warming measures;
- and monitor temperature.

Hypothermia may worsen coagulopathy and physiological instability.

---

## Associated Trauma

### Do Not Miss Non-Burn Trauma

A patient involved in a fire may also have sustained significant trauma.

Possible associated injuries include:

- head injury;
- cervical spine injury;
- chest trauma;
- abdominal injury;
- pelvic injury;
- fractures;
- and penetrating trauma.

The mechanism should determine the breadth of assessment.

---

### Falls

Patients may jump or fall from windows or elevated structures while escaping a fire.

This may produce:

- spinal injury;
- calcaneal fractures;
- pelvic injury;
- long-bone fractures;
- or head injury.

The leader should specifically ask about falls.

---

## Secondary Survey in the Burned Patient

Once immediate threats have been addressed, a structured secondary survey should be performed.

The team should document:

- mechanism;
- time of injury;
- duration of exposure;
- enclosed-space exposure;
- chemical or electrical exposure;
- estimated burn extent;
- burn depth;
- inhalation injury features;
- associated trauma;
- and treatment already provided.

The AMPLE history should be obtained where possible.

---

## Pain Management

Burn injuries can be extremely painful.

Pain should be assessed repeatedly.

The leader should ensure that appropriate analgesia is provided while maintaining awareness of:

- respiratory status;
- neurological status;
- haemodynamics;
- and the possibility of associated injuries.

Pain management should not obscure deterioration in consciousness or respiratory function.

---

## Temperature Management

Temperature management is a major component of burn care.

Large areas of damaged skin result in increased heat loss.

Hypothermia may occur rapidly during:

- examination;
- cleaning;
- transfer;
- and procedures.

The team should therefore actively prevent heat loss throughout resuscitation.

---

## Wound Protection

Burn wounds should be protected appropriately during initial management.

The purpose is to:

- prevent further contamination;
- reduce heat loss;
- reduce pain;
- and facilitate transfer or definitive treatment.

The specific dressing strategy should follow local burns protocols.

The leader should avoid allowing wound dressing to distract from airway, breathing, circulation, or other immediate threats.

---

## Eschar and Compartment Problems

Deep circumferential burns may behave as constricting structures.

As oedema develops beneath the inelastic burned tissue, pressure may rise.

Potential consequences include:

- vascular compromise;
- neurological impairment;
- impaired ventilation;
- and tissue ischaemia.

The leader should therefore ensure serial reassessment rather than relying on a single examination.

---

## When Specialist Burn Referral Is Required

The threshold for specialist consultation should be low when there is:

- significant burn extent;
- suspected inhalation injury;
- deep burns;
- full-thickness burns;
- burns involving the face;
- burns involving the hands;
- burns involving the feet;
- burns involving major joints;
- genital or perineal burns;
- circumferential burns;
- electrical injury;
- chemical injury;
- or significant associated trauma.

Local referral criteria should always be followed.

---

## Transfer and Definitive Care

The Trauma Team Leader should determine early whether the patient requires:

- local definitive management;
- specialist burns assessment;
- transfer to a burns centre;
- critical care;
- operative management;
- or another specialist pathway.

Transfer should not be delayed unnecessarily once the need for specialist care is clear.

The patient must be sufficiently stabilised for transfer, while recognising that definitive care may be required elsewhere.

---

## Documentation

Accurate documentation is particularly important in major burns.

The team should record, where possible:

- time of injury;
- mechanism;
- exposure conditions;
- estimated burn size;
- burn depth;
- affected anatomical regions;
- airway findings;
- respiratory status;
- circulation;
- neurological status;
- fluid administration;
- analgesia;
- procedures;
- and clinical response.

Clear documentation supports:

- ongoing resuscitation;
- specialist communication;
- transfer;
- operative planning;
- and subsequent review.

---

## Reassessment

The burn patient must be reassessed repeatedly.

Reassessment should occur:

- after airway intervention;
- after major fluid administration;
- after deterioration;
- during transfer;
- and whenever new information becomes available.

The leader should repeatedly ask:

> **"What has changed?"**

and:

> **"Does the current management still fit the patient's physiology?"**

---

## Clinical Decision-Making Framework

A useful approach is:

**Mechanism → Primary survey → Immediate treatment → Burn assessment → Associated injury assessment → Resuscitation → Reassessment → Specialist pathway**

This sequence should remain flexible.

The patient should not be forced through a predetermined pathway when physiology demands immediate intervention.

---

## Trauma Team Leader Responsibilities

The Trauma Team Leader should:

1. Establish the mechanism and likely injury pattern.
2. Identify immediate threats using the primary survey.
3. Anticipate airway deterioration.
4. Recognise inhalation and toxic exposure.
5. Coordinate respiratory and circulatory support.
6. Ensure appropriate burn-size estimation.
7. Identify circumferential burns.
8. Prevent hypothermia.
9. Search actively for associated trauma.
10. Ensure appropriate analgesia.
11. Coordinate specialist consultation.
12. Determine the definitive destination.
13. Communicate the patient's condition and treatment clearly.
14. Reassess after major interventions.
15. Escalate when the patient's trajectory is worsening.

---

## Common Cognitive Errors

### Anchoring on the Burn

A visible burn may dominate the team's attention.

This can lead to missed:

- head injury;
- chest trauma;
- abdominal injury;
- fractures;
- or toxic exposure.

The leader should repeatedly return to the mechanism and the overall trauma assessment.

---

### Reassurance From a Normal Oxygen Saturation

A normal or near-normal pulse oximetry reading does not exclude significant toxic exposure.

Clinical context remains essential.

---

### Waiting for Airway Obstruction

Waiting until severe airway obstruction develops may make airway management considerably more difficult.

The leader should anticipate deterioration rather than respond only after complete airway failure.

---

### Underestimating Burn Size

Visual estimation from one body region may underestimate the total injury.

A systematic assessment should be performed.

---

### Excessive Fluid Administration

Large burns may require substantial resuscitation, but excessive fluid administration can produce significant complications.

Fluid therapy should therefore be guided by an appropriate protocol and repeated clinical assessment.

---

### Forgetting Hypothermia

Repeated exposure during examination and procedures can produce significant heat loss.

The team should actively prevent hypothermia from the beginning of treatment.

---

## Case Resolution

Returning to the opening scenario, the patient has several features that raise concern for significant inhalation injury.

The combination of:

- enclosed-space fire;
- facial burns;
- soot contamination;
- hoarseness;
- respiratory distress;
- and altered consciousness

should prompt early senior airway assessment and preparation for deterioration.

The team should simultaneously assess for:

- toxic exposure;
- associated trauma;
- burn extent;
- circumferential injury;
- and evolving respiratory compromise.

The leader should not wait for complete airway obstruction before acting if the overall trajectory indicates that airway protection is likely to become increasingly difficult.

At the same time, the patient should continue to receive systematic trauma assessment and appropriate resuscitation.

The immediate objective is not simply to treat the visible burn.

It is to prevent a potentially reversible injury from becoming a fatal airway, respiratory, toxicological, or circulatory problem.

---

## Clinical Pearl

> **In major burns, treat the mechanism and the physiology—not just the skin.**

A burned patient may deteriorate because of airway oedema, inhalation injury, toxic exposure, associated trauma, hypovolaemia, or hypothermia.

The Trauma Team Leader must continuously integrate:

**Mechanism → Injury pattern → Physiology → Treatment → Response → Trajectory**

---

## Key Takeaways

- Major burns should be managed using trauma principles.
- Airway compromise may evolve rapidly, particularly after inhalation injury.
- Facial burns, hoarseness, soot, and enclosed-space exposure should increase concern for airway and inhalation injury.
- Pulse oximetry does not reliably exclude carbon monoxide exposure.
- Significant burns may require formal fluid resuscitation.
- Burn size should be estimated systematically.
- Circumferential burns may compromise ventilation or limb perfusion.
- Electrical and chemical burns require mechanism-specific assessment.
- Associated trauma must not be overlooked.
- Hypothermia prevention is an essential component of early burn management.
- Definitive burn care and transfer should be considered early.
- Reassessment remains essential throughout the patient's journey.
- The Trauma Team Leader must anticipate deterioration rather than waiting for physiological collapse.
