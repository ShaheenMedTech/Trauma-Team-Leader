# Chapter 9 — Communication During Resuscitation

> **The team leader does not communicate to sound authoritative. The team leader communicates so that the team can understand what is happening, what needs to happen next, who is responsible, and whether it has happened.**

---

# 1. Clinical Scenario

A 34-year-old man arrives following a high-speed road traffic collision.

The trauma team is assembled and the patient is transferred onto the resuscitation trolley.

The airway doctor reports:

> “Airway currently patent.”

The nurse reports:

> “Heart rate 132, blood pressure 78 over 46.”

At the same time:

* The surgeon is asking for an ultrasound machine.
* The anaesthetist is preparing for intubation.
* A nurse is attempting intravenous access.
* The radiographer is asking whether a chest X-ray is required.
* The blood bank is calling to confirm that blood is available.
* The patient is becoming increasingly agitated.

Several conversations are occurring simultaneously.

The trauma team leader says:

> “Everyone stop for a moment.”

The room becomes quiet.

> “We have a shocked trauma patient with suspected major haemorrhage. Anaesthesia, prepare for airway management but do not proceed until I confirm.
>
> Two large-bore IVs and blood sampling now.
>
> Nurse one, activate the major haemorrhage protocol. Confirm when activated.
>
> Surgeon, assess for external haemorrhage and prepare for pelvic assessment.
>
> Radiographer, stand by for chest and pelvic imaging.
>
> I want a rapid primary survey. Call out every critical finding.”

The nurse replies:

> “Major haemorrhage protocol activated.”

The leader responds:

> “Confirmed.”

The team now has a shared understanding of the immediate priorities.

This is the purpose of effective communication during trauma resuscitation.

---

# 2. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain why communication is a clinical intervention during trauma resuscitation.
2. Establish and maintain a shared mental model within the trauma team.
3. Give clear, concise and unambiguous instructions.
4. Use closed-loop communication reliably.
5. Distinguish between commands, call-outs, check-backs and updates.
6. Manage communication during simultaneous high-priority interventions.
7. Prevent information overload and communication failure.
8. Encourage team members to speak up when they identify danger.
9. Correct misunderstandings without creating unnecessary conflict.
10. Communicate effectively during transitions between resuscitation phases.
11. Maintain communication with clinicians and departments outside the resuscitation room.
12. Recognise common communication failures that threaten patient safety.

---

# 3. Why Communication Matters

Trauma resuscitation is a team activity.

The patient may require simultaneous management of:

* Airway
* Breathing
* Circulation
* Haemorrhage control
* Neurological assessment
* Exposure
* Imaging
* Blood products
* Analgesia
* Procedures
* Operative or interventional planning

No single clinician can perform all of these tasks.

The trauma team therefore functions as a distributed clinical system.

The team leader coordinates this system through communication.

Poor communication can result in:

* Delayed interventions
* Duplicate interventions
* Missed findings
* Incorrect assumptions
* Failure to recognise deterioration
* Failure to escalate
* Medication or blood-product errors
* Delayed surgery
* Delayed imaging
* Loss of situational awareness
* Conflicting priorities
* Unclear responsibility

In contrast, effective communication allows the team to work as a single coordinated unit.

---

# 4. Communication Is a Clinical Intervention

Communication should not be considered separate from patient care.

A clear instruction can result in:

* Rapid haemorrhage control
* Earlier administration of blood products
* Timely airway intervention
* Faster recognition of tension pneumothorax
* Earlier activation of surgical resources
* Safer transfer to CT
* Earlier transfer to theatre

The trauma team leader therefore needs to communicate deliberately.

The objective is not to speak more.

The objective is to communicate **better**.

---

# 5. The Trauma Team Leader's Communication Goals

During resuscitation, communication should answer five questions:

### 1. What is happening?

> “This patient is hypotensive with suspected haemorrhagic shock.”

### 2. What matters most now?

> “Our immediate priority is haemorrhage control.”

### 3. What needs to happen?

> “Activate the major haemorrhage protocol.”

### 4. Who is doing it?

> “Nurse two, activate the protocol.”

### 5. Has it happened?

> “Major haemorrhage activated.”

This final question is frequently forgotten.

A task being **assigned** does not mean that it has been **completed**.

---

# 6. Shared Mental Model

A trauma team performs better when members understand the same clinical picture.

The shared mental model should include:

* Mechanism of injury
* Major injuries suspected
* Current physiological state
* Immediate threats
* Interventions already performed
* Interventions currently occurring
* Outstanding tasks
* Anticipated next steps
* Destination

The team leader should continuously update this model.

For example:

> “We have a high-energy blunt trauma patient with ongoing haemodynamic instability despite initial blood products. There is no obvious external haemorrhage. FAST is positive. We are treating this as intra-abdominal haemorrhage and preparing for definitive haemorrhage control.”

This statement gives the team a common framework.

---

# 7. The Communication Loop

Effective communication during trauma resuscitation can be viewed as a continuous loop:

**Information → Interpretation → Instruction → Confirmation → Reassessment**

For example:

**Information**

> “Blood pressure is 68 systolic.”

**Interpretation**

> “The patient remains in profound shock.”

**Instruction**

> “Continue blood products and prepare for immediate haemorrhage control.”

**Confirmation**

> “Blood products continuing.”

**Reassessment**

> “Repeat blood pressure now.”

This loop should continue throughout the resuscitation.

---

# 8. Closed-Loop Communication

Closed-loop communication is one of the most important communication behaviours in trauma resuscitation.

It consists of three components:

### Step 1 — The leader gives a specific instruction

> “Nurse one, give 1 gram tranexamic acid.”

### Step 2 — The recipient repeats or acknowledges the instruction

> “1 gram tranexamic acid.”

### Step 3 — The recipient confirms completion

> “Tranexamic acid given.”

The leader can then acknowledge:

> “Thank you.”

---

# 9. Why Closed-Loop Communication Matters

Without closed-loop communication, the leader may assume that an instruction has been understood and completed.

For example:

> “Get blood.”

Silence.

The leader assumes blood has been requested.

Five minutes later:

> “Where are the blood products?”

The answer:

> “I thought someone else had called the blood bank.”

This is a communication failure.

A better instruction is:

> “Nurse two, call the blood bank and activate the major haemorrhage protocol. Tell me when blood is being issued.”

The task has:

* An owner
* A defined action
* A defined endpoint

---

# 10. Assigning Tasks to Individuals

Avoid vague commands such as:

> “Someone get IV access.”

Instead:

> “Nurse one, obtain two large-bore IVs.”

Avoid:

> “Can we get some blood?”

Instead:

> “Nurse two, activate the major haemorrhage protocol.”

Avoid:

> “Someone call surgery.”

Instead:

> “Doctor Ahmed, contact the trauma surgeon and tell them we have persistent shock with suspected intra-abdominal haemorrhage.”

Specificity reduces ambiguity.

---

# 11. Name the Person

Whenever possible, address the individual responsible for the task.

Compare:

> “Can someone check the blood pressure?”

with:

> “Sarah, repeat the blood pressure now.”

The second instruction is much less likely to be missed.

Using names also reduces the risk that multiple team members assume somebody else is responsible.

---

# 12. One Instruction at a Time

Under pressure, leaders may issue multiple instructions rapidly:

> “Get blood, check the pelvis, do the FAST, call surgery, prepare the airway and get another IV.”

The team may remember only some of these instructions.

A better approach is to prioritise.

> “Nurse one, activate the major haemorrhage protocol.”

Pause.

> “Nurse two, obtain two large-bore IVs and send blood samples.”

Pause.

> “Doctor, perform FAST and call out the findings.”

This makes task ownership clearer.

---

# 13. Prioritise Before Communicating

Not every piece of information deserves immediate verbalisation.

The leader should distinguish:

### Critical information

Requires immediate communication.

Examples:

* Massive haemorrhage
* Airway obstruction
* Tension pneumothorax
* Severe hypoxia
* Profound hypotension
* Sudden neurological deterioration

### Important information

Should be communicated promptly but may not require interruption of a critical procedure.

### Background information

Can be deferred until the team has capacity.

This prevents communication overload.

---

# 14. Call-Outs

A **call-out** is the deliberate verbal communication of important clinical information to the whole team.

Examples:

> “Blood pressure is 62 systolic.”

> “There is active external haemorrhage from the left thigh.”

> “GCS is now 7.”

> “FAST is positive in the right upper quadrant.”

Call-outs are particularly useful when information may change the actions of several team members.

---

# 15. Call-Outs Should Be Concise

Avoid lengthy descriptions during critical moments.

Instead of:

> “I have just checked the abdomen and there seems to be some tenderness and perhaps some guarding although I am not entirely sure…”

Say:

> “Abdomen rigid.”

The team can then respond appropriately.

---

# 16. Check-Backs

A check-back verifies information.

Example:

> “What was the latest haemoglobin?”

> “82.”

> “82, confirmed.”

Or:

> “How much blood has been given?”

> “Four units.”

> “Four units of red cells, confirmed.”

Check-backs are particularly valuable when:

* Information is unexpected
* Numbers are critical
* Communication occurred in a noisy environment
* The clinical situation is changing rapidly

---

# 17. Read-Back of Critical Information

Some information should be repeated to minimise error.

Examples include:

* Drug doses
* Blood-product volumes
* Critical laboratory values
* Patient identifiers
* Imaging findings
* Procedural instructions

For example:

> “Give 100 micrograms fentanyl.”

Response:

> “100 micrograms fentanyl.”

This creates an opportunity to detect an error before administration.

---

# 18. Communicating During the Primary Survey

The primary survey should generate a structured flow of information.

The team leader should establish a predictable communication pattern.

For example:

> “Airway?”

Response:

> “Patent. Speaking.”

> “Breathing?”

> “Respiratory rate 30, oxygen saturation 91%, reduced air entry on the left.”

> “Circulation?”

> “Heart rate 138, blood pressure 72 systolic, active bleeding from the left leg.”

> “Disability?”

> “GCS 12.”

> “Exposure?”

> “No additional major external injuries identified.”

This allows the leader to maintain situational awareness.

---

# 19. Call Out Positive Findings

A common error is to communicate only when something is abnormal after being specifically asked.

Instead, important positive findings should be called out immediately.

For example:

> “Airway obstructed.”

or

> “Chest movement absent on the right.”

or

> “No radial pulse.”

The person identifying the life-threatening problem should not wait for the leader to ask the next question.

---

# 20. Speaking Up

Every team member should be empowered to communicate concerns.

A junior nurse may notice:

> “The blood pressure has fallen.”

A radiographer may notice:

> “The patient is becoming more cyanosed.”

A technician may notice:

> “The oxygen cylinder is nearly empty.”

A junior doctor may recognise:

> “This patient may have a tension pneumothorax.”

The trauma leader should create an environment in which such observations are welcomed.

---

# 21. Speaking Up Is a Safety Behaviour

The team should understand:

> **Hierarchy must never prevent escalation of a safety-critical concern.**

If a team member believes the patient is deteriorating, they should be able to say so clearly.

A useful escalation sequence is:

1. State the concern.
2. State the clinical evidence.
3. State the requested action.

For example:

> “I am concerned about the airway. The patient is becoming less responsive and the oxygen saturation is falling. I think we need to prepare for definitive airway management now.”

---

# 22. The Leader Must Listen

Communication is not simply issuing instructions.

The leader must actively receive information.

Poor leadership may sound like:

> “Yes, yes, continue.”

while the team member is trying to communicate deterioration.

Good leadership requires:

* Listening
* Acknowledgement
* Clarification
* Reassessment
* Action

A leader who does not listen effectively may miss the most important information in the room.

---

# 23. Avoid Communication Tunnels

During a technically difficult procedure, clinicians can become focused on the procedure and lose awareness of the wider resuscitation.

For example, during intubation:

* Blood pressure may fall.
* Oxygen saturation may deteriorate.
* External haemorrhage may continue.
* Blood products may arrive.
* The patient may develop arrhythmia.

The trauma team leader must maintain the wider picture.

Useful communication:

> “Airway team, continue. I am monitoring the circulation. Blood pressure is now 58 systolic. Continue blood products and prepare for immediate haemorrhage control.”

---

# 24. Managing Simultaneous Conversations

Multiple conversations are inevitable.

The leader should intervene when communication becomes unsafe.

A useful technique is:

> “Stop for a moment.”

Then:

> “One issue at a time.”

or:

> “I need the room quiet for the next thirty seconds.”

This is not rude.

It is a patient-safety intervention.

---

# 25. The Power of Silence

There are moments when the best communication is deliberate silence.

During:

* Critical airway procedures
* Interpretation of a difficult ultrasound
* Confirmation of a pulse
* Critical rhythm assessment
* Major clinical deterioration

the leader may say:

> “Quiet for a moment.”

This allows the team to concentrate on critical information.

---

# 26. Avoid Ambiguous Language

Avoid:

* “Maybe”
* “Someone”
* “Can we…?”
* “We should probably…”
* “I think perhaps…”

when a clear action is required.

Instead:

> “Dr Smith, perform a FAST now.”

> “Nurse, activate the major haemorrhage protocol.”

> “Anaesthesia, prepare for RSI.”

Clear language reduces uncertainty.

---

# 27. Avoid Unnecessary Shouting

Volume does not equal leadership.

Shouting may:

* Increase stress
* Reduce listening
* Encourage defensive behaviour
* Make communication less precise
* Cause quieter team members to stop speaking

A calm, deliberate voice is often more effective.

The leader should increase volume only when necessary to overcome environmental noise.

---

# 28. Maintain a Calm Tone

The trauma leader may be concerned or even alarmed.

However, uncontrolled emotional expression can spread through the team.

Compare:

> “This is a disaster! What is happening?”

with:

> “The patient is deteriorating. We have persistent shock despite resuscitation. We need to move to definitive haemorrhage control.”

The second statement communicates urgency without creating chaos.

---

# 29. Communicating Uncertainty

The leader does not need to pretend to know everything.

Useful language includes:

> “The source of bleeding is not yet confirmed.”

> “We are treating this as haemorrhagic shock while we continue assessment.”

> “The diagnosis remains uncertain, but the patient is unstable and requires immediate action.”

This is better than false certainty.

---

# 30. Separate Facts From Interpretation

For example:

**Fact:**

> “Blood pressure is 65 systolic.”

**Interpretation:**

> “This is consistent with severe shock.”

**Plan:**

> “Continue blood-product resuscitation and prepare for definitive haemorrhage control.”

This structure helps the team understand the reasoning behind decisions.

---

# 31. Communicating Changes in Priority

Trauma priorities can change rapidly.

The leader should explicitly announce major changes.

For example:

> “New priority: airway.”

or:

> “The immediate priority is now haemorrhage control.”

or:

> “We are stopping the secondary survey. The patient is deteriorating.”

Without explicit communication, different team members may continue working toward different priorities.

---

# 32. Re-Prioritisation Statements

Useful phrases include:

> “Our priority has changed.”

> “Stop that task for now.”

> “We are moving to the next priority.”

> “The patient is deteriorating; return to the primary survey.”

> “We are no longer stable for CT.”

These statements create a shared change in direction.

---

# 33. Communication During Deterioration

When the patient deteriorates:

### 1. Announce the deterioration

> “The patient is deteriorating.”

### 2. State the objective finding

> “Blood pressure is now 55 systolic.”

### 3. Reassess immediately

> “Return to the primary survey.”

### 4. Reassign priorities

> “Airway and circulation are now the immediate priorities.”

### 5. Allocate tasks

> “Anaesthesia, reassess airway.”

> “Surgery, reassess haemorrhage.”

> “Nurse, repeat observations.”

This prevents the team from continuing the previous plan automatically.

---

# 34. Communication During Procedures

Before an invasive procedure, the leader should ensure:

* Indication is clear
* Operator is identified
* Equipment is available
* Required assistance is available
* Major risks are recognised
* Team knows what is happening

For example:

> “We are proceeding with right thoracostomy for suspected tension pneumothorax. Surgery will perform the procedure. Anaesthesia, continue airway support. Nurse, monitor blood pressure and saturation and report changes immediately.”

---

# 35. Communication Around Blood Products

Blood-product administration requires particularly clear communication.

The team should know:

* What is being administered
* How much has been administered
* What is currently running
* What is expected next
* Whether the major haemorrhage protocol remains active

Useful call-out:

> “Four units of red cells and four units of plasma have been administered. Two units of red cells are currently running.”

This creates a shared understanding.

---

# 36. Communicating With the Blood Bank

The leader should avoid assuming that the blood bank knows the current clinical situation.

Relevant information may include:

* Patient identification
* Major haemorrhage activation
* Estimated ongoing blood loss
* Current requirements
* Urgency
* Special transfusion requirements when relevant

The designated team member should communicate this information and confirm receipt.

---

# 37. Communication With Surgery

The surgical team needs concise clinical information.

A useful format:

> **Mechanism → physiology → suspected injury → interventions → response → current requirement**

Example:

> “High-speed blunt trauma. Persistent systolic pressure around 70 despite blood products. FAST positive. No major external haemorrhage. We have given four units of red cells and four units of plasma. The patient remains unstable. We need definitive haemorrhage control.”

This is more useful than a lengthy chronological narrative.

---

# 38. Communication With Anaesthesia

Important information includes:

* Current airway status
* Oxygenation
* Ventilation
* Haemodynamics
* Suspected injuries
* Cervical spine concerns
* Anticipated procedures
* Current blood-product resuscitation

The leader should communicate when priorities change.

---

# 39. Communication With Radiology

Before imaging, communicate:

* Patient stability
* Suspected injuries
* Immediate clinical question
* Required imaging
* Whether contrast is required
* Whether the patient can safely undergo imaging

For unstable patients, the team should not allow a routine imaging pathway to override immediate life-saving management.

---

# 40. Communication During Transfer

Transfer is a high-risk period.

Before leaving the resuscitation room, the team should know:

* Destination
* Clinical status
* Oxygen requirement
* Monitoring requirements
* IV access
* Blood products
* Infusions
* Airway status
* Personnel accompanying the patient
* Equipment required
* Contingency plan

The leader should explicitly announce:

> “We are transferring to CT. The patient remains unstable. Anaesthesia will manage the airway. Nurse one will continue blood products. Surgery will accompany the patient.”

---

# 41. Communication During CT

The trauma team leader should maintain situational awareness.

CT may reveal:

* Unexpected haemorrhage
* Expanding haematoma
* Major vascular injury
* Intracranial injury
* Spinal injury
* Thoracic injury

A major finding should be communicated immediately.

> “CT demonstrates active intra-abdominal bleeding. We are not returning to the resuscitation room; we are proceeding directly to theatre.”

---

# 42. Handover Is a Communication Procedure

Handover should not be treated as an administrative task.

It is a transfer of:

* Information
* Responsibility
* Clinical priorities
* Outstanding tasks
* Risk

A poor handover can undo good resuscitation.

---

# 43. Structured Handover

A practical trauma handover should include:

### Identification

> “Thirty-four-year-old male.”

### Mechanism

> “High-speed road traffic collision.”

### Major injuries

> “Suspected intra-abdominal haemorrhage and left femoral injury.”

### Physiology

> “Persistent hypotension.”

### Interventions

> “Airway maintained, two large-bore IVs, blood products commenced.”

### Response

> “Transient improvement followed by recurrent hypotension.”

### Current priorities

> “Definitive haemorrhage control.”

### Outstanding issues

> “Crossmatch pending and ongoing transfusion.”

---

# 44. Communication With the Patient

Even during major trauma, communication with the patient matters.

When possible, explain:

> “You have been involved in a serious accident. We are assessing you for internal injuries and treating the problems that could threaten your life.”

If the patient is conscious, avoid discussing them as though they are absent.

The patient should be treated with dignity.

---

# 45. Communication With Family

Family communication should be:

* Honest
* Clear
* Compassionate
* Appropriate to the situation

Avoid premature reassurance.

Instead of:

> “Everything will be fine.”

say:

> “They are seriously injured. The team is currently treating the most immediate threats, and we will update you as soon as we know more.”

---

# 46. Common Communication Failure: Vague Task Assignment

### Failure

> “Someone get blood.”

### Why it fails

No clear owner.

### Better

> “Nurse two, activate the major haemorrhage protocol and confirm when it is active.”

---

# 47. Common Communication Failure: No Confirmation

### Failure

> “Give tranexamic acid.”

No response.

### Risk

The leader does not know whether the instruction was heard or completed.

### Better

> “Give 1 gram tranexamic acid.”

> “1 gram tranexamic acid.”

> “Given.”

---

# 48. Common Communication Failure: Information Overload

### Failure

The leader gives ten instructions in rapid succession.

### Risk

Important tasks may be forgotten.

### Better

Prioritise tasks and confirm completion.

---

# 49. Common Communication Failure: Failure to Escalate

A junior team member notices deterioration but remains silent.

### Why?

* Hierarchy
* Fear of criticism
* Uncertainty
* Previous negative experience

### Leader's responsibility

Create a culture where speaking up is expected.

---

# 50. Common Communication Failure: Parallel Conversations

Multiple people speak simultaneously.

### Risk

Critical information is missed.

### Leader response

> “One conversation at a time.”

or:

> “I need the room quiet while we reassess.”

---

# 51. Common Communication Failure: Assumptions

> “I thought surgery had been called.”

> “I thought anaesthesia knew.”

> “I thought the blood was coming.”

These statements are warning signs.

Critical tasks should be explicitly assigned and confirmed.

---

# 52. Common Communication Failure: Failure to Update the Plan

The initial plan may become inappropriate.

For example:

> “We are going to CT.”

But the patient subsequently develops profound hypotension.

The leader must explicitly change the plan:

> “The patient is no longer stable for CT. We are prioritising haemorrhage control.”

---

# 53. Common Communication Failure: Communication Without Context

A laboratory result alone may not be meaningful.

> “Haemoglobin is 82.”

Better:

> “Haemoglobin is 82 and the patient remains hypotensive with suspected ongoing haemorrhage.”

Context helps the team interpret information.

---

# 54. The Leader's Communication Checklist

During resuscitation, ask:

### Situation

* Does everyone know what is happening?

### Priority

* Does everyone know what matters most now?

### Ownership

* Does every critical task have a named owner?

### Confirmation

* Has completion been confirmed?

### Escalation

* Can team members raise concerns?

### Reassessment

* Are we communicating changes in physiology?

### Plan

* Does the team know the next step?

### Destination

* Does everyone know where the patient is going?

---

# 55. Communication Under Extreme Pressure

As physiological instability increases, communication should generally become:

* Shorter
* More structured
* More explicit
* More repetitive when necessary
* More focused on immediate priorities

Avoid allowing increasing clinical urgency to produce increasingly chaotic communication.

A useful principle is:

> **The more chaotic the situation becomes, the more structured communication should become.**

---

# 56. The Trauma Team Leader's Voice

The leader should sound:

* Calm
* Clear
* Direct
* Respectful
* Decisive

The leader does not need to sound:

* Aggressive
* Angry
* Loud
* Dominating
* Certain about everything

Authority comes from clarity and coordination.

---

# 57. Case-Based Discussion 1 — Missed Instruction

A patient arrives with massive external haemorrhage.

The leader says:

> “Someone apply pressure to the wound.”

Nobody responds.

Two minutes later the wound is still bleeding.

### Questions

1. What went wrong?
2. How could the instruction have been phrased?
3. How should completion have been confirmed?

### Suggested answer

The instruction lacked task ownership.

Better:

> “Nurse Ahmed, apply direct pressure to the wound now and tell me when bleeding is controlled.”

Response:

> “Pressure applied.”

Later:

> “Bleeding controlled.”

---

# 58. Case-Based Discussion 2 — Speaking Up

During resuscitation, the anaesthetic assistant says:

> “The oxygen saturation is falling.”

The team leader is discussing the CT findings with the surgeon.

The leader ignores the comment.

The saturation falls further.

### Questions

1. What communication failure occurred?
2. What should the leader have done?
3. How can the team culture be improved?

### Suggested answer

The leader failed to acknowledge a safety-critical call-out.

The appropriate response would be:

> “Thank you. What is the current saturation?”

Then immediately reassess the airway and breathing.

Team members should be encouraged to communicate deterioration immediately.

---

# 59. Case-Based Discussion 3 — Competing Priorities

A patient has:

* Severe traumatic brain injury
* Hypotension
* Suspected abdominal haemorrhage
* Worsening oxygenation

The team begins discussing CT.

### Leader response

The leader should stop the discussion and establish immediate priorities.

> “The patient is unstable. CT is not our immediate priority. Return to the primary survey. Airway, oxygenation and haemorrhage control first.”

The exact sequence of interventions will depend on the patient's physiology and injuries, but the communication principle remains the same:

**Do not allow a downstream plan to distract from an immediate life threat.**

---

# 60. Case-Based Discussion 4 — Communication Breakdown

Three team members believe that the major haemorrhage protocol has been activated.

It has not.

### Why did this happen?

Likely causes include:

* Ambiguous instruction
* No named individual
* No closed-loop confirmation
* Assumption that another team member had completed the task

### Correct approach

> “Nurse two, activate the major haemorrhage protocol now.”

> “Activating now.”

> “Confirm when active.”

> “Protocol active.”

> “Confirmed.”

---

# 61. Case-Based Discussion 5 — The Quiet Team Member

A junior doctor appears concerned but does not speak.

The leader notices.

A useful approach is:

> “Dr Ahmed, what are you concerned about?”

This invites contribution without undermining the individual.

The leader should actively seek input when:

* The diagnosis is uncertain
* The patient is deteriorating
* The team appears confused
* A major decision is being made

---

# 62. Practical Communication Phrases

Useful phrases for the trauma team leader include:

### To establish control

> “Let's pause for a moment.”

> “I need everyone's attention.”

> “One conversation at a time.”

### To establish priorities

> “Our immediate priority is haemorrhage control.”

> “Return to the primary survey.”

### To assign tasks

> “Dr Smith, assess the airway.”

> “Nurse two, activate the major haemorrhage protocol.”

### To confirm

> “Repeat that back to me.”

> “Confirm when complete.”

### To encourage speaking up

> “Does anyone see a problem with this plan?”

> “Is there anything I have missed?”

> “If anyone is concerned, say so now.”

### To change direction

> “The situation has changed.”

> “We are stopping the current plan.”

> “The patient is no longer stable for CT.”

### To maintain situational awareness

> “Give me the latest observations.”

> “What has been given so far?”

> “What are we waiting for?”

---

# 63. Communication and Cognitive Load

Trauma resuscitation creates substantial cognitive load.

The leader may simultaneously process:

* Vital signs
* Mechanism
* Examination
* Imaging
* Laboratory results
* Procedures
* Blood products
* Team performance
* Available resources
* Destination decisions

Structured communication reduces cognitive burden by externalising information.

The team becomes an information-processing system rather than relying entirely on the memory of one individual.

---

# 64. Communication as a Leadership Tool

Communication allows the leader to:

* Coordinate
* Prioritise
* Delegate
* Monitor
* Reassess
* Escalate
* Correct
* Redirect
* Maintain team focus

Therefore:

> **Communication is one of the primary tools through which trauma leadership is exercised.**

---

# 65. The Three-Level Communication Model

The trauma leader can think about communication at three levels.

### Level 1 — Patient

What is happening physiologically?

### Level 2 — Task

What needs to happen next?

### Level 3 — Team

Who is doing it, and has it been completed?

A leader who manages all three levels is more likely to maintain control of the resuscitation.

---

# 66. Communication During Transition Points

Communication is particularly important when the team moves between phases.

Examples:

* Arrival → primary survey
* Primary survey → secondary survey
* Resuscitation → CT
* Resuscitation → theatre
* Theatre → ICU
* Emergency department → transfer centre

At each transition, explicitly state:

1. Current condition
2. Current priorities
3. Outstanding tasks
4. Destination
5. Responsible team

# 67. The End-of-Resuscitation Brief

Before leaving the resuscitation room, the leader should briefly summarise:

> “We have a 34-year-old male following high-speed trauma. Major injuries include suspected intra-abdominal haemorrhage and left femoral injury. He remains haemodynamically unstable despite blood-product resuscitation. Airway is currently maintained. The patient is going directly to theatre for definitive haemorrhage control. Anaesthesia and surgery are accompanying him. Ongoing blood products will continue during transfer.”

This provides a shared final mental model.

# 68. Key Takeaways

1. Communication is a clinical intervention.
2. The trauma leader should establish a shared mental model.
3. Critical tasks should have a named owner.
4. Use closed-loop communication for important instructions.
5. Call out critical findings immediately.
6. Use check-backs for important information.
7. Avoid vague and ambiguous instructions.
8. One conversation at a time may be necessary during critical moments.
9. Encourage all team members to speak up.
10. Listen actively to the team.
11. Communicate changes in priority explicitly.
12. Do not allow downstream plans to distract from immediate threats.
13. Communication should become more structured as pressure increases.
14. Handover is a transfer of clinical responsibility, not merely information.
15. A calm, clear and decisive leader improves team coordination.

# 70. Final Principle

> **A trauma team can possess excellent clinical knowledge and technical skills yet still fail if the information does not move effectively between team members.**

The trauma team leader therefore has a responsibility not only to make decisions, but to make those decisions **visible, understandable and actionable**.

The goal is simple:

> **Everyone should know what is happening, what matters now, what they are responsible for, and when the plan has changed.**
