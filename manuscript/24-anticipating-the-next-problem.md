# Chapter 24 — Anticipating the Next Problem

## Introduction

A strong trauma team leader does not only respond to the problem that is visible now.

The leader is continuously asking:

> **What is most likely to happen next, and what can we do about it before it becomes an emergency?**

This is one of the defining differences between reactive leadership and anticipatory leadership.

Trauma resuscitation is dynamic. A patient who appears relatively stable can deteriorate rapidly. A problem that has been controlled temporarily may recur. A seemingly successful intervention may create a new problem. A team that has completed one task may suddenly be confronted with another.

The trauma team leader therefore needs to maintain two simultaneous perspectives:

1. **What is happening now?**
2. **What is likely to happen next?**

The first prevents the team from missing the immediate threat.

The second prevents the team from being surprised by the next threat.

Anticipation is not prediction with certainty.

It is the disciplined identification of plausible future problems, followed by preparation for those problems before they become critical.

---

# 24.1 The Core Principle

The central principle is:

> **Do not wait for the next problem to become obvious before preparing for it.**

During major trauma, deterioration may occur because of:

- ongoing haemorrhage
- worsening airway obstruction
- progressive respiratory failure
- tension pneumothorax
- cardiac tamponade
- traumatic brain injury
- hypothermia
- coagulopathy
- metabolic deterioration
- medication effects
- failed vascular access
- loss of monitoring
- equipment failure
- delayed imaging
- delayed operative or interventional treatment
- communication failure
- exhaustion or cognitive overload within the team

The leader should continuously consider which of these problems is becoming more likely.

Anticipation converts:

> **surprise → preparation**

and:

> **crisis → controlled response**

---

# 24.2 Reactive Versus Anticipatory Leadership

A reactive leader waits for events.

An anticipatory leader prepares for them.

### Reactive pattern

```text
Problem appears
        ↓
Team recognises problem
        ↓
Leader gives instructions
        ↓
Equipment is located
        ↓
Team prepares intervention
        ↓
Treatment begins
````

### Anticipatory pattern

```text
Risk recognised
        ↓
Possible deterioration anticipated
        ↓
Equipment and personnel prepared
        ↓
Team informed
        ↓
Problem develops
        ↓
Treatment begins immediately
```

The difference may only be measured in seconds.

In major trauma, seconds may matter.

---

# 24.3 Anticipation Begins With the Mechanism

The mechanism of injury provides an early forecast of potential problems.

The leader should ask:

* What happened?
* How much energy was involved?
* What structures may have been injured?
* What injuries are already identified?
* What injuries may still be occult?
* What complications are likely to develop?
* What treatment has already been given?
* What treatment may become necessary?

Mechanism should therefore influence the leader's mental model from the beginning.

Examples include:

### High-energy blunt trauma

Potential future problems include:

* occult internal haemorrhage
* thoracic injury
* abdominal injury
* pelvic haemorrhage
* spinal injury
* traumatic brain injury
* deterioration after initially normal observations

### Penetrating trauma

Potential future problems include:

* ongoing haemorrhage
* airway compromise
* pneumothorax
* haemothorax
* vascular injury
* cardiac injury
* delayed physiological deterioration

### Crush injury

Potential future problems include:

* airway or respiratory compromise
* occult bleeding
* compartment syndrome
* rhabdomyolysis
* hyperkalaemia
* renal injury
* reperfusion-related deterioration

The purpose is not to memorise every possible complication.

The purpose is to develop a habit of asking:

> **Given what happened, what could happen next?**

---

# 24.4 The Leader Should Think in Time

Trauma problems occur on different timescales.

Some occur within seconds.

Others evolve over minutes.

Others become apparent over hours.

A useful mental model is:

```text
NOW
↓
Next few minutes
↓
Next hour
↓
Definitive treatment
↓
Potential complications
```

The leader should maintain awareness across all of these time horizons.

---

# 24.5 The Immediate Future

The first question is:

> **What could kill this patient in the next few minutes?**

Examples include:

* airway obstruction
* severe hypoxaemia
* tension pneumothorax
* catastrophic haemorrhage
* profound shock
* cardiac tamponade
* rapidly expanding external bleeding

The leader should identify these threats early.

Preparation may include:

* positioning the airway team
* preparing airway equipment
* ensuring suction is available
* preparing oxygen delivery
* obtaining appropriate vascular access
* activating blood products
* preparing haemorrhage control equipment
* preparing thoracic intervention equipment
* ensuring monitoring is functioning

The aim is not to perform every intervention immediately.

The aim is to avoid losing time when intervention becomes necessary.

---

# 24.6 The Next Five to Ten Minutes

The leader should then ask:

> **What is likely to become the next major problem once the immediate threat is controlled?**

Examples:

* persistent hypotension
* ongoing internal bleeding
* failure of initial airway management
* worsening ventilation
* increasing oxygen requirement
* deteriorating neurological status
* hypothermia
* inadequate vascular access
* need for urgent imaging
* need for operative intervention

This allows the team to prepare the next phase while the current phase is still being managed.

---

# 24.7 The Next Major Decision

Every major trauma resuscitation contains decision points.

The leader should identify them before they arrive.

Examples include:

* Does this patient require definitive airway control?
* Is haemorrhage continuing?
* Does the patient require massive transfusion?
* Is imaging appropriate?
* Can the patient safely leave the resuscitation area?
* Is immediate operative intervention required?
* Is interventional radiology required?
* Does the patient require transfer?
* Is the current treatment actually working?

A useful question is:

> **What decision will we need to make next?**

Then:

> **What information will we need to make that decision?**

And:

> **Who is responsible for obtaining it?**

---

# 24.8 Anticipating Failure

One of the most important forms of anticipation is preparing for treatment failure.

Every major intervention should have an implicit question:

> **What will we do if this does not work?**

Examples:

### Airway

If the initial airway strategy fails:

* Who will recognise failure?
* Who will lead the next attempt?
* Is alternative equipment immediately available?
* Is a surgical airway strategy understood?
* Is suction functioning?
* Is the patient adequately oxygenated?

### Breathing

If respiratory status deteriorates:

* Is the cause recognised?
* Is the chest being reassessed?
* Is oxygenation adequate?
* Is ventilation adequate?
* Could a pneumothorax have developed?
* Has an intervention failed?

### Circulation

If blood pressure remains poor:

* Is haemorrhage continuing?
* Is vascular access adequate?
* Is blood available?
* Is the source of bleeding identified?
* Is definitive haemorrhage control being pursued?

### Neurology

If neurological status deteriorates:

* Is this due to hypoxia?
* Hypotension?
* Intracranial injury?
* Seizure?
* Medication?
* Metabolic disturbance?

Anticipation means having the next step mentally available before failure occurs.

---

# 24.9 The "If This, Then That" Model

A simple structure is:

> **If X happens, then we will do Y.**

Examples:

> If the blood pressure continues to fall, reassess haemorrhage control and escalate the resuscitation strategy.

> If oxygenation deteriorates, immediately reassess airway and breathing.

> If the patient becomes less responsive, reassess airway, oxygenation, ventilation and circulation rather than assuming neurological deterioration alone.

> If the first vascular access strategy fails, move rapidly to an alternative strategy.

> If the patient cannot be stabilised sufficiently for imaging, reconsider whether immediate definitive intervention is required.

This creates pre-planned responses.

---

# 24.10 Anticipating the Next Problem Is Not Tunnel Vision

Anticipation must not become fixation.

A leader may correctly predict one problem and still miss another.

For example:

> "This patient is bleeding."

may be true.

But the team may simultaneously need to consider:

* airway compromise
* hypothermia
* traumatic brain injury
* chest injury
* coagulopathy
* pelvic injury
* medication effects

The leader should therefore avoid becoming attached to a single explanation.

The mental model should remain dynamic.

---

# 24.11 Maintain a Working Differential

The leader should maintain a working list of:

1. Known problems
2. Probable problems
3. Dangerous problems not yet excluded
4. Problems that may develop because of treatment

This can be represented as:

```text
KNOWN
↓
What is already demonstrated?

PROBABLE
↓
What is strongly suggested?

DANGEROUS / NOT EXCLUDED
↓
What must not be missed?

TREATMENT-RELATED
↓
What could our interventions cause?
```

This structure protects against premature closure.

---

# 24.12 Anticipating Deterioration in Haemorrhage

Haemorrhage is one of the most important areas for anticipation.

A patient may initially maintain blood pressure despite significant blood loss.

The leader should therefore avoid using a single normal physiological measurement as reassurance.

Watch for trends in:

* heart rate
* blood pressure
* mental status
* peripheral perfusion
* respiratory rate
* skin temperature
* urine output when available
* lactate
* acid-base status
* haemoglobin trends
* coagulation parameters

More importantly, ask:

> **Is the trajectory improving, stable or deteriorating?**

A falling blood pressure after a period of apparent stability is not simply a new number.

It may represent progression of an existing problem.

---

# 24.13 Anticipating Ongoing Bleeding

The leader should ask:

> **If this patient is bleeding, where is the blood going?**

Potential sources include:

* external bleeding
* thoracic bleeding
* abdominal bleeding
* pelvic bleeding
* long-bone bleeding
* retroperitoneal bleeding

If bleeding is suspected, the team should not become reassured merely because one visible source has been controlled.

The question remains:

> **Could another source still be active?**

---

# 24.14 Anticipating Hypothermia

Hypothermia should be anticipated early rather than treated only after it develops.

Trauma patients are vulnerable because of:

* environmental exposure
* clothing removal
* blood loss
* shock
* cold fluids
* prolonged procedures
* exposure during imaging
* prolonged resuscitation

The leader should therefore consider:

* warming measures
* environmental temperature
* warmed fluids when appropriate
* minimising unnecessary exposure
* temperature monitoring
* timely definitive treatment

Hypothermia is not simply a comfort issue.

It may contribute to worsening physiological instability and impaired coagulation.

---

# 24.15 Anticipating Coagulopathy

In major haemorrhage, the leader should anticipate deterioration in coagulation.

The relevant question is not merely:

> "What is the coagulation result?"

It is:

> **"Is the patient's haemostatic situation getting better or worse?"**

This reinforces the importance of:

* early haemorrhage control
* appropriate blood component therapy
* temperature management
* correction of relevant physiological abnormalities
* repeated reassessment

The exact resuscitation strategy should follow local major haemorrhage protocols.

---

# 24.16 Anticipating Airway Failure

Airway deterioration may be progressive.

Warning features may include:

* worsening consciousness
* increasing work of breathing
* inability to protect the airway
* facial trauma
* expanding neck swelling
* blood or secretions
* burns or inhalational injury
* progressive fatigue

The leader should ask:

> **If the airway becomes impossible in five minutes, are we ready now?**

This question is particularly important when airway difficulty is anticipated.

Preparation may include:

* senior airway expertise
* suction
* oxygen
* airway equipment
* backup equipment
* appropriate positioning
* a clearly communicated rescue plan

---

# 24.17 Anticipating Respiratory Deterioration

Respiratory physiology can deteriorate rapidly.

The leader should continuously consider:

* airway obstruction
* pneumothorax
* haemothorax
* pulmonary contusion
* aspiration
* inadequate ventilation
* chest wall injury
* fatigue

The leader should not wait for a dramatic fall in oxygen saturation before recognising deterioration.

Clinical examination, respiratory effort, chest movement, oxygen requirement and overall trajectory remain important.

---

# 24.18 Anticipating Neurological Deterioration

A trauma patient with brain injury may deteriorate because of:

* expanding intracranial bleeding
* cerebral oedema
* hypoxia
* hypotension
* seizures
* hypercapnia
* other physiological disturbances

The leader should therefore avoid treating neurological deterioration in isolation.

When consciousness changes, reassess the whole patient.

A useful sequence is:

```text
Airway
↓
Oxygenation
↓
Ventilation
↓
Circulation
↓
Glucose / metabolic causes
↓
Neurological causes
```

---

# 24.19 Anticipating the Need for Definitive Treatment

Resuscitation is not the endpoint.

The trauma team leader should continually ask:

> **What definitive treatment will this patient ultimately require?**

Potential destinations include:

* operating theatre
* interventional radiology
* intensive care
* specialist trauma service
* another hospital
* ward-level care in selected patients

The timing of this decision matters.

A patient may become trapped in the resuscitation room because the team continues to perform temporary interventions without progressing toward definitive treatment.

The leader must recognise when:

> **resuscitation is becoming a bridge to definitive care.**

---

# 24.20 The "Exit Plan"

Every major resuscitation should have an exit plan.

The leader should know:

* What happens next?
* Where is the patient going?
* Who is receiving the patient?
* What treatment must occur before transfer?
* What monitoring is required?
* What equipment is needed?
* What information must be communicated?
* What could deteriorate during transfer?

A useful question is:

> **What could go wrong immediately after we leave this room?**

This extends anticipation beyond the resuscitation itself.

---

# 24.21 Anticipating Transfer Problems

Transfer is a high-risk transition.

Before moving the patient, confirm:

* airway security
* oxygen supply
* ventilation strategy
* haemodynamic stability
* vascular access
* infusions
* monitoring
* analgesia and sedation where appropriate
* blood products if required
* drains and tubes
* equipment
* receiving team
* destination
* communication

The leader should avoid creating a situation where:

> **the patient is moved before the team is ready.**

---

# 24.22 Anticipating Team Problems

Not every future problem is physiological.

Teams also deteriorate.

Potential team problems include:

* unclear leadership
* duplicated tasks
* missed tasks
* poor communication
* excessive noise
* fixation
* loss of situational awareness
* fatigue
* conflict
* lack of equipment
* uncertainty about the plan

The leader should therefore ask:

> **What is the team likely to struggle with next?**

If the team is becoming overloaded, redistribute tasks.

If communication is becoming chaotic, re-establish structure.

If several people are speaking simultaneously, restore one clear communication pathway.

---

# 24.23 Anticipating Cognitive Overload

As the complexity of the resuscitation increases, cognitive load increases.

The leader may need to:

* prioritise competing problems
* make decisions
* communicate
* monitor the patient
* supervise procedures
* coordinate specialists
* plan definitive care

Trying to personally perform every task increases cognitive overload.

A strong leader therefore uses delegation.

The leader's role is not to personally solve every problem.

The leader's role is to ensure that every important problem has an owner.

---

# 24.24 Anticipating Information Failure

A team may have the right personnel and equipment but still fail because critical information is not available.

Examples include:

* unknown anticoagulant use
* unclear mechanism
* missing previous medical history
* incomplete medication history
* unknown allergy status
* unavailable imaging
* delayed laboratory results
* uncertain timing of deterioration

The leader should ask:

> **What information do we need next?**

Then:

> **Who will obtain it?**

This prevents the entire team from waiting passively for information.

---

# 24.25 Anticipating Equipment Failure

Critical equipment should not be assumed to work.

Before it is needed, the team should know:

* where it is
* who can operate it
* whether it is functional
* what the alternative is

This is particularly important for:

* suction
* oxygen
* airway equipment
* monitoring
* vascular access equipment
* blood warming equipment
* chest drainage equipment
* difficult airway equipment

The principle is:

> **The best time to discover that equipment does not work is before the patient needs it.**

---

# 24.26 Anticipating the Second Crisis

One of the most dangerous moments in trauma is when the team believes the crisis has ended.

The patient may initially improve after an intervention and then deteriorate again.

The leader should therefore ask:

> **What would make this patient deteriorate again?**

Examples:

* ongoing bleeding
* recurrent airway obstruction
* recurrent pneumothorax
* displacement of equipment
* loss of vascular access
* sedation-related deterioration
* delayed physiological consequences
* progression of occult injury

The end of one crisis does not necessarily mean the end of risk.

---

# 24.27 Reassessment Is an Anticipatory Tool

Reassessment is not simply confirmation of what has already been done.

It is a mechanism for detecting what is changing.

After every major intervention, ask:

1. Did it work?
2. How do we know?
3. What changed?
4. What did not change?
5. What problem remains?
6. What problem may appear next?

This converts reassessment into forward-looking clinical reasoning.

---

# 24.28 Trend Over Time

A single observation provides a snapshot.

A series of observations provides a trajectory.

The leader should therefore look for:

```text
Improving
──────────────►

Stable
──────────────►

Deteriorating
──────────────►
```

The direction of travel may be more informative than a single value.

For example:

> A patient with a borderline observation that is improving may be less concerning than a patient with apparently acceptable observations that are progressively worsening.

The leader should therefore continuously monitor the trajectory.

---

# 24.29 The Anticipation Loop

A useful leadership loop is:

```text
ASSESS
   ↓
INTERPRET
   ↓
ANTICIPATE
   ↓
PREPARE
   ↓
ACT
   ↓
REASSESS
   ↓
ANTICIPATE AGAIN
```

This is continuous.

It does not stop after one decision.

---

# 24.30 Anticipation and Closed-Loop Communication

Anticipation must be communicated.

It is not sufficient for the leader to think:

> "We may need blood."

The team needs to know what is required.

For example:

> "There is ongoing concern about haemorrhage. Please activate the major haemorrhage protocol and confirm when blood is available."

The receiver should confirm:

> "Major haemorrhage protocol activated. I will confirm when blood arrives."

The leader now knows the task has been understood and assigned.

---

# 24.31 Make Future Tasks Visible

If a task will probably become necessary, assign it early.

Examples:

> "Please prepare the airway equipment."

> "Please ensure suction is ready."

> "Please contact the operating theatre."

> "Please inform the blood bank."

> "Please prepare the patient for transfer."

This avoids the delay that occurs when the team waits until the problem is fully established.

---

# 24.32 Anticipation Without Premature Action

Anticipation does not mean performing unnecessary interventions.

There is an important distinction between:

> **preparing for a possible problem**

and:

> **treating a problem that has not occurred.**

The leader should avoid unnecessary intervention simply because deterioration is possible.

Preparation may be appropriate while treatment remains conditional.

For example:

> "Prepare the equipment in case the airway deteriorates."

is different from:

> "Perform the intervention immediately."

The decision to intervene should remain based on the clinical situation.

---

# 24.33 Avoiding Overprediction

The leader cannot predict everything.

Trying to anticipate every possible complication creates:

* unnecessary complexity
* distraction
* excessive communication
* cognitive overload
* delayed action

The goal is therefore not:

> **Predict everything.**

The goal is:

> **Predict the most important next problems.**

Prioritise according to:

1. Likelihood
2. Consequence
3. Time sensitivity
4. Ability to prepare

---

# 24.34 A Practical Anticipation Matrix

| Potential problem          | Likelihood    | Consequence  | Time sensitivity      | Preparation                                |
| -------------------------- | ------------- | ------------ | --------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Airway deterioration       | High          | Catastrophic | Immediate             | Airway equipment and expertise             |
| Ongoing haemorrhage        | High          | Catastrophic | Immediate             | Blood, access, haemorrhage control         |
| Respiratory deterioration  | Moderate/High | Major        | Immediate             | Oxygen, monitoring, intervention equipment |
| Hypothermia                | Moderate/High | Significant  | Progressive           | Warming strategy                           |
| Neurological deterioration | Variable      | Major        | Potentially immediate | Monitoring and reassessment                |
| Transfer deterioration     | Variable      | Major        | At transition         | Equipment and receiving team               |

The exact assessment depends on the patient.

The matrix is a thinking tool rather than a rigid protocol.

---

# 24.35 Ask the Team to Anticipate

Anticipation should not be limited to the leader.

The leader can deliberately ask:

> **"What do you think is going to become our next problem?"**

This can be directed to:

* airway lead
* circulation lead
* nursing team
* anaesthesia
* surgery
* radiology
* other specialists

Different team members see different parts of the resuscitation.

The leader can combine these perspectives into a more complete picture.

---

# 24.36 Encourage Speaking Up

A team member may notice deterioration before the leader.

The leader should create an environment in which concerns can be voiced.

Useful phrases include:

> "Tell me if you see anything changing."

> "Call out any deterioration immediately."

> "If you think we are missing something, say so."

This strengthens situational awareness.

---

# 24.37 The Five-Question Anticipation Check

At any point during resuscitation, the leader can ask:

### 1. What is the biggest threat right now?

### 2. What is likely to become the biggest threat next?

### 3. What would tell us that deterioration is occurring?

### 4. What can we prepare now?

### 5. What is our next definitive step?

These five questions provide a compact cognitive framework.

---

# 24.38 The Thirty-Second Forward Scan

When the situation permits, the leader can perform a brief mental scan:

> **What is happening now?**

> **What should happen next?**

> **What could prevent that from happening?**

> **What could suddenly make the patient worse?**

> **What should I prepare before that happens?**

This can often be completed within seconds.

---

# 24.39 Anticipation During Major Haemorrhage

During major haemorrhage, anticipation becomes particularly important.

The leader should think beyond the immediate transfusion.

Potential future problems include:

* continued bleeding
* worsening shock
* hypothermia
* coagulopathy
* electrolyte disturbance
* acidosis
* failure of peripheral access
* delays in definitive haemorrhage control
* exhaustion of available resources

The critical question remains:

> **What will actually stop the bleeding?**

Blood products support the patient.

They do not replace definitive haemorrhage control.

---

# 24.40 Anticipation During Difficult Airway Management

During difficult airway management, the leader should anticipate failure before it occurs.

Questions include:

* Is oxygenation being maintained?
* How many attempts are appropriate?
* Who is performing the next attempt?
* What is the backup strategy?
* Is rescue equipment ready?
* Is senior expertise present?
* What will happen if the next attempt fails?

The team should never discover its rescue strategy only after the primary strategy has failed.

---

# 24.41 Anticipation During Chest Trauma

Chest trauma can evolve rapidly.

The leader should consider:

* pneumothorax
* tension physiology
* haemothorax
* pulmonary contusion
* airway injury
* respiratory fatigue

If the patient's respiratory condition changes, the leader should reassess rather than relying on an earlier diagnosis.

---

# 24.42 Anticipation in Head Injury

In significant head injury, deterioration may occur because of both primary and secondary injury.

The leader should therefore anticipate:

* worsening consciousness
* airway compromise
* hypoxia
* hypotension
* abnormal ventilation
* seizures
* need for specialist neurosurgical input
* need for definitive imaging and treatment

The principle remains:

> **Prevent secondary physiological deterioration while pursuing definitive management.**

---

# 24.43 Anticipation in Pelvic Trauma

A patient with suspected pelvic haemorrhage may deteriorate despite limited external evidence of blood loss.

The leader should consider:

* ongoing haemorrhage
* worsening shock
* need for blood products
* mechanical stabilisation where indicated
* definitive haemorrhage control
* transfer to an appropriate definitive care pathway

The absence of obvious external bleeding does not exclude major internal haemorrhage.

---

# 24.44 Anticipating Diagnostic Delay

Diagnostic testing may take time.

The leader should ask:

> **Will waiting for this result change what we need to do now?**

If the patient is unstable, diagnostic delay may become clinically important.

The team should avoid allowing investigations to become a substitute for treatment.

At the same time, the leader should avoid unnecessary intervention based purely on fear.

The correct balance is:

> **Use the information available while continuing to treat the life-threatening problem.**

---

# 24.45 Anticipating the Effect of Each Intervention

Every intervention changes the system.

After an intervention, ask:

> **What new problem could this intervention create?**

Examples include:

* sedation affecting airway or blood pressure
* positive-pressure ventilation affecting haemodynamics
* invasive procedures causing complications
* prolonged exposure causing hypothermia
* transport interrupting monitoring
* medication causing physiological changes

This does not mean avoiding necessary treatment.

It means recognising that interventions have consequences.

---

# 24.46 The Leader as a Step Ahead

A useful description of effective trauma leadership is:

> **The leader should be one step ahead of the resuscitation, not five steps behind it.**

Being one step ahead means:

* knowing the current problem
* anticipating the next problem
* preparing the team
* communicating the plan
* reassessing continuously

Being five steps behind means:

* reacting to deterioration
* searching for equipment
* discovering missing information
* deciding who should act
* communicating after the crisis has already developed

The difference is preparation.

---

# 24.47 Anticipation and Pace

Anticipation improves pace without creating unnecessary haste.

A team that prepares early can act rapidly when required.

A team that prepares late may become frantic.

The goal is:

> **Calm preparation followed by decisive action.**

This is one of the characteristics of a high-functioning trauma team.

---

# 24.48 Avoiding the "Everything Is Fine" Trap

Temporary improvement can be misleading.

For example:

* blood pressure improves
* oxygen saturation improves
* airway is secured
* bleeding appears controlled
* patient becomes more responsive

The leader should still ask:

> **What problem remains?**

and:

> **What could make the patient deteriorate again?**

Improvement is reassuring.

It is not permission to stop thinking.

---

# 24.49 Anticipation Before Imaging

Before moving a trauma patient to imaging, the leader should consider:

* Is the patient stable enough?
* Is the airway secure?
* Is oxygen available?
* Is monitoring adequate?
* Is vascular access secure?
* Are infusions running?
* Is the team ready?
* What happens if the patient deteriorates in the scanner?

The decision to image is therefore also a logistics and safety decision.

---

# 24.50 Anticipation Before Theatre

Before transfer to theatre, the leader should consider:

* What remains unstable?
* What treatment must occur before transfer?
* Is blood available?
* Is the receiving team ready?
* Has the operative problem been communicated?
* What is the expected next step?
* What could deteriorate during transfer?

The objective is to make the transition controlled rather than improvised.

---

# 24.51 Anticipation Before Handover

Before handover, the leader should ensure that the receiving team understands:

* mechanism
* major injuries
* physiological trajectory
* interventions
* response to treatment
* unresolved problems
* pending investigations
* current risks
* anticipated deterioration
* next planned steps

A good handover should answer:

> **What has happened?**

> **What have we done?**

> **What is still wrong?**

> **What are we worried about next?**

> **What needs to happen now?**

---

# 24.52 Anticipation as a Team Culture

Anticipation becomes most effective when it becomes part of the team's culture.

The team should routinely think:

> **What is next?**

rather than:

> **What just happened?**

The past remains important for understanding the patient.

But leadership must remain forward-looking.

---

# 24.53 Practical Language for the Trauma Team Leader

Useful phrases include:

> "Let's think one step ahead."

> "What is likely to become our next problem?"

> "Prepare for deterioration."

> "Have the backup equipment ready."

> "If this does not work, what is our next step?"

> "What are we waiting for?"

> "Who is responsible for that?"

> "What will happen after this intervention?"

> "Are we moving toward definitive treatment?"

> "Before we move, what could go wrong?"

> "What information do we need next?"

> "Tell me if anything changes."

These phrases make anticipation visible to the whole team.

---

# 24.54 A Structured Anticipation Pause

When the situation allows, the leader can briefly pause and state:

> **"Current problem: ..."**

> **"Expected next problem: ..."**

> **"If that happens: ..."**

> **"We are preparing: ..."**

> **"Definitive plan: ..."**

For example:

> "Current problem is haemodynamic instability with suspected ongoing bleeding. Our next concern is continued blood loss. If the patient deteriorates further, we will escalate haemorrhage control and resuscitation. Blood is being prepared now. We need to move toward definitive haemorrhage control."

This creates a shared mental model.

---

# 24.55 The Shared Mental Model

Anticipation is most powerful when the entire team understands what is expected.

A shared mental model means that team members know:

* what is happening
* what the leader is concerned about
* what is likely to happen next
* what each person is doing
* what the next major decision will be

The team therefore does not need to wait for every instruction.

They can prepare intelligently within their roles.

---

# 24.56 Anticipation Does Not Replace Reassessment

Even a well-constructed prediction can be wrong.

Therefore:

> **Anticipate, then verify.**

The leader should remain willing to change the plan.

If the patient behaves differently from expected:

1. Recognise the discrepancy.
2. Reassess.
3. Reconsider the working diagnosis.
4. Communicate the new plan.
5. Reallocate tasks.
6. Continue toward definitive treatment.

Flexibility is essential.

---

# 24.57 Anticipation and Cognitive Flexibility

A strong leader can hold two ideas simultaneously:

> "This is the most likely explanation."

and:

> "I may still be wrong."

This prevents premature closure.

The leader should remain open to new information.

A deteriorating patient is often telling the team that the current mental model is incomplete.

---

# 24.58 The Anticipation Checklist

Before moving through a major phase of trauma resuscitation, consider:

### Patient

* What is the most serious current problem?
* Is the patient improving?
* Is the patient deteriorating?
* What could deteriorate next?

### Team

* Does everyone know the plan?
* Are responsibilities clear?
* Is senior expertise available?
* Is communication effective?

### Equipment

* Is essential equipment ready?
* Is backup equipment available?
* Is monitoring functioning?

### Information

* What information is missing?
* Who is obtaining it?
* Are pending results likely to change management?

### Treatment

* Is the current treatment working?
* What is the next intervention?
* What is the backup plan?

### Definitive care

* Where does the patient need to go?
* What needs to happen before transfer?
* Is the receiving team ready?

---

# 24.59 The Leader's Forward Scan

A useful final mental model is:

```text
LOOK BACK
What has happened?

LOOK NOW
What is happening?

LOOK FORWARD
What is likely to happen?

PREPARE
What can we do now?

ACT
What needs to happen?

REASSESS
Did it work?

LOOK FORWARD AGAIN
What comes next?
```

This creates a continuous cycle of anticipatory leadership.

---

# 24.60 Key Principles

1. **Do not wait for the next problem to become obvious.**

2. **Use the mechanism and current physiology to predict likely deterioration.**

3. **Think in time horizons: now, next few minutes, next hour and definitive care.**

4. **Anticipate treatment failure and prepare a backup strategy.**

5. **Prepare equipment and personnel before they are urgently required.**

6. **Look for trends rather than isolated observations.**

7. **After every major intervention, ask what could happen next.**

8. **Anticipate both patient problems and team problems.**

9. **Make future tasks visible and assign ownership.**

10. **Do not confuse preparation with unnecessary intervention.**

11. **Prioritise the most likely and most dangerous future problems.**

12. **Keep the entire team informed of the anticipated next step.**

13. **Use closed-loop communication for critical preparatory tasks.**

14. **Maintain flexibility because predictions can be wrong.**

15. **Keep moving toward definitive treatment.**

16. **Before every transition, ask what could go wrong.**

17. **Use anticipation to reduce surprise, delay and cognitive overload.**

18. **A good trauma leader is continuously one step ahead of the resuscitation.**

---

# 24.61 Final Take-Home Message

The trauma team leader should never be thinking only about the problem immediately in front of the team.

The leader should simultaneously ask:

> **What is happening now?**

> **What is likely to happen next?**

> **What can we prepare now?**

> **What will we do if the situation deteriorates?**

> **Where are we ultimately trying to take this patient?**

The objective is not perfect prediction.

The objective is preparedness.

A team that anticipates the next problem can act before the problem becomes a crisis.

That is the essence of anticipatory trauma leadership:

> **Recognise early.
> Prepare early.
> Communicate clearly.
> Act decisively.
> Reassess continuously.
> Stay one step ahead.**
