# Chapter 10 — Extremity Trauma and Limb Threats

## Clinical Scenario

A 29-year-old man is brought to the resuscitation room following a high-speed motorcycle collision.

He has multiple injuries and required initial haemorrhage control at the scene.

During the primary survey, the patient is found to have a grossly deformed left lower limb with an open wound over the distal leg.

The foot is cool and pale.

The patient reports severe pain and paraesthesia in the foot.

The initial assessment also identifies a deformity of the right upper limb.

The Trauma Team Leader recognises that the limb injuries may represent more than simple fractures.

The immediate priorities are to determine:

- whether there is ongoing life-threatening haemorrhage;
- whether arterial perfusion is compromised;
- whether there is major nerve injury;
- whether compartment syndrome is developing;
- whether the limb is open, contaminated, crushed, or mangled;
- whether urgent reduction or realignment is required;
- and whether specialist orthopaedic, vascular, plastic surgical, or other intervention is required.

The leader must ensure that limb-threatening pathology is recognised without losing sight of the patient's overall physiological condition.

Extremity trauma should therefore be assessed within the wider trauma framework rather than treated as an isolated orthopaedic problem.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Recognise limb injuries that may threaten life or limb viability.
2. Perform and document a structured neurovascular assessment.
3. Recognise hard and concerning features of arterial injury.
4. Understand the importance of reassessment after reduction, splinting, or other interventions.
5. Recognise acute compartment syndrome and understand when urgent decompression is required.
6. Identify open fractures and initiate appropriate early management.
7. Recognise major external haemorrhage from an injured limb.
8. Understand the initial management principles of mangled and severely contaminated extremities.
9. Coordinate orthopaedic, vascular, plastic surgical, and other specialist input.
10. Understand the role of imaging in the injured limb.
11. Recognise when definitive limb management should not be delayed by unnecessary investigation.
12. Understand the principles involved in limb salvage versus amputation decisions.
13. Maintain systematic reassessment throughout resuscitation.
14. Identify common errors that can result in preventable limb loss or disability.

---

# Key Concepts

## Extremity Trauma Can Threaten Life

Limb injuries are often associated with fractures, dislocations, soft-tissue injury, and pain.

However, severe extremity trauma can also cause major haemorrhage and contribute directly to haemorrhagic shock.

The team leader should therefore ask:

> **Is this limb injury contributing to the patient's physiological instability?**

Potential sources of major haemorrhage include:

- open wounds;
- arterial injury;
- venous injury;
- long-bone fractures;
- extensive soft-tissue destruction;
- and bleeding from a mangled extremity.

A visibly dramatic limb injury should not distract the team from other sources of haemorrhage.

Equally, apparently isolated limb trauma should not be assumed to be minor.

---

## Extremity Trauma Can Threaten the Limb

Limb-threatening pathology may result from:

- arterial occlusion or disruption;
- prolonged ischaemia;
- compartment syndrome;
- severe crush injury;
- open fracture;
- major nerve injury;
- extensive soft-tissue destruction;
- infection;
- or combinations of these injuries.

The leader should therefore continuously integrate:

**Mechanism → Anatomy → Perfusion → Neurology → Tissue viability → Response to intervention**

---

## The Neurovascular Examination Is a Core Trauma Skill

A neurovascular examination should not be regarded as a single examination performed once.

It should be repeated:

- on arrival;
- after reduction;
- after splinting;
- after manipulation;
- after vascular intervention;
- after deterioration;
- and before transfer whenever clinically appropriate.

The examination should be documented clearly.

A vague statement such as "neurovascularly intact" is less useful than a structured record of the actual findings.

---

# Initial Limb Assessment

## Look

The first assessment should identify obvious abnormalities.

Look for:

- deformity;
- shortening;
- abnormal rotation;
- angulation;
- open wounds;
- exposed bone;
- contamination;
- active bleeding;
- expanding haematoma;
- skin colour changes;
- blistering;
- swelling;
- crushing;
- tissue loss;
- and signs of threatened skin.

The limb should be compared with the contralateral side when appropriate.

---

## Feel

Assessment should include:

- temperature;
- tenderness;
- swelling;
- compartment firmness;
- distal perfusion;
- and pain.

The examiner should avoid excessive manipulation of an unstable injury.

If a fracture or dislocation is obvious, the priority is to assess whether immediate realignment is required to restore circulation, reduce tension on soft tissues, or improve neurological function.

---

## Move

Movement should be assessed carefully.

The examiner should consider:

- active movement;
- passive movement;
- joint function;
- tendon function;
- and pain associated with movement.

Movement should not be forced through an unstable injury.

Particular attention should be paid to pain associated with passive stretch when compartment syndrome is suspected.

---

# Neurovascular Assessment

## Vascular Assessment

The vascular examination should include assessment of:

- skin colour;
- temperature;
- capillary refill where appropriate;
- distal pulses;
- pulse symmetry;
- Doppler signals when required;
- and evidence of active or ongoing bleeding.

The absence of a palpable pulse does not by itself define the mechanism of vascular injury.

Likewise, the presence of a pulse does not completely exclude arterial injury.

The leader should interpret the vascular findings in the context of:

- mechanism;
- fracture or dislocation;
- soft-tissue injury;
- limb appearance;
- neurological findings;
- and trajectory.

---

## Neurological Assessment

The neurological examination should assess the major peripheral nerve territories relevant to the injured limb.

This should include:

- motor function;
- sensory function;
- and comparison with the uninjured limb where possible.

The exact examination should reflect the anatomical region involved.

A new neurological deficit following trauma may indicate:

- direct nerve injury;
- compression;
- dislocation;
- compartment syndrome;
- vascular compromise;
- or an evolving soft-tissue problem.

---

## Document the Examination

The team should document the neurovascular status in clear terms.

For example:

- pulse present or absent;
- Doppler signal present or absent where relevant;
- limb warm or cool;
- colour;
- capillary refill where clinically useful;
- motor function;
- sensory function;
- and the time of assessment.

This becomes particularly important when the patient undergoes:

- reduction;
- splinting;
- transfer;
- imaging;
- surgery;
- or changes in clinical condition.

---

# Hard Signs of Vascular Injury

## Recognising Major Arterial Injury

Certain findings should raise immediate concern for major arterial injury.

These may include:

- active arterial bleeding;
- expanding or pulsatile haematoma;
- distal ischaemia;
- absent distal perfusion;
- or a bruit or thrill associated with vascular injury.

The exact management depends on the anatomical injury and the overall clinical situation.

However, the key principle is:

> **Do not allow diagnostic investigation to delay necessary restoration of limb perfusion or haemorrhage control.**

---

## Limb Ischaemia Is Time Critical

A poorly perfused limb requires urgent assessment.

The leader should consider:

- whether the injury is due to arterial disruption;
- whether fracture or dislocation is causing vascular compression;
- whether the limb requires immediate reduction;
- whether vascular surgery is required;
- whether temporary shunting may be required in selected injuries;
- and whether definitive revascularisation can be achieved without unacceptable delay.

The longer a critically ischaemic limb remains without adequate perfusion, the greater the risk of irreversible tissue injury.

---

# Fracture and Dislocation

## Realignment

Gross deformity may compromise:

- blood flow;
- nerve function;
- skin integrity;
- and soft-tissue perfusion.

Where clinically appropriate, urgent realignment or reduction may therefore be required.

The procedure should be performed with appropriate analgesia, sedation, expertise, and monitoring according to the clinical situation.

---

## Reassess After Reduction

A neurovascular assessment must be repeated after:

- reduction;
- manipulation;
- splint application;
- or any intervention that changes limb alignment.

The leader should specifically ask:

> **Did the neurovascular status improve, remain unchanged, or deteriorate?**

A previously perfused limb that becomes poorly perfused after reduction or splinting requires immediate reassessment.

---

# Splinting

## Purpose of Splinting

Appropriate splinting may:

- reduce pain;
- limit further tissue injury;
- reduce movement at the fracture site;
- protect neurovascular structures;
- reduce bleeding;
- and facilitate safe transport.

Splinting should not compromise circulation.

---

## Avoid Excessive Tightness

After applying a splint or dressing, reassess:

- pulses or Doppler signals;
- colour;
- temperature;
- sensation;
- motor function;
- pain;
- and swelling.

A well-intentioned intervention can become harmful if it compromises perfusion or increases compartment pressure.

---

# Major Limb Haemorrhage

## Immediate Control

Major external haemorrhage from an extremity should be controlled immediately.

Depending on the injury, measures may include:

- direct pressure;
- appropriate wound packing;
- haemostatic dressings where indicated;
- tourniquet application;
- fracture realignment;
- and definitive operative vascular control.

The intervention should be appropriate to the source and severity of bleeding.

---

## Tourniquets

A tourniquet may be lifesaving in uncontrolled major extremity haemorrhage.

The team should:

- apply it according to local protocol;
- ensure that bleeding is controlled;
- document the time of application;
- reassess the patient;
- and communicate the presence and duration of tourniquet use during handover.

A tourniquet should not be applied casually to a minor wound.

The decision should be based on the severity of haemorrhage and the ability to control it effectively by other means.

---

# Open Fractures

## Recognise the Injury

An open fracture should be suspected whenever there is a wound associated with a fracture, even when the wound appears small.

The external appearance of the wound does not reliably indicate the extent of internal contamination or tissue damage.

The leader should avoid unnecessary repeated examination or manipulation of the wound.

---

## Initial Management

Initial management should include:

- control of major haemorrhage;
- assessment of neurovascular status;
- appropriate analgesia;
- sterile protection of the wound;
- fracture realignment when required;
- splinting;
- intravenous antibiotics according to local protocol;
- tetanus assessment and prophylaxis where indicated;
- appropriate imaging;
- and early specialist involvement.

Antibiotics should be given as early as possible when indicated by the open fracture pathway.

---

## Do Not Perform Repeated Wound Manipulation

The open wound should not be repeatedly explored in the emergency department in an attempt to determine its depth or severity.

The priority is:

**Protect → Antibiotics → Realign → Splint → Document → Specialist management**

Definitive wound assessment and debridement should occur through the appropriate surgical pathway.

---

# Contamination

## Highly Contaminated Injuries

The team should identify contamination involving:

- soil;
- agricultural material;
- sewage;
- water;
- foreign bodies;
- road debris;
- or other contaminated environments.

The mechanism and contamination should be communicated clearly to the receiving surgical team.

Highly contaminated wounds may require particularly urgent operative management.

---

# Acute Compartment Syndrome

## Why It Matters

Acute compartment syndrome is a limb-threatening emergency caused by increased pressure within a closed myofascial compartment resulting in impaired tissue perfusion.

It can occur following:

- fractures;
- crush injuries;
- reperfusion;
- vascular injury;
- bleeding;
- burns;
- or other significant soft-tissue trauma.

The condition can result in irreversible muscle and nerve injury if diagnosis and treatment are delayed.

---

## Clinical Features

Important findings include:

- severe pain;
- pain disproportionate to the apparent injury;
- increasing analgesic requirement;
- pain on passive stretch;
- tense or swollen compartments;
- sensory disturbance;
- and progressive neurological deficit.

Classical late findings such as paralysis or absent pulses may occur late and should not be awaited.

---

## Pulses Do Not Exclude Compartment Syndrome

A palpable distal pulse does not exclude acute compartment syndrome.

The pathology occurs at the level of tissue perfusion within the compartment and may develop while major arterial flow remains detectable.

Therefore:

> **Do not use the presence of a distal pulse to rule out compartment syndrome.**

---

## High-Risk Patients

Particular vigilance is required in patients with:

- high-energy fractures;
- tibial fractures;
- crush injuries;
- reperfusion following vascular occlusion;
- prolonged limb compression;
- major soft-tissue trauma;
- or reduced consciousness where symptoms cannot be reliably reported.

---

## Immediate Actions

When compartment syndrome is suspected, the team should:

1. Recognise the possibility early.
2. Remove or loosen restrictive circumferential dressings where appropriate.
3. Reassess the limb promptly.
4. Escalate immediately to the appropriate senior surgical team.
5. Avoid unnecessary delay for investigations when the clinical diagnosis is sufficiently clear.
6. Prepare for urgent surgical decompression when indicated.

The limb should be reassessed repeatedly if the diagnosis remains uncertain.

---

## Pressure Measurement

Intracompartmental pressure measurement may be useful when:

- the clinical examination is unreliable;
- findings are equivocal;
- the patient cannot communicate effectively;
- or the diagnosis remains uncertain.

However, pressure measurement should not delay decompression when the clinical diagnosis is clear and urgent treatment is required.

A commonly used pressure criterion is a low differential between diastolic blood pressure and compartment pressure.

However, pressure measurements must always be interpreted in the clinical context.

---

## The Team Leader's Role in Compartment Syndrome

The leader should ensure that:

- the diagnosis is actively considered;
- the examination is documented;
- reassessment occurs;
- senior surgical input is obtained early;
- unnecessary imaging does not delay treatment;
- and the diagnosis remains visible during handover.

A missed compartment syndrome can result in permanent disability.

---

# Crush Injury

## Systemic Consequences

Severe crush injury may produce complications beyond the injured limb.

These may include:

- rhabdomyolysis;
- hyperkalaemia;
- metabolic abnormalities;
- acute kidney injury;
- compartment syndrome;
- and systemic shock.

The leader should therefore consider both:

**Limb threat + systemic threat**

---

## Reassessment

Patients with significant crush injury require ongoing assessment of:

- urine output;
- renal function;
- electrolytes;
- acid-base status;
- creatine kinase where appropriate;
- ECG;
- and overall fluid balance.

The precise resuscitation strategy should follow local major trauma and critical care protocols.

---

# Mangled Extremity

## Definition

A mangled extremity may involve a combination of:

- bone injury;
- major soft-tissue destruction;
- vascular injury;
- nerve injury;
- contamination;
- and extensive skin loss.

These injuries may threaten both limb viability and the patient's overall physiology.

---

## Initial Priorities

The immediate priorities are:

1. Control life-threatening haemorrhage.
2. Restore or protect perfusion.
3. Assess neurological function.
4. Realign and stabilise the limb where appropriate.
5. Protect the wound.
6. Administer appropriate antibiotics for open injury.
7. Prevent hypothermia.
8. Obtain appropriate imaging without delaying definitive treatment.
9. Activate the relevant specialist teams.
10. Establish an early definitive management strategy.

---

# Limb Salvage Versus Amputation

## A Multidisciplinary Decision

The decision to salvage or amputate a severely injured limb should not be based on a single score or isolated clinical finding.

The decision may require input from:

- orthopaedic surgery;
- vascular surgery;
- plastic surgery;
- trauma surgery;
- rehabilitation specialists;
- critical care;
- and the patient and family where circumstances permit.

---

## Factors Affecting the Decision

The team should consider:

- duration of ischaemia;
- vascular injury;
- extent of muscle destruction;
- nerve injury;
- bone loss;
- soft-tissue loss;
- contamination;
- associated injuries;
- physiological condition;
- expected functional outcome;
- reconstructive requirements;
- rehabilitation potential;
- and the patient's priorities.

A technically salvageable limb is not necessarily a functionally salvageable limb.

---

## Do Not Decide in Isolation

The trauma leader should avoid premature statements such as:

> "The limb cannot be saved."

or:

> "We must save the limb at all costs."

The correct approach is to establish the injury pattern, restore life-threatening physiology, obtain appropriate specialist input, and develop a multidisciplinary plan.

---

# Vascular Injury Associated With Fracture or Dislocation

## Think Anatomically

Certain fracture and dislocation patterns carry a high risk of vascular injury.

Examples include injuries around:

- the knee;
- elbow;
- shoulder;
- hip;
- and major long bones.

A normal pulse examination at one point in time does not eliminate the need for reassessment.

---

## Reduction May Be the First Intervention

If a dislocation is causing vascular compromise, urgent reduction may be required.

The team should then immediately repeat the neurovascular examination.

If perfusion remains compromised, urgent vascular assessment and intervention are required.

---

# Imaging

## Imaging Should Answer a Question

The leader should ask:

> **What decision will this investigation change?**

Potential imaging may include:

- plain radiographs;
- CT;
- CT angiography;
- or other vascular imaging when appropriate.

The choice depends on:

- clinical findings;
- mechanism;
- physiological stability;
- suspected anatomy;
- and the availability of definitive treatment.

---

## Do Not Delay Definitive Treatment

A critically ischaemic limb should not be sent through a prolonged diagnostic pathway when clinical findings already indicate the need for urgent intervention.

Similarly, a patient who requires immediate operative management should not be delayed simply to complete imaging that will not alter the immediate decision.

---

# Documentation

## What Should Be Recorded?

The trauma record should include:

- mechanism;
- limb involved;
- fracture or dislocation;
- open versus closed injury;
- wound characteristics;
- contamination;
- haemorrhage;
- vascular examination;
- neurological examination;
- pain;
- compartment findings;
- interventions;
- response to intervention;
- antibiotics;
- tetanus management where relevant;
- splinting;
- tourniquet use;
- specialist consultations;
- and the definitive management plan.

---

## Time Matters

Where important interventions occur, the time should be recorded.

This is particularly important for:

- tourniquet application;
- vascular compromise;
- reduction;
- antibiotic administration;
- recognition of compartment syndrome;
- and transfer.

Clear documentation improves communication and supports safe continuity of care.

---

# Reassessment

## The Limb Must Be Rechecked

The initial examination is only a snapshot.

Reassessment is required after:

- reduction;
- splinting;
- analgesia;
- vascular intervention;
- deterioration;
- increasing pain;
- changes in sensation;
- changes in temperature or colour;
- and transfer between clinical areas.

---

## A Changing Limb Is a New Clinical Problem

The leader should treat a significant change in limb status as a trigger for reassessment.

For example:

**Previously warm foot → now cool foot**

requires immediate reassessment.

**Previously normal sensation → new sensory deficit**

requires immediate reassessment.

**Increasing pain despite analgesia → new concern for compartment syndrome or evolving injury**

requires immediate reassessment.

---

# Transfer of the Patient

## Transfer Does Not End Responsibility

Patients with complex extremity trauma may require transfer to:

- a major trauma centre;
- vascular surgery;
- orthoplastic services;
- specialist orthopaedic services;
- or another definitive treatment facility.

Before transfer, the leader should ensure that:

- haemorrhage is controlled;
- the limb is appropriately aligned and protected;
- neurovascular status is documented;
- analgesia is adequate;
- antibiotics have been administered when indicated;
- relevant imaging accompanies the patient;
- specialist communication has occurred;
- and the receiving team understands the current limb status.

---

# Common Errors

## Error 1 — Treating the Fracture and Forgetting the Limb

A fracture is not simply a radiological diagnosis.

The team must ask:

> **Is the limb perfused and neurologically functional?**

---

## Error 2 — Documenting "Neurovascularly Intact" Without Details

This may hide important changes.

Record the actual findings.

---

## Error 3 — Assuming Pulses Exclude Compartment Syndrome

They do not.

Clinical assessment remains essential.

---

## Error 4 — Failing to Reassess After Reduction

A reduction can improve or worsen vascular and neurological status.

Always reassess.

---

## Error 5 — Applying a Tight Circumferential Dressing

A dressing or splint that compromises perfusion can worsen an already threatened limb.

Reassess after application.

---

## Error 6 — Delaying Antibiotics in an Open Fracture

Early antibiotics are an important component of open fracture management.

Follow the local open fracture protocol.

---

## Error 7 — Repeatedly Manipulating an Open Wound

Repeated emergency department wound exploration does not replace definitive surgical debridement.

Protect the wound and activate the appropriate pathway.

---

## Error 8 — Sending a Critically Ischaemic Limb for Unnecessary Imaging

If clinical findings indicate immediate intervention, investigation should not create avoidable delay.

---

## Error 9 — Focusing Only on the Limb

A severely injured limb may coexist with:

- major haemorrhage;
- chest trauma;
- abdominal injury;
- head injury;
- pelvic trauma;
- or systemic shock.

The patient remains a trauma patient.

---

## Error 10 — Prematurely Declaring the Limb Unsalvageable

Limb salvage decisions should involve appropriate multidisciplinary expertise and consideration of expected function.

---

# Trauma Team Leader Decision Framework

When faced with severe extremity trauma, the leader should ask:

### 1. Is the patient bleeding?

- Is there major external haemorrhage?
- Is a tourniquet required?
- Is there another source of bleeding?

### 2. Is the limb perfused?

- Is the limb warm?
- What is the colour?
- Are pulses present?
- Are Doppler signals present?
- Is there evidence of ischaemia?

### 3. Is the limb neurologically intact?

- What is motor function?
- What is sensation?
- Has this changed?

### 4. Is there a compartment syndrome?

- Is the pain disproportionate?
- Is there pain on passive stretch?
- Are compartments tense?
- Is the examination reliable?
- Does the patient require urgent surgical assessment?

### 5. Is the injury open?

- Is there a wound?
- Is there contamination?
- Have antibiotics been given?
- Is tetanus prophylaxis required?
- Has the limb been protected?

### 6. Does the limb require reduction?

- Is there gross deformity?
- Is vascular or neurological function compromised?
- Is the skin threatened?

### 7. What specialist input is required?

Consider:

- orthopaedics;
- vascular surgery;
- plastics;
- trauma surgery;
- critical care;
- rehabilitation.

### 8. Does the patient need immediate definitive treatment?

The leader should determine whether the next step is:

- operating theatre;
- vascular intervention;
- orthoplastic management;
- CT or CT angiography;
- critical care;
- or transfer.

---

# Communication Within the Trauma Team

The leader should communicate the limb threat explicitly.

For example:

> "The left lower limb is threatened. There is an open tibial injury with absent distal perfusion. We need urgent vascular and orthopaedic involvement."

This is more useful than simply stating:

> "There is a leg fracture."

The communication should identify:

- the problem;
- its severity;
- the current physiological status;
- the intervention already performed;
- and the required next step.

---

# Clinical Scenario — Revisited

The 29-year-old motorcycle collision patient has a grossly deformed open lower-limb injury.

The initial assessment demonstrates:

- active bleeding;
- absent palpable distal pulse;
- cool foot;
- altered sensation;
- and severe pain.

The Trauma Team Leader immediately recognises that this is not simply a fracture.

The priorities are:

1. Control haemorrhage.
2. Assess and protect the airway and overall physiology.
3. Maintain appropriate resuscitation.
4. Realign the limb where required.
5. Reassess vascular and neurological status.
6. Protect the open wound.
7. Administer appropriate antibiotics according to local protocol.
8. Activate urgent orthopaedic and vascular expertise.
9. Determine whether immediate operative intervention is required.
10. Continue repeated neurovascular assessment.

The leader should not allow a diagnostic pathway to delay definitive treatment of a critically threatened limb.

---

# Clinical Pearl

> **A limb injury is never just a fracture. Always ask: Is it bleeding? Is it perfused? Is it neurologically intact? Is there a compartment syndrome? Is it open? And what does this patient need next?**

---

# Final Takeaways

1. Severe extremity trauma can threaten both life and limb.
2. Major limb haemorrhage requires immediate recognition and control.
3. Neurovascular assessment is fundamental and must be documented clearly.
4. Reassessment after reduction, splinting, and deterioration is essential.
5. A palpable pulse does not exclude compartment syndrome.
6. Suspected compartment syndrome requires urgent senior surgical assessment and should not be delayed unnecessarily by investigation.
7. Open fractures require early antibiotics, wound protection, realignment, splinting, and definitive specialist management.
8. Critically ischaemic limbs require urgent restoration of perfusion.
9. Imaging should answer a clinical question and must not delay definitive treatment when immediate intervention is required.
10. Mangled extremity management requires multidisciplinary decision-making.
11. Limb salvage should be considered in terms of expected function, not simply anatomical survival.
12. The Trauma Team Leader must continuously integrate the limb injury with the patient's overall physiological condition.
