# Chapter 10 — Who Is the Trauma Team Leader?

## Clinical Scenario

A 32-year-old man is brought to the resuscitation room after a high-speed road traffic collision.

The pre-hospital team reports a prolonged extrication, significant blood loss at the scene, and transient hypotension. The patient arrives pale, tachycardic and confused.

The trauma team moves quickly.

One clinician begins airway assessment. Another prepares blood products. The surgeon asks for a pelvic binder. The anaesthetist is preparing for possible intubation. A nurse is attempting intravenous access. Someone asks for the latest vital signs. Another person begins cutting away the patient's clothing.

Everyone is busy.

But nobody is clearly leading.

Several instructions are given simultaneously. Two clinicians begin the same task. No one clearly announces the patient's response to the first interventions. The team becomes increasingly focused on individual tasks rather than the overall trajectory of the patient.

The problem is not a lack of clinical knowledge.

The problem is a lack of leadership.

A trauma team can contain highly experienced clinicians and still perform poorly if responsibility for coordination and decision-making is unclear.

The Trauma Team Leader exists to prevent this.

---

# Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

- Define the role of the Trauma Team Leader.
- Understand the difference between clinical expertise and trauma leadership.
- Describe the primary responsibilities of the team leader.
- Maintain an overview of the patient while multiple interventions occur simultaneously.
- Coordinate the primary survey and parallel resuscitation processes.
- Allocate tasks according to competence, urgency and workload.
- Make major decisions while avoiding unnecessary micromanagement.
- Recognise when the team is losing situational awareness.
- Communicate priorities clearly during resuscitation.
- Understand when leadership should be transferred or supported by another clinician.
- Avoid common behaviours that impair trauma team performance.

---

# 1. What Is the Trauma Team Leader?

The Trauma Team Leader is the clinician responsible for coordinating the overall assessment, resuscitation and immediate decision-making of the major trauma patient.

The role is not simply a senior clinical position.

It is a **coordination role**.

The leader must continuously integrate:

- The patient's physiology.
- The mechanism of injury.
- The findings of the primary survey.
- The response to interventions.
- The anticipated trajectory.
- The actions of individual team members.
- The availability of resources.
- The need for escalation.
- The destination of the patient.

The Trauma Team Leader therefore operates at a different level from most members of the team.

Individual clinicians concentrate on specific tasks.

The leader concentrates on the **whole system**.

A useful principle is:

> The leader does not need to do everything.
>
> The leader needs to make sure that everything important gets done — in the right order, by the right person, at the right time.

---

# 2. Leadership Is Not the Same as Seniority

The most senior clinician in the room is not automatically the most effective Trauma Team Leader.

Seniority may provide:

- Experience.
- Authority.
- Clinical knowledge.
- Familiarity with the hospital system.
- Confidence in difficult decisions.

However, effective trauma leadership also requires:

- Situational awareness.
- Communication.
- Prioritisation.
- Delegation.
- Decision-making under uncertainty.
- Anticipation.
- Team coordination.
- Recognition of deterioration.

A highly experienced surgeon may be an excellent operator but not necessarily the best person to coordinate the entire resuscitation.

Similarly, an emergency physician may have extensive experience in resuscitation but may need to defer specific procedural decisions to another specialist.

The key question is not:

> "Who is the most senior person here?"

The better question is:

> "Who is best placed to coordinate this resuscitation safely and effectively?"

---

# 3. The Leader Owns the Process

The Trauma Team Leader does not personally own every task.

The leader owns the **process**.

This means ensuring that:

1. The team understands the immediate priorities.
2. The primary survey is performed systematically.
3. Life-threatening problems are identified promptly.
4. Appropriate interventions occur without unnecessary delay.
5. The response to treatment is reassessed.
6. Important information reaches the appropriate person.
7. Decisions are communicated clearly.
8. The patient's trajectory is continuously reassessed.
9. Escalation occurs when required.
10. The next destination is anticipated early.

This distinction is fundamental.

A leader who attempts to perform every procedure will eventually lose the overview of the patient.

A leader who delegates appropriately can maintain control while the team performs multiple interventions in parallel.

---

# 4. The Leader's Core Mental Position

The Trauma Team Leader should maintain a position that allows observation and communication with the team whenever possible.

The leader should be able to see:

- The patient.
- The monitor.
- Key team members.
- Major procedures.
- Blood products and resuscitation equipment.
- The airway area.
- The documentation process.

The physical position is less important than the cognitive position.

The leader should remain mentally one level above the individual tasks.

While one clinician is inserting an arterial line, the leader should be thinking:

- What is the patient's current physiology?
- What has changed?
- What is the immediate threat?
- What intervention is currently occurring?
- Is it working?
- What is likely to happen next?
- What resource will be needed next?
- Where is the patient ultimately going?

This is the **big-picture view**.

---

# 5. The Leader Must Know the Patient's Story

Leadership begins before the patient reaches the resuscitation room.

The leader should obtain the available pre-hospital information.

Important questions include:

### Mechanism

- What happened?
- How severe was the mechanism?
- Was there a high-energy transfer?
- Was there a crush component?
- Was there a fall from height?
- Was there penetrating trauma?
- Was there prolonged entrapment?

### Physiology

- What were the initial vital signs?
- Was there hypotension?
- Was there altered consciousness?
- Was there respiratory compromise?
- Was there deterioration during transport?

### Injuries

- What injuries have already been identified?
- Was there external haemorrhage?
- Was there chest injury?
- Was there pelvic instability suspected?
- Was there neurological deficit?

### Interventions

- What airway interventions were performed?
- What vascular access was obtained?
- What fluids or blood products were given?
- Was haemorrhage control attempted?
- What analgesia or other medication was administered?

### Response

Most importantly:

> How did the patient respond?

The response to intervention may be more informative than a single isolated vital sign.

---

# 6. The Leader's First Task: Establish Control

When the patient arrives, the leader should establish control of the resuscitation without creating unnecessary noise.

This does not require shouting.

It requires clarity.

A simple opening can establish the structure:

> "I am leading the trauma resuscitation."

Then:

> "Let's confirm roles."

And:

> "We will work through the primary survey. Call out findings and interventions."

The leader should ensure that important information is communicated openly.

The team should know:

- Who is leading.
- Who is managing the airway.
- Who is managing circulation.
- Who is performing procedures.
- Who is recording events.
- Who is coordinating blood products.
- Who is communicating with radiology, theatre or other services.

Ambiguity at the beginning of a resuscitation creates avoidable problems later.

---

# 7. The Leader Does Not Need to Be the Loudest Person

Volume is not leadership.

A leader who continuously gives rapid-fire instructions may actually reduce team performance.

Effective leadership is often characterised by:

- Short commands.
- Clear priorities.
- Closed-loop communication.
- Appropriate pauses.
- Reassessment.
- Explicit decisions.

For example:

Poor communication:

> "Get access, get blood, check the chest, somebody call surgery, we need imaging, what is the pressure?"

Better communication:

> "Immediate priority is haemorrhage control. Two large-bore IVs. Activate the major haemorrhage protocol. Surgical review now. Keep reporting the blood pressure."

The second approach reduces ambiguity.

---

# 8. Leadership During the Primary Survey

The Trauma Team Leader should coordinate the primary survey rather than personally perform every component.

A structured approach provides the framework.

## Catastrophic Haemorrhage

The leader asks:

- Is there catastrophic external bleeding?
- Is direct pressure effective?
- Is a tourniquet required?
- Is pelvic haemorrhage possible?
- Is there evidence of major internal haemorrhage?
- Has blood been requested or activated?

The leader should simultaneously consider definitive haemorrhage control.

---

## Airway

The leader should ensure that airway assessment is occurring and that deterioration is anticipated.

Questions include:

- Is the airway patent?
- Is the patient protecting it?
- Is there facial or neck trauma?
- Is there contamination?
- Is airway intervention likely to be required?
- Is cervical spine protection being appropriately maintained?
- Is the team prepared for a difficult airway?

The leader should not unnecessarily interfere with the airway operator.

Instead, the leader should maintain oversight of the patient's physiology and the next steps.

---

## Breathing

The leader should ensure that life-threatening thoracic injuries are actively considered.

Questions include:

- Is oxygenation adequate?
- Is ventilation adequate?
- Are there signs of tension pneumothorax?
- Is there an open chest wound?
- Is massive haemothorax possible?
- Is chest injury affecting haemodynamics?

If an intervention is required, the leader should ensure that it happens promptly and that the response is assessed.

---

## Circulation

The leader integrates:

- Blood pressure.
- Heart rate.
- Peripheral perfusion.
- Mental status.
- External haemorrhage.
- Suspected internal bleeding.
- Blood loss.
- Response to resuscitation.

The leader must continuously ask:

> "Is this patient bleeding?"

And:

> "Where is the bleeding?"

And:

> "What are we doing about it?"

---

## Disability

The leader ensures assessment of:

- Consciousness.
- Pupils.
- Glucose where appropriate.
- Focal neurological abnormalities.
- Seizures.
- Evidence of traumatic brain injury.

The leader must avoid allowing neurological assessment to distract the team from uncontrolled haemorrhage or other immediately reversible threats.

---

## Exposure and Environmental Control

The patient must be adequately exposed to identify injuries.

At the same time:

> Exposure must not become unnecessary heat loss.

The leader ensures that the patient is examined systematically while maintaining appropriate warming measures.

---

# 9. The Leader Must Continuously Reassess

Trauma resuscitation is dynamic.

A patient's condition can change within minutes.

Therefore, the leader should repeatedly ask:

> "What has changed?"

A useful cycle is:

**Assess → Intervene → Reassess → Reprioritise**

This cycle should continue throughout the resuscitation.

For example:

A patient arrives hypotensive.

The team identifies external haemorrhage and applies direct pressure.

The blood pressure remains low.

The leader must not simply move on because the initial intervention was performed.

The question becomes:

> "Did it work?"

If not:

> "Why not?"

And then:

> "What is the next most important action?"

---

# 10. The Leader Must Think in Parallel

Major trauma is rarely a sequence of completely isolated tasks.

Multiple processes occur simultaneously.

For example:

- One clinician manages the airway.
- Another establishes vascular access.
- Blood products are being prepared.
- A pelvic binder is applied.
- The patient is being exposed.
- A surgeon is assessing for operative bleeding.
- The anaesthetic team prepares for airway intervention.
- The nurse records events.
- Imaging is being considered.

The leader coordinates these parallel processes.

The leader therefore needs to distinguish between:

### Tasks being performed

and

### Decisions that need to be made.

A task can be delegated.

A major decision must remain under clear leadership.

---

# 11. Delegation

Delegation is one of the most important skills of the Trauma Team Leader.

A useful delegation structure is:

> **Person + Task + Expected outcome**

For example:

> "Ahmed, obtain two large-bore IVs and tell me when blood can be given."

Or:

> "Anaesthesia, please assess the airway and prepare for definitive airway management if the patient's mental status deteriorates."

Or:

> "Nurse in charge, activate the major haemorrhage protocol and confirm when blood arrives."

This is more effective than:

> "Someone get access."

---

# 12. Delegate According to Competence

The leader should consider:

- Clinical competence.
- Experience.
- Familiarity with the procedure.
- Current workload.
- Availability of supervision.
- Time-criticality.

The most appropriate person is not necessarily the most senior person.

A junior clinician may be entirely appropriate to perform a straightforward task.

A complex procedure may require a more experienced operator.

The leader should know the capabilities of the team.

---

# 13. Avoid Micromanagement

Micromanagement occurs when the leader becomes excessively involved in low-level tasks.

Examples include:

- Personally adjusting every piece of equipment.
- Repeatedly asking about tasks already delegated.
- Interrupting clinicians unnecessarily.
- Giving detailed procedural instructions to experienced specialists.
- Attempting to control every technical decision.

Micromanagement has several consequences:

- Cognitive overload.
- Reduced autonomy.
- Communication noise.
- Loss of situational awareness.
- Delayed decision-making.

The leader should intervene when necessary, but should otherwise allow competent team members to perform their roles.

---

# 14. The Leader Must Challenge the Team

Leadership is not simply giving instructions.

It also involves asking questions.

Useful questions include:

> "What are we missing?"

> "What is the biggest immediate threat?"

> "Is the patient responding?"

> "What is the next step if this intervention fails?"

> "Does anyone have a different concern?"

> "What is the destination?"

These questions encourage the team to contribute information.

A strong leader creates an environment in which a team member can say:

> "I am concerned."

without fear of being dismissed.

---

# 15. Psychological Safety

Trauma teams operate under significant time pressure.

Hierarchical behaviour can discourage junior staff from speaking up.

The leader should actively encourage important concerns to be voiced.

For example:

> "If anyone sees something I have missed, say it immediately."

This simple statement establishes permission to challenge.

If a nurse notices worsening blood loss, the team should hear it.

If an anaesthetist identifies a deteriorating airway, the leader should know.

If a junior doctor notices a new neurological deficit, it should be communicated.

The leader does not need to be the person who notices every problem.

The leader needs to ensure that problems are noticed and acted upon.

---

# 16. Decision Ownership

The leader does not have to personally make every decision.

However, the team should know who owns the major decisions.

Examples include:

- Whether the patient requires immediate airway intervention.
- Whether major haemorrhage activation should continue.
- Whether the patient requires urgent operative intervention.
- Whether CT is safe.
- Whether the patient should proceed directly to theatre.
- Whether interventional radiology is required.
- Whether transfer to another facility is necessary.

When responsibility is unclear, decisions may be delayed.

Therefore:

> Decision-making responsibility should be explicit.

---

# 17. The Leader Should Anticipate the Next Problem

Reactive leadership is often insufficient in major trauma.

The leader should continuously think one step ahead.

If the patient is receiving blood rapidly:

> What will we need next?

If the airway is deteriorating:

> Are we prepared for definitive airway management?

If the patient remains hypotensive:

> Where is the bleeding?

If a pelvic injury is suspected:

> Is haemorrhage control being addressed?

If the patient is stabilising:

> Is CT appropriate?

If the patient is deteriorating:

> What is the definitive intervention?

This is anticipation.

---

# 18. Maintaining the Big Picture

A useful mental model is:

### Patient

What is happening physiologically?

### Problems

What are the immediate threats?

### Interventions

What are we doing?

### Response

Is it working?

### Resources

What do we need?

### Destination

Where does the patient need to go?

The leader should repeatedly cycle through these questions.

This prevents fixation on a single problem.

---

# 19. Avoiding Fixation

A trauma team may become fixated on a dramatic finding.

Examples include:

- A severe limb injury.
- A large scalp wound.
- A visible deformity.
- A suspected fracture.
- A striking facial injury.

The leader must continually return to:

> "What will kill this patient first?"

A dramatic injury is not necessarily the most immediately dangerous injury.

The leader must prioritise physiology and life-threatening pathology.

---

# 20. Managing Competing Priorities

Trauma often produces multiple simultaneous problems.

For example:

A patient may have:

- Airway compromise.
- Massive haemorrhage.
- Chest trauma.
- Long-bone fractures.
- Traumatic brain injury.

The leader must determine which threats require immediate action and which can safely wait.

A practical principle is:

> **Treat the immediately reversible threats that are most likely to cause death first.**

This does not mean following a rigid sequence regardless of circumstances.

The primary survey provides structure.

Clinical judgement determines priority.

---

# 21. When the Leader Should Intervene Personally

The leader may need to become directly involved when:

- No appropriate team member is available.
- A time-critical intervention is failing.
- The team is becoming disorganised.
- A major decision is being delayed.
- Patient deterioration is occurring.
- The available operator lacks the required competence.
- Communication has broken down.

However, whenever possible, the leader should preserve the overall view of the resuscitation.

A leader who becomes completely occupied with one procedure may cease to function as the leader.

---

# 22. When Leadership Should Be Shared

Some trauma cases require several specialties.

Examples include:

- Severe traumatic brain injury.
- Complex vascular injury.
- Major burns.
- Pelvic haemorrhage.
- Paediatric trauma.
- Pregnancy-associated trauma.

In such cases, effective leadership does not mean excluding specialist expertise.

The Trauma Team Leader coordinates the overall resuscitation while specialist clinicians provide focused expertise.

For example:

> "Neurosurgery, please advise on the intracranial injury. I will continue coordinating the overall resuscitation."

This maintains a clear leadership structure while using specialist knowledge.

---

# 23. When Leadership Should Be Transferred

Leadership may need to change when:

- A more appropriate senior clinician arrives.
- The patient's complexity exceeds the current leader's expertise.
- The patient moves to a specialist environment.
- The initial leader becomes directly involved in another critical task.
- The patient's management transitions to another service.

Leadership transfer should be explicit.

Avoid ambiguous transitions such as:

> "You take over."

A better handover is:

> "I am transferring leadership to you. The patient remains hypotensive despite blood products. Pelvic haemorrhage is suspected. Airway is currently stable. The immediate plan is haemorrhage control and definitive destination."

The team should know who is now leading.

---

# 24. Communication With the Team

The leader should use clear, structured communication.

Important information should be verbalised.

For example:

> "Blood pressure is 78 systolic despite initial resuscitation. We are treating this as ongoing haemorrhagic shock."

This creates a shared mental model.

The team should understand:

- What is happening.
- What matters most.
- What has already been done.
- What is happening now.
- What will happen next.

---

# 25. Closed-Loop Communication

Important instructions should be acknowledged.

Example:

**Leader:**

> "Give one unit of emergency blood."

**Team member:**

> "One unit of emergency blood."

**Team member later:**

> "One unit given."

**Leader:**

> "Acknowledged."

This reduces the risk that critical instructions are misunderstood or forgotten.

Closed-loop communication is particularly important for:

- Blood products.
- Medication.
- Airway interventions.
- Major procedures.
- Time-critical imaging.
- Calls to theatre.
- Activation of major haemorrhage systems.

---

# 26. The Leader and the Monitor

The monitor provides information.

It does not lead the resuscitation.

The leader should interpret trends rather than isolated numbers.

For example:

A blood pressure of 90 mmHg may mean something very different depending on:

- Previous blood pressure.
- Heart rate.
- Mental status.
- Peripheral perfusion.
- Blood loss.
- Response to treatment.

The leader should ask:

> "What is the trend?"

not simply:

> "What is the number?"

---

# 27. The Leader Must Know When the Plan Is Failing

A major leadership skill is recognising when the current strategy is not working.

Warning signs include:

- Persistent hypotension.
- Increasing tachycardia.
- Worsening mental status.
- Increasing vasopressor or transfusion requirements.
- Persistent hypoxia.
- Increasing respiratory distress.
- Ongoing external bleeding.
- Recurrent deterioration after temporary improvement.

When the patient does not respond as expected, the leader should reconsider the diagnosis and strategy.

The question is:

> "What are we missing?"

---

# 28. The Leader Must Think Beyond the Resuscitation Room

The trauma resuscitation does not exist in isolation.

The leader should anticipate the patient's next destination early.

Potential destinations include:

- CT.
- Operating theatre.
- Interventional radiology.
- Intensive care.
- Specialist trauma centre.
- Transfer to another hospital.

The decision should be based on:

- Physiology.
- Injury pattern.
- Response to resuscitation.
- Available resources.
- Need for definitive intervention.

A patient who clearly requires operative haemorrhage control should not undergo unnecessary delays simply because the resuscitation room remains busy.

---

# 29. Common Leadership Errors

## 29.1 Doing Everything Personally

The leader becomes a proceduralist rather than a coordinator.

### Consequence

Loss of situational awareness.

### Better approach

Delegate appropriately.

---

## 29.2 Giving Too Many Instructions

Constant commands create communication noise.

### Better approach

Prioritise and communicate only what the team needs.

---

## 29.3 Failing to Reassess

An intervention is performed and the team moves on.

### Better approach

Always ask:

> "Did it work?"

---

## 29.4 Fixating on One Injury

A dramatic injury dominates attention.

### Better approach

Return repeatedly to the primary survey and physiology.

---

## 29.5 Ignoring Team Input

Junior team members may notice important abnormalities.

### Better approach

Create psychological safety and invite concerns.

---

## 29.6 Delayed Escalation

The team continues the same strategy despite deterioration.

### Better approach

Recognise when definitive intervention is required.

---

## 29.7 Unclear Leadership

Multiple clinicians give competing instructions.

### Better approach

Identify one overall leader.

---

## 29.8 Failure to Communicate the Plan

Team members work without knowing the overall strategy.

### Better approach

State the working diagnosis, priorities and next steps.

---

## 29.9 Failure to Anticipate

The team reacts to problems only after they occur.

### Better approach

Think one or two steps ahead.

# 30. Case-Based Discussion

A 24-year-old patient arrives following a high-speed motorcycle collision.

Initial observations:

- HR 138/min
- BP 76/48 mmHg
- RR 30/min
- SpO₂ 91%
- GCS 12

There is obvious deformity of the left thigh and blood is present around the pelvis.

The airway is currently patent.

The team begins several interventions.

### Question 1

Who should lead?

The person best positioned to coordinate the overall resuscitation should assume leadership.

The decision should consider experience, competence, familiarity with major trauma and the ability to coordinate the available team.

---

### Question 2

What is the immediate leadership priority?

The leader should establish control, confirm roles and identify the immediate life threats.

The physiology strongly suggests major haemorrhage.

The leader should ensure that haemorrhage control and resuscitation occur without neglecting airway and breathing assessment.

---

### Question 3

What should the leader avoid?

The leader should avoid becoming distracted by the obvious femoral deformity.

The fracture is important, but the patient's shock requires immediate attention to potentially life-threatening haemorrhage.

---

### Question 4

How should the leader coordinate the team?

Tasks should be delegated clearly.

For example:

> "Airway team, continue airway assessment and prepare for deterioration."

> "Circulation team, establish rapid access and initiate blood-based resuscitation."

> "Apply pelvic haemorrhage control."

> "I want repeated blood pressure measurements and verbal updates."

The leader then reassesses the response.

---

### Question 5

The blood pressure remains 72/44 mmHg despite initial blood products.

What should happen?

The leader should recognise that the current strategy is insufficient.

The team should reconsider the source of haemorrhage and escalate toward definitive haemorrhage control.

The question is no longer simply:

> "What can we give?"

It becomes:

> "What is the source of bleeding, and what definitive intervention does this patient require?"

---

# 31. A Practical Mental Model for the Trauma Team Leader

During the resuscitation, repeatedly ask:

### 1. What is killing the patient?

Identify the most immediate threat.

### 2. What are we doing about it?

Ensure an intervention is underway.

### 3. Is it working?

Assess the response.

### 4. What are we missing?

Challenge the current diagnosis.

### 5. What happens next?

Anticipate deterioration or definitive treatment.

### 6. Where is the patient going?

Plan the destination.

This creates a continuous leadership loop:

**Identify → Prioritise → Delegate → Intervene → Reassess → Reprioritise → Escalate → Disposition**

---

# 32. The Leader's Role in Creating Order

The resuscitation room can appear chaotic.

Noise, movement, alarms, procedures and multiple conversations are expected.

The Trauma Team Leader creates structure within that environment.

The leader converts:

**Chaos → Priorities**

**Priorities → Tasks**

**Tasks → Actions**

**Actions → Reassessment**

**Reassessment → Decisions**

This is the essence of trauma leadership.

---

# 33. Leadership Is a Clinical Intervention

Leadership should not be considered an administrative activity that occurs alongside clinical care.

Effective leadership directly affects clinical performance.

Clear leadership can:

- Reduce duplication.
- Reduce delays.
- Improve communication.
- Improve prioritisation.
- Detect deterioration earlier.
- Facilitate escalation.
- Reduce cognitive overload.
- Improve team coordination.

The leader therefore contributes to patient care even when not personally performing a procedure.

---

# 34. Key Takeaways

- The Trauma Team Leader coordinates the overall resuscitation.
- Leadership is not synonymous with seniority.
- The leader owns the process rather than every individual task.
- Maintain the big picture while the team works in parallel.
- Establish clear roles early.
- Delegate using clear instructions and expected outcomes.
- Avoid unnecessary micromanagement.
- Reassess the patient after every major intervention.
- Use physiology and response to guide priorities.
- Encourage team members to raise concerns.
- Maintain a shared mental model.
- Anticipate the next problem.
- Recognise when the current strategy is failing.
- Escalate toward definitive treatment when required.
- Plan the patient's destination early.
- Transfer leadership explicitly when appropriate.
- Effective leadership is itself a clinical intervention.

# Chapter Summary

The Trauma Team Leader is the clinician who maintains control of the **whole resuscitation**.

The leader does not need to perform every procedure.

The leader must ensure that the right problems are identified, the right priorities are established, the right tasks are delegated, and the patient's response is continuously reassessed.

The defining question for the Trauma Team Leader is therefore not:

> "What should I do?"

It is:

> **"What does this patient need next, who should do it, and how will I know whether it worked?"**

That question should remain at the centre of every major trauma resuscitation.
