# 26. Leading the Trauma Team in Rare and High-Risk Scenarios

## Introduction

Rare trauma scenarios are among the most demanding situations for a trauma team leader.

They are difficult not because the underlying principles of trauma care suddenly change, but because the team has less experience, fewer reliable expectations, greater uncertainty, and a higher risk of cognitive and communication failure.

The leader's task is therefore not to become an encyclopaedia of rare conditions.

The leader's task is to create a safe system in which the team can recognise danger, establish priorities, control immediate threats, obtain expert help, and adapt as new information appears.

A rare mechanism should never be allowed to displace the primary survey.

A dramatic injury should never automatically become the first priority.

A familiar algorithm should never be followed mechanically when the patient's physiology is deteriorating.

The central principle is:

> **Rare presentations require stronger fundamentals, not different fundamentals.**

The trauma team leader must be able to maintain structure when the case itself is unfamiliar.

This chapter describes how to lead the team when the scenario is uncommon, unusually complex, or associated with a high risk of catastrophic deterioration.

---

# 26.1 What Makes a Trauma Scenario High Risk?

A high-risk trauma scenario may be defined by one or more of the following:

- an unusual mechanism;
- an uncommon injury pattern;
- rapidly changing physiology;
- multiple simultaneous life threats;
- difficult access to the patient;
- limited equipment;
- environmental hazards;
- pregnancy;
- paediatric age;
- anticoagulation;
- major comorbidity;
- difficult airway anatomy;
- suspected vascular injury;
- major burns;
- crush injury;
- prolonged extrication;
- penetrating trauma;
- traumatic cardiac arrest;
- mass-casualty context;
- delayed recognition;
- transfer between institutions;
- or a combination of several factors.

The leader should recognise that complexity is often cumulative.

A patient does not need a rare diagnosis to become a high-risk patient.

A common injury occurring in an unusual context may be more dangerous than a rare injury in a controlled environment.

---

# 26.2 The First Principle: Do Not Chase the Rarity

Rare cases generate a powerful cognitive temptation.

The team hears an unusual mechanism and immediately begins discussing the unusual diagnosis.

This can create premature narrowing.

Examples include:

- focusing on spinal injury before controlling the airway;
- discussing aortic injury while the patient is becoming hypotensive;
- debating pelvic fracture patterns while external haemorrhage remains uncontrolled;
- discussing toxic exposure before addressing hypoxia;
- or focusing on a dramatic limb injury while the patient has an occult chest catastrophe.

The leader should actively prevent this.

A useful internal question is:

> **What is killing the patient right now?**

Then:

> **What could kill the patient in the next few minutes?**

Only after immediate threats are controlled should the team spend substantial cognitive effort on the unusual diagnosis.

---

# 26.3 Rare Does Not Mean Immediately Important

The leader should separate three concepts:

1. **Unusual**
2. **Serious**
3. **Immediately life-threatening**

These are not synonymous.

A rare finding may be clinically important but not immediately life-threatening.

Conversely, a common problem such as haemorrhage or airway obstruction may be immediately fatal.

The leader should therefore prioritise according to:

> **Physiology → threat → intervention → diagnosis**

rather than:

> **Interesting diagnosis → investigation → intervention**

---

# 26.4 Establish the Situation Before the Patient Arrives

High-risk trauma leadership begins before physical contact with the patient.

The leader should obtain the most important information available from the prehospital team.

Useful questions include:

- What happened?
- When did it happen?
- What was the mechanism?
- What was the patient's condition initially?
- What has changed?
- What interventions have already been performed?
- What was the response to those interventions?
- Is there ongoing haemorrhage?
- What are the latest observations?
- Has the patient deteriorated?
- Are there environmental or contamination risks?
- Is there anything unusual about the case?
- What resources are expected to be required?

The leader should listen for deterioration rather than simply collecting facts.

For example:

> "The patient was initially talking but became progressively confused during transport."

This is more important than simply knowing that the mechanism was unusual.

---

# 26.5 Build a High-Risk Mental Model

Before arrival, the leader should create a provisional mental model.

This should include:

### Mechanism

What forces were involved?

### Anatomy

Which structures are potentially injured?

### Physiology

What is the patient's current physiological state?

### Time

How long has the patient been injured?

### Trajectory

Is the patient improving, stable, or deteriorating?

### Resources

What will be required immediately?

### Uncertainty

What important information is still missing?

A useful formulation is:

> **Mechanism + physiology + trajectory + uncertainty = initial risk model**

The model is provisional.

It must be updated continuously.

---

# 26.6 Resource the Team Early

Rare and high-risk cases often fail because resources are requested too late.

Depending on the scenario, the leader may need early access to:

- senior anaesthetic support;
- surgical expertise;
- emergency medicine senior support;
- orthopaedic expertise;
- vascular surgery;
- neurosurgery;
- interventional radiology;
- cardiothoracic surgery;
- obstetric expertise;
- paediatric expertise;
- burns services;
- transfusion support;
- theatre staff;
- critical care;
- radiology;
- laboratory services;
- blood products;
- specialist equipment;
- additional nursing staff;
- security;
- or transfer coordination.

The principle is:

> **Call early when delay could become irreversible.**

Calling for help is not a sign that the leader has lost control.

It is a component of good leadership.

---

# 26.7 The Leader Must Remain the Integrator

In a high-risk case, several specialists may become involved simultaneously.

This creates a potential leadership problem.

Each specialist may focus appropriately on their own area.

The trauma team leader must maintain the global view.

The leader should know:

- what has been identified;
- what remains uncontrolled;
- what each team is doing;
- what decisions have been made;
- what information is still required;
- and what the next major decision point will be.

The leader should not attempt to perform every specialist task.

The leader integrates the work.

---

# 26.8 Avoid the Specialist Vacuum

When an unusual case arrives, the team may naturally look toward the most senior specialist present.

This can create an unintended transfer of leadership.

For example:

> "The vascular surgeon is here, so they are now leading."

That may or may not be appropriate.

The leadership structure should remain explicit.

A simple statement can prevent confusion:

> "I will continue to coordinate the overall resuscitation. Please lead the vascular assessment and tell me what you need."

This preserves both specialist autonomy and team coordination.

---

# 26.9 When the Leader Should Transfer Leadership

There are situations in which leadership should appropriately be transferred.

Examples include:

- a specialist team taking definitive procedural control;
- transfer to a surgical environment;
- transfer to another institution;
- a paediatric specialist team assuming care;
- or a consultant-level transition according to local policy.

The transition should be explicit.

The leader should communicate:

- current condition;
- major injuries;
- interventions;
- unresolved threats;
- response to treatment;
- pending investigations;
- and immediate concerns.

Leadership should never disappear accidentally.

---

# 26.10 Use the Primary Survey as the Anchor

Even in the rarest scenario, the primary survey remains the central organising structure.

A useful framework is:

> **Catastrophic haemorrhage → Airway → Breathing → Circulation → Disability → Exposure**

The exact institutional sequence may vary.

The principle does not.

The leader should repeatedly ask:

> "What has changed?"

and:

> "Does this change our immediate priorities?"

---

# 26.11 Reassessment Is More Important Than the Initial Impression

Rare cases frequently evolve.

The initial diagnosis may be incomplete.

The patient's physiology may reveal the real problem.

For example:

A patient may arrive apparently stable with a complex mechanism.

Ten minutes later:

- blood pressure falls;
- mental status worsens;
- respiratory effort increases;
- peripheral perfusion deteriorates.

The diagnosis now matters less than the physiological change.

The leader should respond to the patient in front of them.

---

# 26.12 The High-Risk Reassessment Loop

Use a repeated cycle:

1. Observe.
2. Interpret.
3. Prioritise.
4. Intervene.
5. Reassess.
6. Escalate if required.
7. Update the mental model.

Then repeat.

This prevents the team from becoming attached to the first diagnosis.

---

# 26.13 Penetrating Trauma

Penetrating trauma can create multiple rapidly evolving threats.

The leader should consider:

- external haemorrhage;
- airway injury;
- chest injury;
- cardiac injury;
- major vascular injury;
- abdominal injury;
- pelvic injury;
- spinal injury;
- retained foreign body;
- contamination;
- and associated injuries.

The trajectory of the patient is crucial.

A patient who is deteriorating after penetrating trauma should not be delayed by an unnecessary diagnostic search for a perfect anatomical map.

The leader should identify the intervention that is most likely to prevent death.

---

# 26.14 Multiple Penetrating Wounds

Multiple wounds may create diagnostic confusion.

The leader should ensure that the team performs a systematic assessment rather than concentrating on the most obvious wound.

Useful questions include:

- How many wounds are present?
- Where are the entry sites?
- Are exit wounds present?
- Is there active bleeding?
- Could there be multiple body cavities involved?
- Is there vascular compromise?
- Has the entire patient been examined?

The rule is:

> **Do not allow the most visible injury to hide the most dangerous injury.**

---

# 26.15 Major Vascular Injury

Suspected major vascular injury requires rapid recognition and escalation.

The leader should look for:

### Hard signs

- uncontrolled haemorrhage;
- expanding haematoma;
- absent distal perfusion;
- bruit or thrill;
- distal ischaemia;
- or obvious vascular disruption.

### Concerning features

- unexplained shock;
- proximity of injury to major vessels;
- neurological deficit;
- reduced pulses;
- or evolving limb changes.

The leader should coordinate:

- haemorrhage control;
- resuscitation;
- specialist involvement;
- imaging where appropriate;
- operative planning;
- and transfer if definitive capability is unavailable.

---

# 26.16 Junctional Haemorrhage

Junctional haemorrhage can be particularly difficult because conventional tourniquets may not control bleeding.

The leader must ensure that:

- bleeding is identified;
- direct pressure is applied when appropriate;
- haemostatic techniques are used when indicated;
- specialist haemorrhage-control strategies are considered;
- blood products are activated early when appropriate;
- and definitive control is pursued without unnecessary delay.

The leader should repeatedly ask:

> "Is the bleeding actually controlled?"

A dressing covering blood is not the same as haemorrhage control.

---

# 26.17 Crush Injury

Crush injuries create both immediate and delayed threats.

The leader should anticipate:

- occult haemorrhage;
- compartment syndrome;
- reperfusion-related deterioration;
- hyperkalaemia;
- acidosis;
- renal injury;
- rhabdomyolysis;
- and cardiovascular instability.

A prolonged extrication may also create a false sense of reassurance if the patient initially appears stable.

The team should plan ahead for deterioration rather than waiting for it.

---

# 26.18 Prolonged Entrapment

The leader should establish:

- duration of entrapment;
- location of compression;
- degree of tissue involvement;
- environmental temperature;
- fluids already given;
- analgesia;
- bleeding;
- consciousness;
- and available extraction plan.

Communication with the rescue team is essential.

The leader should avoid creating conflicting priorities.

---

# 26.19 Traumatic Cardiac Arrest

Traumatic cardiac arrest requires a different emphasis from medical cardiac arrest.

The leader must rapidly consider reversible traumatic causes.

These may include:

- catastrophic haemorrhage;
- airway obstruction;
- tension pneumothorax;
- cardiac tamponade;
- and other immediately reversible traumatic causes.

The precise intervention sequence should follow local trauma and resuscitation protocols.

The important leadership principle is:

> **Treat reversible traumatic causes while maintaining effective team coordination.**

The leader should ensure that CPR, where indicated, does not become a substitute for correcting the underlying traumatic cause.

---

# 26.20 Major Burns

Major burns introduce additional complexity.

The leader should anticipate:

- airway injury;
- inhalation injury;
- hypoxia;
- thermal injury;
- chemical exposure;
- ongoing heat loss;
- fluid requirements;
- associated trauma;
- circumferential burns;
- and the need for specialist transfer.

The burn should not become the only focus.

A burned patient may also have:

- cervical injury;
- blunt trauma;
- penetrating injury;
- fractures;
- or occult haemorrhage.

---

# 26.21 Inhalation Injury

The leader should maintain a low threshold for recognising evolving airway compromise in an appropriate clinical context.

Potential warning features may include:

- facial burns;
- soot;
- airway swelling;
- voice change;
- respiratory distress;
- altered consciousness;
- or significant exposure to smoke or hot gases.

The important leadership issue is anticipation.

An airway that is currently manageable may become difficult later.

The leader should therefore coordinate early expert airway assessment when the risk is significant.

---

# 26.22 Chemical Trauma

Chemical contamination introduces a second problem:

> **The patient may also be a hazard to the team.**

The leader must establish:

- scene safety;
- decontamination requirements;
- personal protective equipment;
- isolation requirements;
- specialist advice;
- and safe access.

No trauma resuscitation should create preventable secondary casualties among staff.

---

# 26.23 Electrical Injury

Electrical injury can produce injuries that are not immediately visible.

The leader should consider:

- cardiac effects;
- burns;
- neurological injury;
- muscular injury;
- falls;
- associated fractures;
- and compartment problems.

The mechanism should therefore prompt a systematic assessment rather than reliance on external appearance.

---

# 26.24 Blast Injury

Blast trauma can produce multiple injury patterns simultaneously.

The leader should consider:

### Primary blast effects

Pressure-related injury.

### Secondary effects

Penetrating injury from fragments.

### Tertiary effects

Blunt trauma from displacement.

### Quaternary effects

Burns, inhalation injury, toxic exposure, or other associated injury.

The leader should therefore avoid anchoring on one mechanism.

---

# 26.25 High-Energy Falls

A high-energy fall should trigger structured assessment without automatically dictating a specific diagnosis.

The leader should establish:

- estimated height;
- landing surface;
- body position;
- impact sequence;
- consciousness;
- secondary impact;
- and subsequent symptoms.

The physiological response remains more important than the dramatic description of the mechanism.

---

# 26.26 Difficult Airway in Trauma

The difficult airway is one of the highest-risk situations for trauma leadership.

Potential contributors include:

- facial trauma;
- blood;
- vomitus;
- airway swelling;
- burns;
- cervical injury;
- anatomical distortion;
- limited mouth opening;
- or reduced access.

The leader must establish:

- who is leading the airway;
- what the primary plan is;
- what the backup plan is;
- what equipment is available;
- and when escalation will occur.

A useful principle is:

> **Do not wait until the first airway plan fails before thinking about the second.**

---

# 26.27 Airway Leadership

The trauma team leader should not crowd the airway operator.

The leader should establish the plan and monitor the global situation.

Useful communication includes:

> "What is your primary airway plan?"

Then:

> "What is your backup if that fails?"

And:

> "Tell me immediately if you need additional help."

This creates shared awareness without micromanagement.

---

# 26.28 Cervical Spine Risk

Cervical spine concerns can influence airway management, positioning, and transport.

The leader should avoid allowing immobilisation concerns to delay management of immediate life threats.

The principle is:

> **Protect the spine while treating the patient, but do not allow theoretical protection to prevent essential life-saving care.**

Local protocols should determine the precise approach.

---

# 26.29 Pregnancy and Major Trauma

Pregnancy creates additional physiological and logistical complexity.

The leader should consider:

- maternal resuscitation;
- fetal considerations;
- gestational age;
- obstetric involvement;
- haemorrhage;
- positioning;
- imaging;
- operative requirements;
- and transfer destination.

The mother remains the immediate resuscitation priority because maternal stabilisation is fundamental to fetal survival.

The leader should involve obstetric expertise early when appropriate.

---

# 26.30 Paediatric High-Risk Trauma

Children present additional leadership challenges.

The leader must account for:

- age;
- size;
- developmental stage;
- equipment availability;
- weight-based medication;
- communication with caregivers;
- physiological reserve;
- and rapid deterioration.

The team should ensure that paediatric expertise and appropriately sized equipment are available.

The leader should not assume that adult trauma processes can simply be scaled down.

---

# 26.31 The Child Who Appears Well

A child who appears initially well may still have significant injury.

The leader should therefore avoid false reassurance.

Mechanism, examination, trajectory, and repeated reassessment remain important.

The question is:

> "Is the child genuinely stable, or merely compensating?"

---

# 26.32 Anticoagulated Trauma Patient

Anticoagulation increases concern about occult or progressive bleeding.

The leader should establish:

- medication;
- indication;
- timing;
- dose where relevant;
- renal function where relevant;
- and available reversal strategy.

The presence of anticoagulation should increase vigilance without automatically replacing the primary survey.

---

# 26.33 The Patient With Significant Comorbidity

Major trauma may occur in patients with:

- cardiovascular disease;
- respiratory disease;
- renal disease;
- liver disease;
- diabetes;
- frailty;
- or other significant conditions.

The leader should distinguish:

> **What is caused by trauma?**

from:

> **What is caused by the patient's baseline physiology?**

This distinction may be difficult.

The safest approach is often to assume a dangerous cause until a less dangerous explanation is adequately supported.

---

# 26.34 Frailty and Older Trauma Patients

Older patients may sustain major injury from apparently minor mechanisms.

The leader should recognise that:

- physiological reserve may be reduced;
- anticoagulation may be present;
- osteoporosis may increase fracture risk;
- baseline cognition may complicate assessment;
- and deterioration may occur with little warning.

A low-energy mechanism should not automatically produce a low-risk assessment.

---

# 26.35 Hypothermia

Hypothermia is particularly relevant in prolonged trauma resuscitation.

The leader should anticipate:

- impaired coagulation;
- altered physiology;
- worsening bleeding;
- delayed recovery;
- and increased resuscitation difficulty.

Warmth should be treated as part of resuscitation rather than an optional comfort measure.

---

# 26.36 The Patient Who Deteriorates Unexpectedly

Unexpected deterioration is one of the clearest tests of trauma leadership.

The leader should immediately create a new shared mental model.

A useful statement is:

> "The patient's physiology has changed. Stop non-essential tasks. Let's reassess from the beginning and identify the immediate reversible cause."

This resets the team.

---

# 26.37 Resetting the Team

A team reset may include:

1. State the change.
2. State the current concern.
3. Re-establish priorities.
4. Allocate tasks.
5. Confirm interventions.
6. Reassess response.

Example:

> "Blood pressure is falling and the patient is becoming less responsive. We are now treating this as active deterioration. Continue airway monitoring, reassess breathing, establish haemorrhage control, prepare blood, and update me after the next set of observations."

---

# 26.38 Avoiding Diagnostic Anchoring

Anchoring occurs when the team becomes attached to an initial explanation.

Examples:

> "It is probably neurogenic shock."

> "The hypotension is probably from the analgesia."

> "The patient is confused because of intoxication."

> "The chest findings are probably just contusions."

The leader should challenge assumptions when the physiology does not fit.

A useful question is:

> **"What else could explain this deterioration?"**

---

# 26.39 Avoiding Premature Closure

Premature closure occurs when the team stops searching after finding one plausible diagnosis.

The leader should ask:

> "What have we not explained?"

For example:

A patient has a pelvic fracture.

But:

- Why is the airway deteriorating?
- Why is the oxygen requirement increasing?
- Why is the blood pressure continuing to fall?
- Why is the abdomen becoming distended?

One diagnosis does not exclude another.

---

# 26.40 Managing Conflicting Information

High-risk cases frequently generate contradictory information.

One clinician may report:

> "The abdomen is soft."

Another may report:

> "There is significant abdominal tenderness."

The leader should not immediately choose one opinion.

Instead:

1. Clarify the observation.
2. Repeat the assessment if necessary.
3. Consider the clinical context.
4. Identify whether the disagreement changes immediate management.

The leader's goal is not to determine who is correct.

The goal is to establish the safest next action.

---

# 26.41 When the Team Disagrees

Disagreement can be useful when handled correctly.

The leader should invite relevant concerns.

For example:

> "I hear your concern. What finding is driving it?"

Then:

> "Does this change what we need to do in the next five minutes?"

This converts disagreement into clinical information.

---

# 26.42 Psychological Safety

Team members must be able to speak up.

A high-performing leader makes it acceptable to say:

- "I am concerned."
- "I do not think this is working."
- "The patient is getting worse."
- "I think we are missing something."
- "I need help."
- "Can we reassess?"

The leader should never punish appropriate escalation.

---

# 26.43 The Two-Challenge Principle

If a team member identifies a serious safety concern and it is not acknowledged, they should be encouraged to raise it again clearly.

For example:

> "I am concerned the bleeding is not controlled."

If ignored:

> "I need to escalate this concern. I do not think the bleeding is controlled."

The leader should respond explicitly.

---

# 26.44 Managing a Specialist Who Becomes Task-Focused

A specialist may become deeply focused on a procedure.

This is understandable.

However, the overall team may lose situational awareness.

The leader can intervene respectfully:

> "Continue the procedure. I will coordinate the overall resuscitation. Tell me immediately if the situation changes."

This preserves focus while restoring coordination.

---

# 26.45 Managing Multiple Procedures

High-risk cases may involve several procedures simultaneously.

The leader should consider:

- Which procedure is immediately life-saving?
- Which can wait?
- Which requires the most experienced operator?
- Which requires additional resources?
- Which may interfere with another intervention?

The aim is not maximum activity.

The aim is maximum benefit.

---

# 26.46 Avoiding Parallel Chaos

Parallel processing is useful only when coordinated.

Without coordination, multiple simultaneous tasks can create:

- duplicated work;
- missed tasks;
- conflicting interventions;
- poor communication;
- equipment shortages;
- and loss of awareness.

The leader should maintain a task map.

---

# 26.47 The Task Map

A simple mental or verbal task map may include:

### Airway
Who is responsible?

### Breathing
Who is assessing and treating?

### Circulation
Who is controlling haemorrhage and obtaining access?

### Monitoring
Who is tracking observations?

### Investigations
Who is coordinating imaging and laboratory work?

### Blood
Who is managing blood products?

### Procedure
Who is performing the definitive intervention?

### Communication
Who is updating family, receiving teams, and specialists?

### Documentation
Who is recording key events?

---

# 26.48 Managing Limited Resources

High-risk trauma may occur when resources are constrained.

Examples include:

- limited blood products;
- limited theatre access;
- unavailable specialist services;
- equipment shortage;
- simultaneous trauma patients;
- or transfer delays.

The leader must make priorities explicit.

A useful principle is:

> **Use scarce resources where they have the greatest immediate effect on survival.**

This requires coordination with senior staff and local systems.

---

# 26.49 When the Required Capability Is Not Available

If definitive treatment is unavailable locally, the leader should identify this early.

The team should determine:

- what can be done immediately;
- what cannot be done;
- what must be stabilised before transfer;
- which receiving service is required;
- transport requirements;
- and who is responsible for communication.

The worst situation is recognising the need for transfer only after the patient has deteriorated beyond safe transport.

---

# 26.50 Transfer Is an Active Intervention

Transfer should not be viewed as administrative movement.

It is a clinical intervention.

The leader must ensure that:

- the patient is appropriately stabilised;
- airway and breathing are secure;
- haemorrhage control is adequate;
- monitoring is appropriate;
- medications and blood products are available as required;
- equipment accompanies the patient;
- receiving clinicians understand the case;
- and deterioration contingencies are understood.

---

# 26.51 Communicating the Rare Case

A complex case should not be communicated as a long unstructured story.

Use a structured format.

For example:

### Situation

Who is the patient and what is happening now?

### Mechanism

What caused the trauma?

### Injuries

What has been identified?

### Physiology

What are the current observations?

### Interventions

What has already been done?

### Response

How did the patient respond?

### Concerns

What remains uncontrolled?

### Request

What do you need from the receiving or specialist team?

---

# 26.52 The Leader's Briefing

A high-risk team briefing should be concise.

Example:

> "This is a high-energy penetrating trauma with suspected major vascular injury and ongoing shock. We will continue structured trauma assessment while controlling haemorrhage. Vascular surgery is being contacted now. Anaesthesia will lead the airway. Blood is being prepared. We will reassess after the next intervention and decide whether the patient proceeds directly to definitive haemorrhage control."

This gives the team a shared direction.

---

# 26.53 Keep the Team Ahead of the Patient

The best leaders anticipate the next decision.

Ask:

> "If the patient deteriorates, what will we do?"

> "If the airway fails, who takes over?"

> "If the blood pressure falls further, what is our next intervention?"

> "If the scan shows major vascular injury, where does the patient go?"

> "If the patient cannot be managed here, who is receiving them?"

Anticipation converts surprises into prepared actions.

---

# 26.54 The Three-Horizon Model

A useful framework is to think across three horizons.

### Horizon 1 — Now

What must happen in the next minute?

### Horizon 2 — Next

What is likely to be required in the next five to fifteen minutes?

### Horizon 3 — Definitive

What is the likely definitive pathway?

The leader should keep all three horizons active.

---

# 26.55 Do Not Over-Plan

Anticipation should not become speculation.

The leader should prepare for plausible deterioration rather than every theoretical possibility.

A useful principle is:

> **Prepare for what is plausible, not everything that is possible.**

---

# 26.56 Recognising the Point of No Return

Some decisions become progressively harder to reverse.

Examples may include:

- delaying definitive haemorrhage control;
- delaying specialist activation;
- delaying airway escalation;
- delaying transfer;
- or allowing prolonged physiological deterioration.

The leader should recognise when waiting has become more dangerous than acting.

---

# 26.57 Escalation Thresholds

The team should know what findings will trigger escalation.

Examples include:

- persistent shock;
- worsening mental status;
- uncontrolled haemorrhage;
- increasing oxygen requirement;
- failed initial airway strategy;
- worsening acidosis;
- recurrent deterioration;
- or inability to provide definitive treatment.

The exact thresholds depend on the clinical context and local protocols.

The leadership principle is to make escalation criteria explicit.

---

# 26.58 The High-Risk Safety Pause

A brief safety pause may be appropriate before a major transition.

For example:

> "Before we move: airway plan confirmed, haemorrhage controlled as far as possible, blood available, monitoring attached, destination confirmed, and receiving team aware."

A pause of a few seconds can prevent major omissions.

---

# 26.59 When the Case Becomes Chaotic

If the team begins talking over each other, moving without coordination, or repeatedly changing plans, the leader should slow the system down.

A firm reset may be necessary:

> "Stop for a moment. One person speaks at a time. I need the current observations, the uncontrolled threat, and the next intervention."

This is not a delay.

It is a safety intervention.

---

# 26.60 The Leader's Voice During Crisis

The leader should use:

- short sentences;
- clear names;
- direct instructions;
- closed-loop communication;
- explicit priorities;
- and calm tone.

Avoid:

- long speeches;
- vague instructions;
- rhetorical questions;
- multiple instructions to one person;
- and unnecessary discussion during immediate threats.

---

# 26.61 Rare Cases and Cognitive Load

High-risk trauma increases cognitive load for everyone.

The leader should reduce unnecessary cognitive demand by:

- using structured assessments;
- naming priorities;
- assigning tasks clearly;
- repeating critical information;
- using checklists when appropriate;
- and externalising important information.

The more complex the situation becomes, the more valuable structure becomes.

---

# 26.62 Use Checklists as Cognitive Support

A checklist should support—not replace—clinical judgement.

It can help the team remember:

- equipment;
- blood;
- medications;
- specialist calls;
- transfer requirements;
- documentation;
- and reassessment.

The leader should not allow checklist completion to become more important than patient care.

---

# 26.63 Managing Information Overload

High-risk cases can generate too much information.

The leader should filter information according to:

### Does it change immediate management?

If yes, communicate it.

### Does it change the next decision?

If yes, retain it.

### Is it interesting but not actionable?

Defer it.

This protects attention.

---

# 26.64 The Information Hierarchy

The leader should prioritise:

1. Immediate life threats.
2. Physiological deterioration.
3. Response to interventions.
4. Definitive treatment requirements.
5. Important diagnostic information.
6. Background information.

This hierarchy should remain stable even when the case is unusual.

---

# 26.65 When the Diagnosis Is Uncertain

Uncertainty is normal.

The leader should be able to say:

> "The diagnosis is not yet clear. We are treating the immediate threats while we clarify the cause."

This is better than pretending certainty.

Good leadership does not require perfect diagnostic confidence.

It requires safe action under uncertainty.

---

# 26.66 The Difference Between Confidence and Certainty

A leader can be confident about the next action without being certain about the diagnosis.

For example:

> "I am not certain which structure is injured, but the patient is in shock with ongoing bleeding, so haemorrhage control is the priority."

This is appropriate clinical reasoning.

---

# 26.67 When New Information Changes the Plan

Changing the plan is not failure.

Failure occurs when the team refuses to change despite new evidence.

The leader should explicitly state:

> "The new information changes our priorities."

This helps the team understand why the plan has changed.

---

# 26.68 Avoiding Commitment to a Failed Strategy

Once an intervention has failed, the team should not continue repeating it indefinitely.

The leader should ask:

> "Is this working?"

If not:

> "What is our next strategy?"

This is particularly important in airway management, haemorrhage control, and resuscitation.

---

# 26.69 The Failure-to-Rescue Concept

High-risk trauma often involves deterioration despite appropriate initial management.

The leader's responsibility is to recognise failure early and rescue the patient.

This requires:

- early recognition;
- escalation;
- reassessment;
- alternative strategies;
- and specialist involvement.

A failed first intervention should trigger adaptation.

---

# 26.70 Leadership When the Patient Is Dying

Some patients will remain critically unstable despite maximal treatment.

The leader must maintain:

- clinical focus;
- dignity;
- communication;
- team coordination;
- and appropriate decision-making.

The team should know whether the goal remains:

- active resuscitation;
- definitive intervention;
- damage-control treatment;
- or transition toward end-of-life care.

Ambiguity can cause both unnecessary treatment and inadequate treatment.

---

# 26.71 When Goals of Care Change

If the clinical situation changes significantly, the leader should make the new goal explicit.

For example:

> "Our priority is now comfort and dignity rather than further invasive resuscitation."

Or, when escalation remains appropriate:

> "We are continuing active resuscitation. The patient remains potentially salvageable, and we are moving toward definitive haemorrhage control."

The team should hear the change clearly.

---

# 26.72 Family Communication in High-Risk Trauma

Family communication should be structured and honest.

Avoid:

- false reassurance;
- unnecessary speculation;
- excessive technical language;
- or contradictory messages from multiple clinicians.

A suitable approach is:

> "Your relative has sustained very serious injuries. The team is treating several immediate problems. We do not yet know the final outcome, but we are actively managing the life-threatening injuries."

---

# 26.73 Managing Media and External Pressure

Some rare trauma cases attract attention.

The trauma team should not allow external pressure to disrupt clinical care.

Information sharing should follow:

- institutional policy;
- confidentiality requirements;
- and designated communication pathways.

The clinical team should remain focused on patient safety.

---

# 26.74 High-Risk Trauma and Team Fatigue

Complex cases can be prolonged.

Fatigue increases the risk of:

- omission;
- poor communication;
- irritability;
- tunnel vision;
- and decision error.

The leader should consider rotation of tasks where possible.

A clinician who has been performing a demanding task for a prolonged period may need relief.

---

# 26.75 Protecting the Team

The leader should monitor both patient and team.

Look for:

- exhaustion;
- confusion;
- conflict;
- repeated errors;
- loss of communication;
- or emotional overload.

A short reset can restore performance.

---

# 26.76 When the Leader Is Overloaded

The leader should recognise their own cognitive limitations.

A useful response is to delegate.

For example:

> "I need someone to monitor the overall timeline and keep track of pending tasks."

This creates an additional cognitive support layer.

The leader does not need to remember everything personally.

---

# 26.77 The Second Leader

In a prolonged or exceptionally complex case, appointing a second senior clinician to assist with coordination can be valuable.

That person may track:

- time;
- interventions;
- investigations;
- blood products;
- specialist calls;
- and pending decisions.

This creates redundancy.

---

# 26.78 The Team Leader's Dashboard

The leader should mentally maintain five domains:

### Patient
What is the physiology?

### Threats
What can kill the patient?

### Tasks
What is being done?

### Resources
What is needed?

### Destination
Where is the patient going?

If any of these becomes unclear, the leader should re-establish it.

---

# 26.79 Rare Trauma and Documentation

Documentation should capture the major clinical events.

Important elements include:

- time of arrival;
- mechanism;
- initial observations;
- major findings;
- interventions;
- response;
- deterioration;
- specialist involvement;
- major decisions;
- transfer decisions;
- and destination.

Documentation should reflect the clinical reasoning, particularly when major decisions were made under uncertainty.

---

# 26.80 Learning From Rare Cases

Rare cases are valuable learning opportunities.

After the event, the team should ask:

- What went well?
- What did not go well?
- What surprised us?
- What information was missing?
- What resources were delayed?
- Where did communication fail?
- What could be anticipated earlier?
- What should change next time?

The objective is not blame.

The objective is system improvement.

---

# 26.81 From Case Memory to System Memory

A dangerous outcome should not become merely a story that clinicians remember.

It should become an improvement opportunity.

For example:

> "We nearly ran out of blood products."

should lead to:

> "What system change will prevent this in the future?"

Similarly:

> "We struggled to find the equipment."

should lead to:

> "Where should the equipment be stored and how should its availability be checked?"

---

# 26.82 Rare Cases Should Improve Common Practice

A lesson from a rare case may improve everyday trauma care.

For example:

- clearer team briefing;
- better equipment preparation;
- earlier specialist activation;
- improved documentation;
- better escalation pathways;
- stronger closed-loop communication;
- or more reliable transfer processes.

The value of a rare case extends beyond the individual patient.

---

# 26.83 The High-Risk Trauma Leader's Checklist

Before the patient arrives:

- What is the mechanism?
- What is the likely physiology?
- What could kill the patient immediately?
- What resources may be required?
- Which specialists should be alerted?
- Is there an environmental hazard?
- Is a transfer likely?

During the primary survey:

- Is catastrophic bleeding controlled?
- Is the airway safe?
- Is ventilation adequate?
- Is circulation adequate?
- Is the patient neurologically deteriorating?
- Has the patient been fully exposed and assessed?

During deterioration:

- What has changed?
- What is the most likely immediate cause?
- What reversible threats must be treated now?
- Who is doing what?
- Do we need additional help?
- Does the destination need to change?

Before transfer:

- Is the patient as stable as reasonably achievable?
- Is the airway plan clear?
- Is haemorrhage control adequate?
- Is monitoring appropriate?
- Are medications and equipment available?
- Has the receiving team been informed?
- Is the transport team prepared for deterioration?

After the case:

- What happened?
- What went well?
- What failed?
- What surprised us?
- What should change?

---

# 26.84 The Leader's Rare-Case Script

A useful opening statement is:

> "This is an unusual and high-risk trauma case. We will not allow the unusual mechanism to distract us from the immediate threats. I will coordinate the overall resuscitation. Please use names, confirm critical instructions, and call out deterioration immediately."

Then:

> "Our priorities are catastrophic haemorrhage, airway, breathing, circulation, disability, and exposure. We will reassess continuously."

Then:

> "I want specialist support activated early where definitive treatment may be required."

This establishes structure before complexity takes over.

---

# 26.85 The Leader's Escalation Script

When the patient deteriorates:

> "The patient is deteriorating. Stop non-essential activity."

> "Give me the current observations."

> "What has changed?"

> "What immediate reversible cause are we treating?"

> "Who is responsible for that intervention?"

> "What is our backup plan?"

> "I want senior support involved now."

This sequence restores shared awareness.

---

# 26.86 The Leader's Transfer Script

Before transfer:

> "Let's confirm the transfer plan."

> "Current diagnosis and major injuries?"

> "Current physiology?"

> "What interventions have been performed?"

> "What remains uncontrolled?"

> "What could deteriorate during transport?"

> "Who is receiving the patient?"

> "Is the receiving team aware of the current condition?"

> "Do we have the equipment and personnel required?"

Only then should the patient leave.

---

# 26.87 The Leader's Final Mental Model

In a rare and high-risk trauma scenario, the leader should repeatedly think:

> **What is happening?**

> **What is killing the patient?**

> **What are we doing about it?**

> **Is it working?**

> **What happens next?**

> **What if it fails?**

> **Who needs to know?**

> **Where does the patient need to go?**

These questions provide a stable framework even when the case is unfamiliar.

---

# 26.88 Core Principles

The most important principles of leading rare and high-risk trauma are:

1. Do not chase the rarity.
2. Treat physiology before pursuing diagnostic elegance.
3. Use the primary survey as the anchor.
4. Anticipate deterioration.
5. Call for specialist help early when appropriate.
6. Maintain explicit leadership.
7. Give specialists clear domains while retaining global coordination.
8. Use closed-loop communication.
9. Reassess after every major intervention.
10. Challenge anchoring and premature closure.
11. Prepare for failure of the first strategy.
12. Protect the team from preventable hazards.
13. Recognise when local capability is insufficient.
14. Treat transfer as a clinical intervention.
15. Communicate uncertainty honestly.
16. Make changing priorities explicit.
17. Protect psychological safety.
18. Use structure to reduce cognitive load.
19. Learn from rare cases systematically.
20. Keep the patient—not the diagnosis—at the centre of the resuscitation.

---

# 26.89 The Central Leadership Principle

Rare trauma is not primarily a test of how many unusual diagnoses the leader can name.

It is a test of whether the leader can maintain a reliable system when uncertainty is high.

The strongest trauma leader does not need to know every possible rare injury.

They need to know:

- how to recognise deterioration;
- how to prioritise;
- how to mobilise expertise;
- how to communicate;
- how to anticipate failure;
- how to adapt;
- and how to keep the team focused on the patient's immediate needs.

The leader's greatest value is not knowing everything.

It is ensuring that the team collectively sees what matters.

> **When the scenario is rare, make the leadership more structured.**
>
> **When the physiology is unstable, make the priorities simpler.**
>
> **When uncertainty increases, communicate more clearly.**
>
> **When the first plan fails, adapt early.**
>
> **When the case becomes complex, return to fundamentals.**

---

# 26.90 Closing Perspective

The trauma team will occasionally encounter cases for which no one in the room has a complete mental script.

That is normal.

The dangerous response is to allow uncertainty to produce chaos.

The safer response is to increase structure.

The leader establishes the immediate priorities, allocates responsibility, activates appropriate expertise, monitors the patient's trajectory, anticipates the next problem, and repeatedly reassesses whether the current strategy is working.

In this environment, leadership is not about appearing certain.

It is about creating a team capable of acting safely despite uncertainty.

The rare case should therefore not cause the team to abandon its fundamentals.

It should make those fundamentals more visible.

> **Structured leadership turns an unfamiliar trauma into a manageable sequence of decisions.**
