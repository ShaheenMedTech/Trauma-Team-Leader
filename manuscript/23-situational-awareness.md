# Chapter 23 — Situational Awareness

## 17.1 Why Situational Awareness Matters

Situational awareness is the ability to understand what is happening now, recognise what it means, and anticipate what is likely to happen next.

For the trauma team leader, situational awareness is not simply knowing the patient's vital signs.

It is maintaining an accurate mental model of:

- the patient's current physiological state
- the mechanism and likely injuries
- the interventions already performed
- the patient's response to those interventions
- the trajectory of the resuscitation
- the actions currently being performed by the team
- the tasks that remain outstanding
- the resources available
- the resources that may soon be required
- the likely next clinical problem
- the definitive destination of the patient

A leader can make technically correct decisions and still lose control of a resuscitation if situational awareness is poor.

The leader's job is therefore not merely to react to events.

The leader must remain ahead of the resuscitation.

> **Know where the patient is. Know where the team is. Know where the resuscitation is going.**

---

## 17.2 The Three Levels of Situational Awareness

A useful framework divides situational awareness into three levels.

### Level 1 — Perception

What is happening?

The leader gathers relevant information from:

- the patient
- monitors
- examination
- team members
- procedures
- investigations
- mechanism
- response to treatment

Examples:

- blood pressure is falling
- oxygen saturation is worsening
- the patient is becoming more agitated
- the chest is becoming increasingly asymmetrical
- blood loss is increasing
- the pelvis is unstable
- the patient has not responded to initial resuscitation

This is the information-gathering stage.

---

### Level 2 — Comprehension

What does it mean?

The leader must interpret the information rather than simply collect it.

For example:

> "The blood pressure is 70 systolic."

is perception.

Whereas:

> "The patient remains profoundly shocked despite initial blood product resuscitation, suggesting ongoing major haemorrhage."

is comprehension.

The leader converts observations into clinical meaning.

---

### Level 3 — Projection

What is likely to happen next?

This is the highest level.

The leader anticipates the next problem before it becomes an emergency.

For example:

- "The patient is continuing to bleed and will probably require massive transfusion."
- "The airway is currently patent, but the patient's mental state is deteriorating."
- "We are approaching the point where definitive haemorrhage control will be required."
- "If the blood pressure continues to fall, we will need immediate surgical intervention."
- "The CT scan is unlikely to be appropriate while the patient remains physiologically unstable."

Projection converts situational awareness into anticipation.

> **The strongest trauma leaders do not merely recognise deterioration. They anticipate it.**

---

# 17.3 Building the Mental Picture

At any moment, the leader should be able to answer a small number of questions.

### The patient

- Who is this patient?
- What is the mechanism?
- What are the major suspected injuries?
- What is the current physiological state?
- Is the patient improving, stable, or deteriorating?

### The resuscitation

- What has already been done?
- What has worked?
- What has failed?
- What remains outstanding?
- What is the immediate life threat?

### The team

- Who is doing what?
- Which critical tasks are currently in progress?
- Has each task been acknowledged?
- Is anyone overloaded?
- Is any important task unattended?

### The destination

- Where does this patient need to go?
- What definitive intervention is required?
- What must happen before transfer?
- What resources will be needed?

If the leader cannot answer these questions, situational awareness may already be deteriorating.

---

# 17.4 Situational Awareness Is Dynamic

Situational awareness is not something established once at the beginning of the resuscitation.

It must be continuously updated.

Trauma patients change.

The team changes.

The available information changes.

The diagnosis may change.

The priorities may change.

The leader must therefore repeatedly update the mental model.

A useful cycle is:

> **Observe → Interpret → Anticipate → Act → Reassess**

This cycle continues throughout the resuscitation.

---

# 17.5 Observe

The leader continuously collects relevant information.

Sources include:

- direct patient assessment
- vital signs
- monitor trends
- physical examination
- blood results
- imaging
- procedural findings
- blood loss
- urine output
- response to interventions
- information from prehospital teams
- information from nursing staff
- information from procedural clinicians

The leader should avoid collecting information indiscriminately.

Not every available piece of information is equally important.

The question is:

> **What information changes what we do next?**

---

# 17.6 Interpret

Information must be converted into meaning.

For example:

A heart rate of 130 is not simply a number.

In context, it may represent:

- haemorrhage
- pain
- hypoxia
- agitation
- hypothermia
- medication effect
- another physiological stressor

Likewise, a falling blood pressure is not simply a monitor reading.

It is a signal requiring interpretation in the context of:

- bleeding
- cardiac injury
- tension pneumothorax
- spinal injury
- anaesthetic effect
- evolving physiological failure

The leader should continuously ask:

> **"What does this change mean?"**

---

# 17.7 Anticipate

After interpreting the current situation, the leader asks:

> **"What happens next if nothing changes?"**

This question is extremely powerful.

If the patient is bleeding:

> What will happen if haemorrhage control is delayed?

If the airway is deteriorating:

> What will happen if the airway is lost?

If the patient is becoming hypothermic:

> What will happen if warming is delayed?

If the team is becoming overloaded:

> What task is likely to be missed?

Anticipation allows intervention before deterioration becomes catastrophic.

---

# 17.8 Act

Once the leader identifies the likely problem, action should follow.

The action may involve:

- treating a life threat
- changing priorities
- assigning a new task
- requesting additional personnel
- activating a massive transfusion pathway
- obtaining blood products
- preparing for surgery
- preparing for transfer
- stopping an inappropriate investigation
- changing the destination
- escalating to a specialist team

Action should be explicit.

> "We are concerned about ongoing haemorrhage. Activate the massive transfusion protocol and prepare the patient for definitive haemorrhage control."

---

# 17.9 Reassess

After intervention, the leader must determine whether the situation has changed.

Ask:

- Did the patient improve?
- Did the intervention achieve its intended effect?
- Is the original diagnosis still likely?
- Is another problem now more important?
- Has the team's priority changed?

Failure to reassess is a common cause of ineffective resuscitation.

An intervention is not complete simply because it has been performed.

Its effect must be evaluated.

---

# 17.10 Trends Matter More Than Isolated Values

A single vital sign may be misleading.

Trends are often more informative.

For example:

> BP 100 → 92 → 84 → 72

is more concerning than any individual value alone.

Likewise:

> HR 105 → 118 → 130 → 145

suggests deterioration even if the blood pressure has not yet collapsed.

The leader should therefore think in terms of trajectory.

Ask:

> **"Where is the patient going?"**

not simply:

> **"Where is the patient now?"**

---

# 17.11 The Importance of Response to Treatment

The response to an intervention provides diagnostic information.

For example:

- Does oxygenation improve after airway optimisation?
- Does ventilation improve after chest intervention?
- Does circulation improve after haemorrhage control?
- Does blood pressure respond appropriately to blood products?
- Does mental status improve after correction of hypoxia or shock?

Failure to respond should trigger reassessment.

> **A treatment that does not produce the expected physiological response is information.**

The leader should not simply repeat the same intervention without asking why it failed.

---

# 17.12 The Trauma Team as a Sensor Network

The leader does not personally see everything.

The team is an information network.

Different team members observe different aspects of the patient.

For example:

The airway clinician may notice:

- increasing difficulty with ventilation
- worsening airway obstruction
- changing airway pressures
- blood or secretions affecting the airway

The nurse may notice:

- increasing blood loss
- abnormal monitor trends
- difficulty obtaining access
- delays in medication or blood products

The surgeon may notice:

- findings suggesting operative haemorrhage
- worsening abdominal distension
- uncontrolled external bleeding

The radiographer may notice:

- difficulty obtaining imaging safely
- unexpected findings on initial imaging

The leader must therefore actively invite information.

Useful phrases include:

> "Any concerns from the airway?"

> "Nursing team, what are you seeing?"

> "What is the blood loss?"

> "Has the patient responded to the blood?"

> "Any concerns before we move?"

The leader should not assume that silence means everything is normal.

---

# 17.13 Closed-Loop Information Sharing

Important information should be acknowledged.

For example:

> "The blood pressure is now 70."

Leader:

> "Thank you. We have ongoing shock."

This confirms that the information has entered the team's shared mental model.

For critical information:

> "The patient remains hypotensive despite transfusion."

Leader:

> "Understood. Ongoing haemorrhagic shock. We are moving toward definitive haemorrhage control."

This is more powerful than simply receiving the number.

---

# 17.14 Shared Mental Model

A trauma team functions better when members have a common understanding of:

- the patient's condition
- the current priorities
- the working diagnosis
- the treatment plan
- the next expected step
- the destination

The leader should periodically verbalise this.

For example:

> "We have a severely injured patient with ongoing haemorrhagic shock. Airway and ventilation are currently controlled. Our immediate priority is haemorrhage control. Blood products are running. We are preparing for definitive intervention."

This creates a shared mental model.

---

# 17.15 When the Team Loses the Shared Mental Model

Warning signs include:

- multiple people pursuing different priorities
- repeated questions about the plan
- tasks being duplicated
- important tasks being forgotten
- investigations continuing despite deterioration
- disagreement about destination
- team members working without knowing the overall objective

The leader should stop and reset.

> "Let's pause for a moment. Current priority is haemorrhage control. Airway is controlled. We are not proceeding to CT at this stage. We are preparing for definitive intervention."

A short reset can restore team alignment.

---

# 17.16 Situational Awareness and the Primary Survey

The primary survey provides a structured framework for assessing immediate threats.

However, the leader must avoid interpreting it as a rigid sequence that prevents recognition of evolving problems.

The patient may deteriorate while the team is examining another body system.

For example:

During assessment of the abdomen:

- oxygen saturation falls
- blood pressure collapses
- airway obstruction develops

The leader must recognise the change immediately.

The correct question is not:

> "Where were we in the sequence?"

It is:

> **"What is the most important problem now?"**

---

# 17.17 The Leader Must Look Up

A common failure mode is becoming trapped in the details of a single task.

The leader may become focused on:

- airway equipment
- ultrasound images
- vascular access
- a chest drain
- a blood gas
- an imaging report
- a procedural difficulty

While this is occurring, the overall resuscitation may deteriorate.

The leader should periodically step back mentally.

Ask:

> "What is happening to the whole patient?"

> "What is the most immediate threat?"

> "What is the team currently doing?"

> "What have we not yet addressed?"

---

# 17.18 Cognitive Tunnelling

Cognitive tunnelling occurs when attention becomes excessively focused on one problem while other important information is missed.

Examples:

- focusing on the airway while haemorrhage worsens
- focusing on a suspected fracture while the patient becomes hypotensive
- focusing on obtaining CT while the patient is physiologically unstable
- repeatedly treating hypotension without addressing the source of bleeding

The antidote is deliberate reassessment.

> **Zoom in when necessary. Zoom out regularly.**

---

# 17.19 Diagnostic Fixation

Diagnostic fixation occurs when the team becomes committed to an early diagnosis and interprets subsequent information through that diagnosis.

For example:

> "This is just alcohol intoxication."

may become an unsafe assumption in a patient with trauma and reduced consciousness.

Similarly:

> "The hypotension is due to bleeding."

may become problematic if the patient subsequently develops another cause of cardiovascular collapse.

The leader should ask:

> **"What else could explain this?"**

and:

> **"What finding would make our current diagnosis wrong?"**

---

# 17.20 Confirmation Bias

Teams may preferentially notice information that supports their existing diagnosis.

The leader should deliberately seek disconfirming information.

For example:

> "If this is haemorrhage, what should we expect to see?"

> "What finding would make us reconsider?"

> "Is the patient's response consistent with our working diagnosis?"

This protects against premature closure.

---

# 17.21 Premature Closure

Premature closure occurs when the team stops searching for additional explanations after finding one plausible diagnosis.

Trauma patients may have multiple simultaneous life threats.

Finding one injury does not exclude another.

For example:

- a major chest injury does not exclude abdominal haemorrhage
- a head injury does not exclude hypovolaemia
- a pelvic fracture does not exclude thoracic injury
- an obvious limb injury does not explain unexplained shock

The leader must maintain diagnostic breadth while prioritising immediate threats.

---

# 17.22 The "What Are We Missing?" Question

At appropriate moments, ask:

> **"What are we missing?"**

This should not become a ritual performed without thought.

It should trigger deliberate reassessment.

Useful variations include:

- "What life threat have we not yet excluded?"
- "What explains the deterioration?"
- "What has not responded to treatment?"
- "Is there another source of bleeding?"
- "Is our current diagnosis still convincing?"
- "What would kill this patient next?"

---

# 17.23 Monitoring the Team

Situational awareness includes awareness of the team itself.

The leader should monitor:

- workload
- communication
- confusion
- fatigue
- missing equipment
- duplicated tasks
- unacknowledged instructions
- unresolved concerns

A technically capable team can still become unsafe under cognitive overload.

---

# 17.24 Cognitive Overload

Trauma resuscitations can generate a large amount of information simultaneously.

The leader may need to process:

- airway status
- ventilation
- circulation
- neurological status
- bleeding
- procedures
- blood products
- laboratory results
- imaging
- medication
- team coordination
- transfer planning

Trying to retain everything mentally is unreliable.

The solution is structured processing.

Use:

- explicit priorities
- clear task allocation
- verbal summaries
- written information when appropriate
- repeated reassessment
- closed-loop communication
- delegation

---

# 17.25 Externalising Information

Important information should not depend solely on memory.

Use the team's available systems:

- trauma documentation
- whiteboards
- observation charts
- blood product records
- task lists
- verbal summaries

The leader should ensure that critical information remains visible to the team.

---

# 17.26 Priorities Must Remain Visible

At any point, the team should know:

### Priority 1

What must happen immediately?

### Priority 2

What must happen next?

### Priority 3

What can wait?

This prevents low-priority tasks from competing with life-saving interventions.

---

# 17.27 Anticipating the Next Five Minutes

A practical leadership exercise is to ask:

> **"What will we need in the next five minutes?"**

Depending on the patient, this may include:

- blood products
- additional vascular access
- airway equipment
- chest drainage equipment
- senior surgical support
- anaesthetic support
- operating theatre
- interventional radiology
- warming equipment
- transport personnel
- monitoring equipment

Anticipating resource requirements prevents avoidable delays.

---

# 17.28 Anticipating the Next Decision

The leader should also anticipate decision points.

Examples:

> "If the patient remains unstable after initial haemorrhage control, we will proceed to definitive intervention."

> "If ventilation deteriorates, we will reassess the chest immediately."

> "If the airway cannot be maintained, we will proceed to definitive airway management."

This creates contingency planning.

---

# 17.29 The "If–Then" Model

A simple framework is:

> **If X happens, then we will do Y.**

Examples:

> If the blood pressure continues to fall, then we will escalate haemorrhage control.

> If the airway becomes compromised, then we will proceed with definitive airway management.

> If the patient deteriorates during transfer preparation, then we will stop and reassess.

This reduces hesitation during deterioration.

---

# 17.30 Recognising Deterioration Early

The leader should look for patterns rather than waiting for catastrophic collapse.

Potential warning signs include:

- worsening mental status
- increasing respiratory effort
- increasing oxygen requirement
- worsening ventilation
- falling blood pressure
- increasing heart rate
- worsening peripheral perfusion
- increasing blood loss
- poor response to treatment
- increasing need for intervention
- increasing team concern

A deterioration pattern should trigger reassessment.

---

# 17.31 The Patient's Trajectory

A useful mental classification is:

### Improving

The patient is responding appropriately to treatment.

### Stable

The patient is maintaining physiological parameters but remains at risk.

### Deteriorating

The patient is showing worsening physiology or failing to respond.

### Imminently unstable

The patient is likely to deteriorate rapidly without definitive intervention.

The leader should know which category best describes the patient.

---

# 17.32 Situational Awareness During Imaging

Imaging can create a dangerous shift in attention.

The team may become focused on obtaining or interpreting images while physiological instability progresses.

The leader should ask:

> "Is the patient stable enough for this investigation?"

> "Will the result change management immediately?"

> "What are the risks of delaying definitive treatment?"

Imaging should support management, not replace clinical judgement.

---

# 17.33 Situational Awareness During Procedures

Procedures create another risk.

The procedural clinician may become intensely focused on the technical task.

The leader must maintain the broader view.

During a chest drain, for example, the leader continues to monitor:

- oxygenation
- ventilation
- blood pressure
- bleeding
- overall response

The procedure is one part of the resuscitation.

It is not the resuscitation itself.

---

# 17.34 Situational Awareness During Transfer

Transfer creates particular risks because the team may mentally classify the resuscitation as "finished."

Before transfer, confirm:

- airway
- breathing
- circulation
- monitoring
- vascular access
- analgesia and sedation where relevant
- blood products where required
- drains and tubes
- equipment
- destination
- receiving team
- communication of major injuries
- outstanding investigations
- outstanding interventions

The patient's risk does not disappear because they leave the resuscitation room.

---

# 17.35 Situational Awareness and Handover

A good handover transfers the mental model.

It should communicate:

- mechanism
- major injuries
- physiological state
- interventions
- response
- ongoing concerns
- pending investigations
- definitive plan

For example:

> "This is a patient with major blunt trauma and ongoing haemorrhagic shock. Airway and ventilation are currently controlled. Blood products have been started, but the patient remains unstable. The major concern is ongoing internal haemorrhage. Definitive haemorrhage control is now the priority."

This is more useful than simply listing events chronologically.

---

# 17.36 When Situational Awareness Is Failing

Warning signs in the leader include:

- repeatedly asking for the same information
- forgetting previously assigned tasks
- losing track of priorities
- becoming fixated on one problem
- inability to summarise the patient
- becoming increasingly reactive
- allowing multiple people to speak simultaneously
- losing awareness of time
- losing awareness of destination
- becoming frustrated with the team
- making decisions without reassessment

These are signals to reset.

---

# 17.37 The Situational Awareness Reset

When the leader feels overwhelmed, pause briefly.

### Step 1 — Look at the patient

> "What is the patient's immediate physiological problem?"

### Step 2 — Look at the team

> "What is everyone doing?"

### Step 3 — Identify the priority

> "What is the most important intervention now?"

### Step 4 — Identify the next priority

> "What comes immediately after that?"

### Step 5 — Communicate

> "Team, let's reset. Our priority is..."

This takes seconds but can restore control.

---

# 17.38 The Leader's Thirty-Second Mental Reset

At appropriate transition points:

> **Patient:** What is happening?

> **Problem:** What is killing the patient?

> **Priority:** What must happen now?

> **People:** Who is doing what?

> **Plan:** What happens next?

> **Projection:** What could happen next?

This creates a compact mental reset.

---

# 17.39 Situational Awareness During Major Haemorrhage

Major haemorrhage is particularly demanding because deterioration can be rapid.

The leader must simultaneously monitor:

- physiological response
- blood loss
- blood product administration
- access
- warming
- coagulation concerns
- calcium and metabolic issues
- mechanical haemorrhage control
- definitive intervention
- communication with blood bank and receiving teams

The key question remains:

> **"Are we controlling the source of bleeding?"**

Blood replacement alone does not constitute definitive haemorrhage control.

---

# 17.40 Situational Awareness During Airway Crisis

During an airway crisis, the leader must avoid becoming trapped in the technical details.

The team should maintain awareness of:

- oxygenation
- ventilation
- airway patency
- aspiration risk
- haemodynamics
- available rescue strategies
- duration of attempts
- team fatigue
- need for escalation

The leader should recognise when repeated attempts are becoming unsafe.

> **A failed attempt is information. Repeated failure should change the plan.**

---

# 17.41 Situational Awareness During Cardiorespiratory Collapse

When the patient deteriorates suddenly, the leader must rapidly establish:

- what changed
- when it changed
- what intervention was occurring immediately beforehand
- airway status
- breathing status
- circulation
- bleeding
- reversible causes

The team should not continue with the previous plan automatically.

A sudden change requires a new assessment.

---

# 17.42 Information Hierarchy

Not all information deserves equal attention.

### Critical information

Information that changes immediate management.

Examples:

- airway failure
- severe hypoxia
- profound hypotension
- uncontrolled haemorrhage
- sudden neurological deterioration

### Important information

Information that affects the next stage of management.

Examples:

- imaging findings
- blood results
- response to treatment
- procedural findings

### Background information

Information that may be relevant but does not change immediate priorities.

The leader must protect attention for critical information.

---

# 17.43 Protecting the Team From Noise

Trauma rooms can become noisy.

Sources include:

- alarms
- multiple conversations
- equipment
- phone calls
- imaging communication
- blood bank communication
- family communication
- procedural discussions

The leader can reduce noise by establishing communication discipline.

For example:

> "One person speaking at a time."

> "Critical information to me directly."

> "Please confirm important instructions."

This improves signal-to-noise ratio.

---

# 17.44 The Leader Should Not Become the Information Bottleneck

Although critical information should reach the leader, not every decision requires central approval.

Over-centralisation can slow the team.

The leader should delegate decisions appropriately.

For example:

> "Airway lead, manage the airway and tell me immediately if the strategy is failing."

> "Nurse in charge, coordinate blood products and keep me updated on availability."

> "Surgical lead, assess definitive haemorrhage control options."

This preserves the leader's cognitive bandwidth.

---

# 17.45 Maintaining a Global View

The leader should periodically move between:

### Local view

What is happening with this specific problem?

and

### Global view

What is happening to the whole patient and team?

Both are necessary.

A purely global view may miss technical problems.

A purely local view may miss catastrophic deterioration elsewhere.

---

# 17.46 The Zoom-In / Zoom-Out Technique

### Zoom in

Focus on the immediate problem when:

- a procedure is required
- an airway is failing
- bleeding must be controlled
- a specific intervention is underway

### Zoom out

Return to the whole patient when:

- the intervention is complete
- new information arrives
- physiology changes
- the team becomes confused
- priorities change
- a major decision is required

This deliberate movement between levels is a core leadership skill.

---

# 17.47 The Importance of Time Awareness

Time matters in trauma.

The leader should know:

- when the patient arrived
- when major interventions started
- how long procedures have taken
- when blood products were started
- how long the patient has remained unstable
- when definitive treatment must occur

Time awareness helps prevent unnecessary delay.

---

# 17.48 Avoiding "One More Thing"

A dangerous pattern is repeated delay caused by:

> "Let's just do one more thing first."

Examples:

- one more imaging sequence
- one more procedure attempt
- one more laboratory result
- one more reassessment without changing management

The leader should ask:

> **"Will this actually change what we do next?"**

If not, it may be delaying definitive treatment.

---

# 17.49 Situational Awareness and Escalation

A leader must recognise when the current team or resources are insufficient.

Escalation may include:

- additional surgical support
- anaesthesia
- critical care
- interventional radiology
- specialist teams
- operating theatre
- blood bank support
- transfer to a higher-level trauma centre

Escalation should occur early enough to be useful.

> **Calling for help is not loss of control. Failing to call for help can be.**

---

# 17.50 The Leader Must Anticipate Resource Failure

Consider what happens if:

- blood is delayed
- vascular access fails
- the airway becomes difficult
- equipment is unavailable
- the patient cannot tolerate imaging
- the operating theatre is unavailable
- transfer is delayed

For high-risk patients, contingency planning should occur before the resource is needed.

---

# 17.51 Situational Awareness and Safety

The leader should continuously consider:

> **"Is the current plan still safe?"**

This includes:

- patient safety
- team safety
- procedural safety
- medication safety
- transfer safety
- equipment safety

A plan that was safe five minutes ago may become unsafe after deterioration.

---

# 17.52 Reassessment After Every Major Change

Major changes include:

- airway intervention
- chest intervention
- major haemorrhage treatment
- intubation
- blood product escalation
- operative decision
- imaging result
- sudden physiological deterioration
- arrival of additional specialists

After a major change:

> **Pause → reassess → update the plan.**

---

# 17.53 The Leader's Internal Questions

A useful internal checklist is:

1. What is happening?
2. What does it mean?
3. What is killing the patient?
4. What has been done?
5. Did it work?
6. What remains untreated?
7. What happens next?
8. What do I need from the team?
9. What resources will we need?
10. Where is this patient going?

These questions should become habitual.

---

# 17.54 The Leader's External Questions

Useful questions to the team include:

> "What are you seeing?"

> "What concerns you?"

> "Has the patient responded?"

> "What is the blood loss?"

> "What is the airway doing?"

> "What is the next step?"

> "What are we waiting for?"

> "What could deteriorate next?"

> "Do you need help?"

These questions maintain information flow.

---

# 17.55 Encourage Team Members to Speak Up

Situational awareness improves when team members feel able to communicate concerns.

The leader should explicitly create permission for escalation.

For example:

> "If you are concerned, say so."

> "If you think the plan is becoming unsafe, tell me."

> "If you see a change in the patient, call it out."

This creates psychological safety.

---

# 17.56 The Two-Challenge Principle

If a team member identifies a serious safety concern and the concern is not addressed, it should be raised again clearly.

For example:

> "Doctor, I am concerned that the patient is deteriorating."

If the issue remains unresolved:

> "I am still concerned. I think we need to stop and reassess the patient."

The purpose is not confrontation.

The purpose is to prevent important information from being lost.

---

# 17.57 Situational Awareness Is a Team Property

Although the leader has overall responsibility for coordination, situational awareness should not exist only in the leader's mind.

The team should understand:

- the current problem
- the immediate priority
- their role
- the next anticipated step

A strong leader therefore distributes situational awareness.

---

# 17.58 The Team Brief as a Situational Awareness Tool

At the beginning:

> "We have a severely injured patient. Current priorities are airway, haemorrhage control and rapid assessment. We will reassess continuously and prepare for definitive intervention."

During the resuscitation:

> "Current problem remains haemorrhagic shock. Blood products are running. We are preparing for definitive control."

Before transfer:

> "The patient remains high risk. Airway is controlled, blood products are ongoing, and definitive haemorrhage control is required at the receiving destination."

These short statements keep the team aligned.

---

# 17.59 Situational Awareness During Transitions

Transitions are high-risk periods.

Examples:

- arrival from prehospital care
- moving from resuscitation to imaging
- moving from imaging to theatre
- changing teams
- changing leaders
- transfer to ICU
- transfer to another hospital

At each transition, the mental model must be transferred.

---

# 17.60 Leadership Handover

If leadership changes, the outgoing leader should provide:

### Situation

What is happening?

### Background

What happened and what injuries are suspected?

### Assessment

What is the current physiological state?

### Actions

What has been done?

### Response

What worked and what did not?

### Risks

What is likely to deteriorate?

### Plan

What must happen next?

This prevents loss of situational awareness during leadership transition.

---

# 17.61 When the Leader Becomes Part of the Problem

Leadership can itself create situational-awareness failure.

Examples:

- speaking continuously
- micromanaging every task
- performing procedures unnecessarily
- refusing to delegate
- ignoring team input
- changing plans without explanation
- becoming emotionally reactive
- focusing on proving the diagnosis rather than treating the patient

The leader should remain sufficiently detached to coordinate the entire resuscitation.

---

# 17.62 Emotional Regulation

Stress changes cognition.

Under pressure, the leader may:

- narrow attention
- become impatient
- speak faster
- stop listening
- make premature decisions
- become fixated
- lose awareness of the team

A deliberate reduction in speech speed and a brief pause can improve cognitive control.

> **Slow is smooth. Smooth is fast.**

The goal is not to slow the resuscitation.

The goal is to prevent chaotic decision-making.

---

# 17.63 The Power of a Pause

A pause does not mean inactivity.

A few seconds may be used to:

- look at the monitor
- look at the patient
- listen to the team
- identify the priority
- decide the next action

A controlled pause may prevent a major error.

---

# 17.64 Situational Awareness and Decision-Making

Good decisions depend on an accurate mental model.

If the mental model is wrong, even technically excellent decision-making can produce the wrong result.

Therefore:

> **Before asking "What should we do?", first ask "What is actually happening?"**

This distinction is fundamental.

---

# 17.65 Situational Awareness Failure Cascade

A common cascade is:

1. A small change occurs.
2. The change is not recognised.
3. The clinical situation worsens.
4. The team continues the previous plan.
5. The leader becomes overloaded.
6. Communication deteriorates.
7. Tasks become duplicated or missed.
8. Definitive treatment is delayed.

The goal is to interrupt this cascade early.

---

# 17.66 Early Warning Signs of Cognitive Drift

Cognitive drift may occur when the team gradually becomes less alert to changing risk.

Examples:

- "The patient has been stable for a while."
- "The numbers are probably fine."
- "We'll get the scan first."
- "Let's finish this procedure."
- "Someone else is dealing with it."

These statements may signal that the team is assuming stability rather than actively reassessing it.

---

# 17.67 Avoiding Normalisation of Deviance

Repeated exposure to abnormal situations can make them feel normal.

For example:

> "The blood pressure is always low in these patients."

or:

> "The oxygen saturation usually sits around this level."

The leader should ask whether the current state is acceptable rather than simply familiar.

> **Familiar does not mean safe.**

---

# 17.68 Situational Awareness and the "Big Picture"

The big picture can be summarised as:

> **Mechanism → Injury pattern → Physiology → Response → Threat → Intervention → Definitive care**

The leader should understand how each part connects.

---

# 17.69 A Practical Situational Awareness Framework

At any point, the leader can use:

## NOW

What is happening now?

## WHY

Why is it happening?

## NEXT

What is likely to happen next?

## ACTION

What are we doing about it?

## DESTINATION

Where does the patient need to go?

This is deliberately simple.

Simple frameworks are more useful under pressure than complicated ones.

---

# 17.70 Situational Awareness During a Stable Phase

Even when the patient appears stable, the leader should ask:

- What injuries remain unrecognised?
- What could deteriorate?
- What intervention is pending?
- Is definitive care delayed?
- Is the patient safe for transfer or imaging?
- What resources will be needed?

Stability should create an opportunity for planning.

It should not create complacency.

---

# 17.71 Situational Awareness During a Deteriorating Phase

When deterioration occurs:

1. Recognise the change.
2. State it aloud.
3. Reassess immediate life threats.
4. Identify the likely cause.
5. Treat immediately reversible threats.
6. Reassess response.
7. Escalate if required.
8. Update the destination and definitive plan.

For example:

> "The patient is deteriorating. Blood pressure is falling despite ongoing transfusion. We are concerned about uncontrolled haemorrhage. Continue blood products, reassess for immediate reversible causes, and prepare for definitive haemorrhage control."

---

# 17.72 The Leader Should State the Trajectory

Useful phrases include:

> "The patient is improving."

> "The patient remains unstable."

> "The patient is deteriorating."

> "We have not achieved adequate response."

> "We are approaching definitive intervention."

This makes the team's understanding explicit.

---

# 17.73 Avoiding Ambiguous Language

Ambiguous statements reduce situational awareness.

Avoid:

> "Someone should get blood."

Instead:

> "Sarah, please activate the massive transfusion protocol now."

Avoid:

> "We may need theatre."

Instead:

> "Please alert theatre that this patient may require urgent operative haemorrhage control."

Specific language creates shared understanding.

---

# 17.74 Situational Awareness and Communication Load

Communication should increase when risk increases.

During routine periods:

- concise updates may be sufficient

During deterioration:

- explicit priorities
- named instructions
- closed-loop communication
- repeated summaries

The leader should increase communication structure as complexity rises.

---

# 17.75 A Simple Rule

> **The more unstable the patient, the more structured the communication should become.**

---

# 17.76 Maintaining Awareness of the Destination

The leader should think beyond the resuscitation room.

Ask:

> "What definitive treatment does this patient need?"

Possible destinations include:

- operating theatre
- interventional radiology
- intensive care
- trauma ward
- another specialist service
- higher-level trauma centre

The destination should influence current actions.

---

# 17.77 Destination-Driven Resuscitation

Once the likely definitive pathway is clear, the team should prepare for it.

For example:

If the patient requires theatre:

- alert theatre
- involve the surgical team
- arrange blood products
- ensure necessary monitoring
- minimise unnecessary delay

If transfer is required:

- identify receiving centre
- communicate early
- arrange transport
- stabilise essential problems
- prepare equipment and documentation

The leader should avoid treating the resuscitation room as the final destination.

---

# 17.78 Situational Awareness and Definitive Haemorrhage Control

A recurring leadership error is allowing resuscitation activity to continue indefinitely without progressing toward definitive haemorrhage control.

The leader should repeatedly ask:

> **"What are we doing to stop the bleeding?"**

This may involve:

- direct pressure
- tourniquet
- pelvic stabilisation where appropriate
- operative management
- interventional radiology
- other definitive interventions depending on the injury

Resuscitation and haemorrhage control should progress together.

---

# 17.79 Situational Awareness and the Time–Risk Relationship

As time passes, the cost of delay may increase.

The leader should therefore continuously consider:

- Is the patient tolerating the delay?
- Is the intervention still appropriate?
- Is definitive treatment available?
- Are we delaying because of useful information or because of process?

This prevents process from becoming more important than the patient.

---

# 17.80 The Leader's Situational Awareness Checklist

### Patient

- What is the current physiological state?
- What are the major injuries?
- Is the patient improving or deteriorating?

### Threats

- What is currently killing the patient?
- What could kill the patient next?

### Treatment

- What has been done?
- Did it work?
- What remains outstanding?

### Team

- Who is doing what?
- Is anyone overloaded?
- Is communication working?

### Resources

- What do we have?
- What do we need?
- What might we need next?

### Destination

- Where is the patient going?
- What definitive treatment is required?

### Projection

- What is likely to happen in the next five minutes?
