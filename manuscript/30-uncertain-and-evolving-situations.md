# Chapter 30 — Leading the Trauma Team in Uncertain and Evolving Situations

## Introduction

Trauma leadership is rarely a matter of managing a completely known problem.

The trauma team leader is often required to make decisions while the diagnosis is incomplete, the patient's physiology is changing, investigations are still pending, and the significance of individual findings is uncertain.

A patient may initially appear stable and then deteriorate.

A mechanism may suggest one pattern of injury while the clinical examination suggests another.

An apparently reassuring investigation may be followed by a new physiological abnormality.

A treatment may improve one problem while creating another.

In these situations, the quality of trauma leadership depends less on knowing every answer immediately and more on maintaining a reliable process for identifying what is known, what is unknown, what is changing, and what must happen next.

The leader must create enough structure to prevent chaos without creating false certainty.

The central principle of this chapter is:

> **When the situation is uncertain, the leader's job is not to eliminate uncertainty. The leader's job is to manage uncertainty safely.**

---

# 30.1 What Makes Trauma Situations Uncertain?

Uncertainty may arise from several sources.

## 1.1 Incomplete information

The team may not yet know:

- the exact mechanism;
- the timing of injury;
- the patient's baseline condition;
- the medications being taken;
- the extent of blood loss;
- the presence of occult injury;
- the response to treatment;
- the significance of an abnormal investigation.

The leader should explicitly recognise missing information rather than unconsciously treating missing information as negative information.

There is an important difference between:

> "There is no evidence of injury."

and:

> "We have not yet demonstrated injury."

The second statement preserves uncertainty appropriately.

---

# 30.2 Dynamic Rather Than Static Assessment

Trauma patients must be understood as dynamic systems.

A patient's condition at 14:00 is not proof of their condition at 14:15.

The leader should continually ask:

> "What has changed?"

and:

> "What is likely to change next?"

This is especially important when:

- bleeding may be ongoing;
- airway swelling may progress;
- respiratory failure may develop;
- intracranial pathology may evolve;
- analgesia or sedation may alter physiology;
- temperature is falling;
- acidosis is worsening;
- treatment has altered the clinical picture.

The trauma team should therefore avoid thinking of assessment as a single event.

Assessment is a continuing process.

---

# 30.3 The Difference Between Uncertainty and Confusion

Uncertainty is unavoidable.

Confusion is not.

A team may not know exactly what is happening while still having a clear plan.

For example:

> "We do not yet know the source of the hypotension. We are treating life-threatening haemorrhage while we assess chest, abdomen, pelvis and external bleeding."

This is uncertainty managed through structured action.

By contrast:

> "We are not sure what's going on. Let's see what happens."

is uncertainty without leadership.

The leader should therefore distinguish between:

1. what is known;
2. what is suspected;
3. what is unknown;
4. what is dangerous if missed;
5. what action is required now.

---

# 30.4 Establish the Current State

When the situation becomes unclear, the leader should briefly reconstruct the patient's current state.

A useful framework is:

### A — Airway

- Is the airway currently patent?
- Is there evidence of obstruction?
- Has the patient's level of consciousness changed?
- Is airway protection deteriorating?
- Has facial, neck or airway swelling progressed?

### B — Breathing

- What is the respiratory rate?
- What is the oxygen saturation?
- Is the patient tiring?
- Is there asymmetry?
- Is there new chest pathology?
- Has ventilation changed?

### C — Circulation

- What is the blood pressure?
- What is the heart rate?
- Is the pulse character changing?
- Is peripheral perfusion deteriorating?
- Is there evidence of ongoing haemorrhage?
- Is the patient responding appropriately to resuscitation?

### D — Disability

- Has consciousness changed?
- Is there a new neurological deficit?
- Has the pupil examination changed?
- Is agitation new?
- Could medication, hypoxia, hypotension or intracranial injury explain the change?

### E — Exposure and environment

- Is there another injury that has not been identified?
- Is the patient becoming hypothermic?
- Has positioning changed?
- Has a device, line, drain or dressing failed?

This reassessment should be concise and clinically meaningful.

---

# 30.5 The Leader Must Detect Trajectory

A single observation may be less important than the direction of change.

For example:

- blood pressure 100/60 → 90/55 → 80/45;
- heart rate 105 → 118 → 130;
- oxygen requirement increasing;
- respiratory rate progressively increasing;
- neurological status deteriorating;
- increasing transfusion requirement;
- progressively worsening perfusion.

The important observation is not simply the current value.

It is the trajectory.

The leader should ask:

> "Where is this patient going?"

rather than only:

> "Where is this patient now?"

---

# 30.6 Use Trends, Not Isolated Numbers

Trauma leadership requires interpretation of trends.

A single normal blood pressure does not exclude significant injury.

A single normal haemoglobin does not exclude acute haemorrhage.

A single reassuring neurological examination does not guarantee neurological stability.

A single normal oxygen saturation does not exclude evolving respiratory failure.

The leader should therefore integrate:

- clinical examination;
- physiological observations;
- laboratory trends;
- imaging;
- treatment response;
- mechanism;
- time.

The patient's physiology should always be interpreted in context.

---

# 30.7 Recognise the "Looks Stable" Trap

One of the most dangerous situations in trauma is the patient who appears stable while an important process is developing.

The leader should be cautious when there is:

- a high-risk mechanism;
- persistent unexplained tachycardia;
- increasing analgesia requirement;
- recurrent hypotension;
- increasing oxygen requirement;
- unexplained metabolic abnormalities;
- worsening pain;
- decreasing urine output;
- altered mental status;
- repeated episodes of transient instability.

A patient who repeatedly requires intervention to remain apparently stable may not truly be stable.

The interventions may be temporarily compensating for deterioration.

---

# 30.8 Reassessment After Every Major Intervention

Every major intervention should generate a reassessment.

Examples include:

- airway intervention;
- intubation;
- chest decompression;
- chest drain insertion;
- haemorrhage control;
- pelvic stabilisation;
- blood product administration;
- major fluid administration;
- sedation;
- analgesia;
- transfer;
- positioning.

The leader should ask:

> "Did that intervention produce the expected effect?"

If yes:

> "How durable is the improvement?"

If no:

> "What alternative explanation must we consider?"

---

# 30.9 Failure to Improve Is a Diagnostic Signal

Failure to respond to treatment is itself information.

If hypotension persists despite appropriate initial management, the leader should reconsider:

- ongoing haemorrhage;
- missed bleeding source;
- chest pathology;
- cardiac injury;
- obstructive physiology;
- inadequate haemorrhage control;
- incorrect assumptions;
- treatment failure.

The response should not simply be:

> "Give more."

It should be:

> "Why has the patient not responded as expected?"

---

# 30.10 Avoid Anchoring

Anchoring occurs when the team becomes attached to an initial diagnosis or explanation.

Examples include:

- assuming hypotension is caused by medication;
- assuming altered consciousness is due to alcohol;
- assuming tachycardia is pain;
- assuming hypoxia is pulmonary contusion;
- assuming abdominal pain is minor because the initial examination was reassuring.

The leader should actively challenge the working diagnosis.

Useful questions include:

> "What else could explain this?"

> "What would we expect if our current diagnosis were correct?"

> "What finding would make our current diagnosis less likely?"

> "What dangerous diagnosis have we not excluded?"

---

# 30.11 Use a Working Diagnosis Without Treating It as Final

A working diagnosis is necessary.

But it must remain revisable.

A useful formulation is:

> "Our current working diagnosis is X. We are treating X because it is dangerous and plausible. We will reassess if the patient does not respond as expected."

This allows the team to act decisively without becoming cognitively trapped.

---

# 30.12 Maintain Diagnostic Flexibility

The leader should be willing to change direction when new evidence appears.

Changing the plan is not failure.

It is appropriate clinical reasoning.

The leader should model this explicitly:

> "Our initial assumption was abdominal haemorrhage. The examination and response to treatment are now less consistent with that. We need to reassess the chest and pelvis."

This communicates that revision is expected.

---

# 30.13 The Importance of "What Doesn't Fit?"

One of the most useful questions in complex trauma is:

> **"What doesn't fit?"**

If the patient has:

- severe shock but little obvious external bleeding;
- profound hypoxia without obvious chest pathology;
- neurological deterioration without an obvious cause;
- persistent tachycardia despite adequate analgesia;
- recurrent hypotension after apparently successful haemorrhage control;

then the discrepancy should trigger reassessment.

Unexpected findings deserve attention.

---

# 30.14 Identify the Most Dangerous Plausible Explanation

When multiple explanations are possible, prioritise the one that is:

- immediately life-threatening;
- plausible;
- treatable;
- time-sensitive.

The leader should not attempt to solve every uncertainty simultaneously.

Instead:

> **Identify the uncertainty that could cause the greatest harm if ignored.**

Then address it.

---

# 30.15 Use the "Worst Reasonable Case" Principle

The leader should occasionally ask:

> "If this is the worst reasonable diagnosis, what would we need to do now?"

This is not an invitation to catastrophise.

It is a safety strategy.

For example:

If the patient could have ongoing internal haemorrhage, the team should ensure haemorrhage control and blood product support are not delayed while waiting for complete diagnostic certainty.

If the airway could deteriorate, the team should prepare for definitive airway management before complete obstruction occurs.

If intracranial deterioration is possible, neurological reassessment and appropriate escalation should not wait until collapse.

---

# 30.16 Do Not Confuse Possibility With Probability

Not every theoretical diagnosis deserves equal attention.

The leader should distinguish:

### Possible

Could this occur?

### Plausible

Does the mechanism and clinical picture support it?

### Probable

Is it currently the best explanation?

### Immediately dangerous

Even if less likely, would missing it cause catastrophic harm?

This distinction helps the team remain focused.

---

# 30.17 Parallel Thinking

Complex trauma often requires several processes to occur simultaneously.

For example:

- one clinician controls the airway;
- another evaluates circulation;
- another obtains blood;
- another prepares imaging;
- another obtains collateral history;
- the leader integrates the information.

This is parallel processing.

The leader should avoid personally performing every task.

The leader's role is to coordinate the system.

---

# 30.18 Keep the Team Updated

When the situation changes, the leader should provide a short update.

A useful structure is:

> **Situation → Interpretation → Plan**

For example:

> "The patient remains hypotensive despite blood products. I am concerned about ongoing internal haemorrhage. We are moving toward definitive haemorrhage control and will continue reassessment."

This creates a shared mental model.

---

# 30.19 Shared Mental Models During Uncertainty

The team should understand:

- what the current problem is;
- what is suspected;
- what has already been done;
- what remains outstanding;
- what the next decision point is.

Without this, different team members may operate according to different assumptions.

One clinician may think:

> "The patient is going to theatre."

Another may think:

> "We are still deciding whether to scan."

Another may think:

> "The patient is stable."

The leader must eliminate these discrepancies.

---

# 30.20 Make the Next Decision Point Explicit

A useful leadership technique is to define the next decision point.

For example:

> "After the next blood pressure and examination, we will decide whether the patient requires immediate operative control."

Or:

> "If the oxygen requirement continues to rise, we will proceed to definitive airway management."

This converts uncertainty into a monitored process.

---

# 30.21 Contingency Planning

Every major plan should include:

> "What if this does not work?"

Examples:

### Airway

> "If intubation fails, we move to the agreed rescue strategy."

### Haemorrhage

> "If the patient remains unstable despite resuscitation, we escalate haemorrhage control."

### Breathing

> "If oxygenation deteriorates, we will reassess for an evolving chest problem and escalate respiratory support."

### Transfer

> "If the patient's condition deteriorates during preparation, we will stop the transfer process and stabilise the immediate threat."

Contingency planning prevents the team from improvising during crisis.

---

# 30.22 Anticipate Deterioration Before It Happens

A strong leader asks:

> "What is the next likely deterioration?"

Potential examples include:

- worsening airway oedema;
- recurrent bleeding;
- respiratory fatigue;
- increasing intracranial pressure;
- hypothermia;
- coagulopathy;
- loss of vascular access;
- equipment failure;
- deterioration during transport.

The purpose is not prediction for its own sake.

The purpose is preparation.

---

# 30.23 Prepare the Team for the Next Problem

A useful statement is:

> "The patient is currently stable, but I am concerned about X. Please prepare Y in case the patient deteriorates."

This allows preparation without creating unnecessary alarm.

For example:

> "The airway is currently patent, but facial swelling is increasing. Prepare the definitive airway equipment and call for senior airway support."

---

# 30.24 Avoid Premature Closure

Premature closure occurs when the team stops searching because an explanation appears satisfactory.

The leader should periodically ask:

> "Have we identified the cause, or have we simply found one abnormality?"

This is particularly important when multiple injuries are possible.

Trauma patients can have more than one explanation for deterioration.

---

# 30.25 Multiple Simultaneous Problems

A patient may have:

- haemorrhage;
- chest injury;
- traumatic brain injury;
- hypothermia;
- metabolic disturbance;

at the same time.

The leader should therefore avoid forcing every abnormality into a single diagnosis.

The correct model may be:

> "There are several concurrent problems, and we need to determine which is currently causing the greatest physiological threat."

---

# 30.26 When New Information Arrives

New information should be actively integrated.

Examples:

- a relative provides a medication history;
- paramedics report prolonged hypotension;
- imaging reveals an unexpected injury;
- laboratory results show worsening coagulopathy;
- a nurse notices increasing drain output;
- the patient's neurological status changes.

The leader should not merely acknowledge the information.

They should ask:

> "Does this change our working diagnosis or management?"

---

# 30.27 Information Has Different Levels of Reliability

Not all information carries equal certainty.

Consider:

- direct clinical observation;
- measured physiological data;
- imaging;
- laboratory results;
- patient history;
- collateral history;
- assumptions;
- incomplete reports.

The leader should distinguish fact from interpretation.

For example:

**Fact:**

> "The blood pressure is 78 systolic."

**Interpretation:**

> "The patient is probably bleeding."

**Decision:**

> "We need immediate haemorrhage-focused management."

Keeping these levels separate improves reasoning.

---

# 30.28 Ask for Evidence

When a team member states a conclusion, the leader can ask:

> "What makes you think that?"

This should not be confrontational.

It encourages explicit reasoning.

Similarly:

> "What are you seeing?"

may be more useful than:

> "Are you sure?"

The objective is to uncover the evidence behind the conclusion.

---

# 30.29 Encourage Speaking Up

Uncertainty increases the risk of hierarchy-related communication failure.

Junior clinicians and nurses may notice deterioration before the leader does.

The leader should explicitly invite concerns:

> "If anyone sees a change that concerns them, speak up immediately."

When a team member raises a concern, the leader should acknowledge it.

> "Thank you. Let's reassess that now."

This creates psychological safety.

---

# 30.30 Listen for Weak Signals

Deterioration is not always announced by a dramatic event.

Weak signals may include:

- "The patient doesn't look right."
- increasing restlessness;
- subtle change in respiratory pattern;
- increasing oxygen requirement;
- repeated small episodes of hypotension;
- difficulty obtaining reliable observations;
- increasing nursing concern;
- unexpected treatment requirements.

The leader should take these signals seriously.

---

# 30.31 The "Something Is Wrong" Signal

Experienced clinicians may recognise deterioration before they can immediately explain it.

This should not replace objective assessment.

But it should trigger reassessment.

A useful response is:

> "Something has changed. Let's stop and reassess the patient from the beginning."

This is often safer than continuing with the existing plan.

---

# 30.32 Stop and Reframe

When the team becomes confused, the leader may need to pause.

A brief reset can be:

> "Everyone pause for a moment."

Then:

> "This is where we are."

> "These are the immediate threats."

> "This is what we know."

> "This is what we do not know."

> "This is what we are doing next."

This can restore structure within seconds.

---

# 30.33 Re-run the Primary Survey

When the clinical picture becomes unclear, returning to cABCDE is often appropriate.

Do not assume that because the primary survey was completed earlier, it remains valid.

A new deterioration means the patient's priorities may have changed.

The leader should be willing to say:

> "Let's repeat the primary survey."

This is not going backwards.

It is returning to fundamentals.

---

# 30.34 Reassess the Basics Before Escalating Complexity

When a patient deteriorates, check:

- airway;
- breathing;
- circulation;
- neurological status;
- exposure;
- equipment;
- lines;
- drains;
- monitoring.

Simple causes should not be missed because the team is searching for sophisticated explanations.

---

# 30.35 Equipment and Human Failure

Deterioration may result from:

- disconnected oxygen;
- blocked tubing;
- displaced airway device;
- malfunctioning monitor;
- blocked intravenous access;
- incorrectly positioned drain;
- empty infusion;
- failed pump;
- inadequate oxygen supply.

The leader should maintain situational awareness of the environment as well as the patient.

---

# 30.36 The Patient Is the Ultimate Monitor

Numbers are useful.

But the patient remains the primary source of information.

Observe:

- colour;
- work of breathing;
- speech;
- consciousness;
- movement;
- perfusion;
- behaviour;
- response to intervention.

A monitor can display a reassuring number while the patient is visibly deteriorating.

The leader must reconcile the two.

---

# 30.37 Avoid Chasing Numbers

A single abnormal value should not automatically dictate management without context.

Likewise, a normal value should not override a concerning clinical picture.

The question is:

> "What does this measurement mean in this patient at this time?"

---

# 30.38 Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

The leader should balance:

- urgency;
- probability;
- consequence;
- reversibility;
- available resources;
- risk of delay.

A highly dangerous condition with a reasonable probability may justify action before complete confirmation.

A low-probability condition with minimal immediate consequence may not.

---

# 30.39 Time Has a Cost

Waiting for certainty can be dangerous.

The leader should ask:

> "What is the cost of waiting?"

If the cost is high, the threshold for action should be lower.

Examples include:

- threatened airway;
- uncontrolled haemorrhage;
- tension physiology;
- rapidly deteriorating neurological status.

---

# 30.40 But Action Also Has a Cost

Not every uncertain situation requires immediate invasive intervention.

The leader must consider:

- procedural risk;
- resource use;
- complications;
- delay caused by intervention;
- effect on subsequent management.

The objective is not maximal intervention.

It is appropriate intervention at the appropriate time.

---

# 30.41 Reversibility Matters

When uncertain, reversible actions may be preferable while information is gathered.

Examples include:

- positioning;
- oxygenation;
- haemorrhage control;
- warming;
- monitoring;
- vascular access;
- preparation for escalation.

The leader should avoid unnecessary irreversible decisions when the clinical situation permits further clarification.

---

# 30.42 Escalate Early When the Situation Exceeds the Team

Uncertainty should lower the threshold for seeking appropriate help when:

- the patient is deteriorating;
- the diagnosis is unclear and potentially life-threatening;
- advanced airway management may be required;
- major haemorrhage is suspected;
- specialist intervention may be needed;
- resources are insufficient;
- the team is becoming overwhelmed.

Escalation is not an admission of failure.

It is a safety intervention.

---

# 30.43 Communicate the Reason for Escalation

A vague request:

> "We need help."

is less effective than:

> "This patient has persistent haemodynamic instability despite resuscitation and we are concerned about uncontrolled haemorrhage. We need urgent senior surgical and anaesthetic support."

The second statement communicates:

- the problem;
- the severity;
- what has been attempted;
- the concern;
- the required response.

---

# 30.44 Maintain Leadership During Diagnostic Uncertainty

The leader should not become passive because the diagnosis is unclear.

Leadership remains active:

- establish priorities;
- assign tasks;
- monitor response;
- update the team;
- anticipate deterioration;
- revise the plan;
- escalate appropriately.

Uncertainty does not remove the need for leadership.

It increases it.

---

# 30.45 The Danger of "Waiting for the Scan"

Imaging can be extremely valuable.

But imaging should not become a substitute for clinical judgement.

The leader should ask:

> "Is the patient stable enough for this investigation?"

and:

> "Will the result change what we need to do immediately?"

If the patient requires immediate life-saving treatment, diagnostic confirmation should not create avoidable delay.

---

# 30.46 The Danger of "Waiting for the Blood Result"

Similarly, laboratory confirmation should not delay treatment when the clinical situation already supports urgent action.

The leader should distinguish between:

> information needed to confirm a diagnosis

and:

> information needed before a treatment can safely proceed.

They are not always the same.

---

# 30.47 Reassessment After Transfer

A change in location is also a change in risk.

The patient may deteriorate:

- during movement;
- during imaging;
- during transfer to theatre;
- during inter-hospital transfer;
- during handover.

The receiving environment should be prepared before movement.

The leader should communicate:

- current condition;
- active threats;
- treatments given;
- response;
- anticipated deterioration;
- contingency plan.

---

# 30.48 Avoid Losing the Clinical Narrative

During prolonged trauma care, the team can become focused on individual tasks.

The leader must maintain the overall story:

> "What happened?"

> "What injuries do we believe are present?"

> "What has changed?"

> "What has been treated?"

> "What remains dangerous?"

> "Where is the patient going next?"

This prevents fragmented care.

---

# 30.49 A Structured Situation Update

A useful update can be:

### Situation

What is happening now?

### Background

What happened and what has been identified?

### Assessment

What is the current physiological and clinical state?

### Recommendation

What needs to happen next?

The precise communication format can vary, but the principles remain constant.

---

# 30.50 Managing Competing Priorities

Uncertainty often produces competing priorities.

For example:

- the patient needs imaging;
- blood is being prepared;
- airway risk is increasing;
- surgery is being contacted;
- the family is requesting information.

The leader must establish sequence.

Ask:

> "What must happen now?"

> "What can happen in parallel?"

> "What can safely wait?"

This prevents important tasks from competing unnecessarily.

---

# 30.51 Protect the Critical Path

The critical path is the sequence of actions required to prevent deterioration and achieve definitive treatment.

The leader should identify:

1. immediate life threat;
2. stabilising intervention;
3. definitive diagnostic or therapeutic step;
4. destination;
5. contingency.

Tasks that do not contribute to this path should not unnecessarily delay it.

---

# 30.52 Avoid Task Saturation

As uncertainty increases, teams often generate more tasks.

This can create overload.

The leader should prioritise.

A long list is less useful than:

> "These are the three things that must happen now."

---

# 30.53 Delegate Clearly

Each task should have:

- a named person;
- a clear objective;
- an expected timeframe.

For example:

> "Ahmed, obtain two large-bore intravenous accesses now."

> "Sara, repeat the observations and tell me the trend in five minutes."

> "Khalid, contact the operating team and report the patient's current physiology."

The leader should close the loop by confirming completion.

---

# 30.54 Monitor Task Completion

During uncertainty, tasks may fail silently.

The leader should ask:

> "Has that been done?"

> "What was the result?"

> "What happened after the intervention?"

This is particularly important when several parallel processes are occurring.

---

# 30.55 Do Not Let the Team Drift

Team drift occurs when the original plan continues despite changing circumstances.

The leader should periodically reset:

> "Our situation has changed."

> "The priority is now different."

This is especially important after:

- new imaging;
- new physiology;
- treatment failure;
- arrival of additional expertise;
- change in destination.

---

# 30.56 Use Explicit Reassessment Triggers

Examples:

> "Reassess after the next unit of blood."

> "Repeat the neurological examination after transfer."

> "If the systolic pressure falls again, activate the next escalation."

> "If oxygen requirements increase, reassess the chest and airway immediately."

Explicit triggers make deterioration actionable.

---

# 30.57 Recognise Cognitive Overload

The leader should monitor not only the patient but also the team.

Warning signs include:

- repeated instructions;
- missed tasks;
- confusion;
- poor communication;
- silence;
- contradictory plans;
- fixation on one problem;
- failure to recognise new information.

When this occurs, simplify.

---

# 30.58 Simplification as a Leadership Skill

The leader can say:

> "Let's simplify this."

Then identify:

1. immediate threat;
2. immediate action;
3. next decision.

This reduces cognitive load.

---

# 30.59 The Leader Must Remain Calm Without Becoming Passive

Calm leadership is not the absence of urgency.

The leader can be calm while communicating clearly:

> "This is deteriorating. We need to act now."

The team should feel that the situation is serious but organised.

---

# 30.60 Avoid False Reassurance

Statements such as:

> "The patient looks okay."

should be replaced with specific observations.

For example:

> "The blood pressure is currently 105 systolic, oxygen requirement is unchanged, and there is no current evidence of deterioration."

Specific language is safer than vague reassurance.

---

# 30.61 Avoid Unnecessary Alarm

The opposite problem is excessive escalation of every possible threat.

The leader should communicate proportionately.

Instead of:

> "This patient could arrest."

say:

> "There is a risk of deterioration because of X. We are preparing Y while continuing close monitoring."

This creates preparedness without chaos.

---

# 30.62 When the Diagnosis Changes

If new evidence changes the diagnosis, communicate the change explicitly.

> "We are changing our working diagnosis."

> "The new information suggests X is more likely."

> "This changes our management because..."

This prevents old assumptions from persisting within the team.

---

# 30.63 When the Plan Changes

Similarly:

> "The plan has changed."

Then explain:

- why;
- what happens now;
- who is responsible.

This is particularly important when multiple teams are involved.

---

# 30.64 Leadership When Information Conflicts

Sometimes information appears contradictory.

For example:

- clinical examination suggests one diagnosis;
- imaging suggests another;
- physiology suggests a third.

The leader should avoid immediately choosing one source.

Instead ask:

> "How can these findings all be explained?"

The answer may be multiple simultaneous injuries or a changing physiological state.

---

# 30.65 Seek the Most Coherent Model

The best working model is the one that explains the greatest number of important findings with the fewest unsupported assumptions.

But it must remain provisional.

If new information no longer fits the model, revise it.

---

# 30.66 The Value of a Second Look

A second examination can be extremely valuable.

In a dynamic trauma patient:

> "Look again."

may reveal:

- new bruising;
- expanding swelling;
- increasing abdominal distension;
- new neurological deficit;
- worsening respiratory effort;
- occult bleeding;
- changes in perfusion.

The first examination provides a baseline.

The second examination provides a trend.

---

# 30.67 Reassess After Time Has Passed

Time itself changes the clinical picture.

A patient who was examined immediately after injury may develop clearer signs later.

Therefore:

> **A normal early examination does not necessarily remain normal.**

The leader should determine when reassessment is required based on injury pattern and clinical trajectory.

---

# 30.68 The Importance of Collateral Information

Information from:

- paramedics;
- relatives;
- witnesses;
- referring clinicians;
- nursing staff;
- previous records;

may change the interpretation of the patient.

The leader should actively seek missing context when it can alter management.

---

# 30.69 Medication and Baseline Factors

Uncertainty may be increased by:

- anticoagulants;
- antiplatelet therapy;
- sedatives;
- alcohol or recreational drugs;
- beta-blockers;
- significant comorbidity;
- pregnancy;
- frailty;
- pre-existing neurological impairment.

These factors may alter both presentation and response to injury.

The leader should identify them early when relevant.

---

# 30.70 When the Patient Cannot Communicate

In an unconscious, intubated or severely injured patient, the leader should recognise that absence of history is not absence of risk.

Use:

- mechanism;
- collateral history;
- previous records;
- medications;
- examination;
- physiological trends.

The team must tolerate uncertainty while continuing to act on the available evidence.

---

# 30.71 Managing Uncertainty During Procedures

Uncertainty does not stop when a procedure begins.

During procedures, the leader should continue to monitor:

- physiological response;
- technical success;
- complications;
- unexpected findings;
- need for escalation.

A procedure should never become an end in itself.

The objective remains patient stabilisation and definitive management.

---

# 30.72 Know When to Stop

If an intervention is not working or is causing harm, the leader must be willing to stop and reconsider.

Useful questions include:

> "Is this helping?"

> "What complication might we have created?"

> "What is our next option?"

Continuing simply because an intervention has already started is another form of anchoring.

---

# 30.73 The "Three Questions" Reset

When the team becomes uncertain, ask:

### 1. What is killing the patient now?

Identify the immediate physiological threat.

### 2. What are we doing about it?

Identify the active intervention.

### 3. What happens if it does not work?

Identify the contingency.

This provides a rapid leadership reset.

---

# 30.74 The "What Changed?" Reset

Another useful approach is:

> "What changed?"

Then:

> "When did it change?"

> "What intervention occurred immediately before it?"

> "What explanation best fits the change?"

This can rapidly identify treatment-related deterioration or evolving pathology.

---

# 30.75 The "What Are We Missing?" Question

At appropriate points, ask:

> "What are we missing?"

The question should be directed at the whole team.

Different disciplines may identify different risks.

A nurse may identify a trend.

A radiographer may notice an imaging concern.

A junior doctor may recognise a neurological change.

A paramedic may provide critical mechanism information.

---

# 30.76 Create a Culture of Challenge

A strong trauma team should be able to challenge the leader respectfully.

The leader should welcome statements such as:

> "I'm concerned that..."

> "I don't think that explains..."

> "Could we reassess...?"

The response should be:

> "Let's examine that."

not:

> "We've already decided."

---

# 30.77 When Team Members Disagree

Disagreement should be converted into explicit reasoning.

Ask:

> "What is your concern?"

> "What evidence supports it?"

> "What would we do differently if you're right?"

Then decide.

The objective is not to eliminate disagreement.

It is to use disagreement safely.

---

# 30.78 Escalation of Disagreement

If a team member remains concerned about an immediate safety issue, the leader should take the concern seriously even if the final decision remains unchanged.

Safety concerns should never be dismissed solely because of hierarchy.

---

# 30.79 Uncertainty and Ethical Decision-Making

Sometimes uncertainty involves not only diagnosis but also goals and proportionality of treatment.

This may occur in:

- severe pre-existing disease;
- devastating injury;
- prolonged resuscitation;
- uncertain prognosis;
- limited resources.

The leader should involve appropriate senior and specialist support and ensure that decisions remain patient-centred.

---

# 30.80 Avoid Futility Language Without Appropriate Assessment

Terms such as:

> "Nothing can be done."

should be avoided unless the clinical situation has been appropriately assessed.

A more accurate statement is:

> "We need to determine whether there is a reversible cause and what treatment options remain."

---

# 30.81 Resource-Aware Uncertainty

In constrained environments, the leader may not have immediate access to:

- specialist imaging;
- operating theatre;
- blood products;
- specialist clinicians;
- advanced monitoring;
- definitive procedures.

The leader must then ask:

> "What is the safest achievable next step with the resources available?"

This is not abandoning ideal care.

It is adapting care to reality while continuing to pursue escalation or transfer when appropriate.

---

# 30.82 Avoid Letting Resource Limitations Create Therapeutic Nihilism

Limited resources do not mean limited leadership.

The leader should still:

- control preventable haemorrhage;
- protect the airway;
- support breathing;
- maintain perfusion;
- prevent hypothermia;
- monitor trends;
- communicate clearly;
- arrange escalation.

---

# 30.83 When Transfer Is the Solution

If definitive care is unavailable locally, the leader should recognise this early.

The transfer decision should consider:

- current stability;
- likely deterioration;
- required interventions;
- receiving capability;
- transport capability;
- escort requirements;
- equipment;
- contingency planning.

Transfer should not simply mean:

> "Send the patient elsewhere."

It should be a controlled clinical process.

---

# 30.84 The Transfer Threshold

The threshold for escalation should consider both:

- the patient's current condition;
- the capability of the current environment.

A patient who is manageable in a major trauma centre may be high risk in a resource-limited facility.

---

# 30.85 Uncertainty During Prolonged Resuscitation

Long resuscitations create additional risks:

- fatigue;
- loss of situational awareness;
- repeated assumptions;
- communication failure;
- unclear ownership;
- missed deterioration.

The leader should periodically reset the team.

> "Let's review where we are."

---

# 30.86 Rotate Attention

The leader should avoid fixation on a single problem.

During prolonged care, deliberately scan:

- airway;
- breathing;
- circulation;
- neurological status;
- bleeding;
- temperature;
- monitoring;
- team performance;
- destination.

---

# 30.87 Protect the Team's Cognitive Capacity

A leader should avoid unnecessary interruptions and irrelevant discussion during critical moments.

Information should be prioritised.

Critical information should be communicated immediately.

Non-urgent information can wait.

---

# 30.88 Use Closed-Loop Communication

In uncertain situations, closed-loop communication becomes even more important.

Leader:

> "Prepare blood products."

Team member:

> "Blood products being prepared."

Later:

> "Blood is ready."

Leader:

> "Good."

This prevents assumptions about task completion.

---

# 30.89 Document Important Changes

Important changes in:

- diagnosis;
- physiology;
- treatment;
- response;
- escalation;
- transfer plan;

should be documented appropriately.

Documentation preserves the clinical narrative and supports continuity of care.

---

# 30.90 Handover During an Evolving Situation

The receiving team should know:

- what happened;
- what has changed;
- current physiological state;
- major injuries;
- treatment given;
- response;
- unresolved uncertainty;
- immediate risks;
- contingency plan.

A useful statement is:

> "The diagnosis remains uncertain, but our major concern is ongoing haemorrhage. The patient has responded only partially to resuscitation and requires definitive haemorrhage control."

This is safer than falsely presenting certainty.

---

# 30.91 The Leader's Internal Checklist

When uncertainty is increasing, mentally ask:

- What do I know?
- What do I not know?
- What is changing?
- What is the immediate threat?
- What dangerous diagnosis remains possible?
- Has the patient responded as expected?
- What does not fit?
- What needs to happen now?
- What can happen in parallel?
- What happens if the current plan fails?
- Who needs to know?
- Do we need more senior help?
- Is the current environment capable of managing this patient?

---

# 30.92 A Practical Uncertainty Framework

A concise framework for the trauma team leader is:

## STOP

**S — Situation**

What is happening now?

**T — Threat**

What could kill or permanently harm the patient?

**O — Options**

What are the reasonable immediate actions?

**P — Plan**

What are we doing now, and what is our contingency?

Then:

> **Reassess.**

---
