# Chapter 29 — Handover, Transfer, Complex Trauma, and Resource Constraints

## Handover, Transfer, Complex Trauma, and Resource Constraints in Trauma Leadership

> Safe trauma leadership requires more than managing an individual clinical problem. The leader must maintain control across transitions, coordinate complex and competing threats, anticipate deterioration, and adapt safely when resources are constrained.

This chapter integrates three closely related leadership domains:

- handover and transfer of the critically injured patient
- leadership of complex and multi-system trauma
- leadership when resources, staffing, expertise, equipment, or infrastructure are constrained

The central principle is continuity of clinical ownership: the trauma team leader must maintain situational awareness, communicate clearly, anticipate the next threat, and ensure that responsibility is transferred safely.

## Introduction

Trauma leadership does not end when the primary survey is complete, the major intervention has been performed, or the decision has been made to move the patient to another clinical area.

A critically injured patient often passes through several transitions:

* ambulance to resuscitation room,
* resuscitation room to CT,
* CT to operating theatre,
* emergency department to intensive care,
* emergency department to interventional radiology,
* operating theatre to ICU,
* one hospital team to another,
* or one clinical shift to the next.

Every transition creates an opportunity for information to be lost, responsibility to become unclear, equipment to be unavailable, or deterioration to occur without an appropriate response.

For the trauma team leader, therefore, **handover and transfer are clinical leadership tasks, not administrative tasks**.

The leader must ensure that:

1. the receiving team understands the patient's current condition;
2. the critical problems have been clearly identified;
3. interventions and responses are communicated;
4. pending investigations and decisions are explicit;
5. responsibility is transferred deliberately;
6. the patient is appropriately prepared for movement;
7. the team knows what may happen next;
8. deterioration during transfer is anticipated;
9. and no important information depends on memory alone.

A high-quality handover should not merely describe what has happened.

It should allow the receiving team to understand:

> **Who is this patient, what has happened, what have we done, what is happening now, what remains dangerous, and what needs to happen next?**

---

# 1. The Leader's Responsibility at Handover

The trauma team leader is responsible for creating a reliable transition between teams.

This responsibility includes both **information transfer** and **clinical transition**.

A handover is inadequate if the information is technically accurate but the receiving team does not understand its significance.

For example:

> "Pelvic fracture. CT done. Blood given."

This may be factually correct.

But it does not communicate:

* whether the patient is haemodynamically stable;
* whether haemorrhage is ongoing;
* how much blood has been administered;
* whether pelvic haemorrhage is suspected;
* whether a pelvic binder remains in place;
* whether further intervention is planned;
* whether the patient responded to resuscitation;
* or what the receiving team should do if the patient deteriorates.

The leader should therefore communicate **meaning, not merely events**.

A stronger handover might communicate:

> "This is a patient with blunt high-energy trauma and an unstable pelvic injury. They remain at risk of ongoing haemorrhage. They initially responded to blood products but remain physiologically fragile. A pelvic binder is in place. CT has demonstrated active pelvic bleeding, and the current plan is urgent haemorrhage control. If there is further deterioration, treat this as ongoing haemorrhage rather than assuming the problem has resolved."

The second handover gives the receiving team a clinical model.

That is the purpose of leadership during handover.

---

# 2. Handover Is a Transfer of Responsibility

Handover is not simply the transfer of information.

It is also a transfer of **clinical responsibility**.

The leader should know:

* who currently has responsibility;
* who will assume responsibility;
* when that responsibility changes;
* what information must accompany the transfer;
* and whether the receiving team has accepted the patient.

This becomes particularly important when several teams are involved.

For example:

* emergency medicine;
* trauma surgery;
* orthopaedics;
* anaesthesia;
* radiology;
* interventional radiology;
* intensive care;
* neurosurgery;
* vascular surgery.

Multiple teams can create ambiguity.

Everyone may believe that someone else is responsible.

The trauma leader should actively eliminate this ambiguity.

A useful principle is:

> **There should never be uncertainty about who is responsible for the patient at any point during the transition.**

---

# 3. The Handover Should Have a Clear Beginning and End

A structured handover should not happen gradually in the background.

The leader should establish the transition deliberately.

For example:

> "I am going to hand over the patient to the ICU team."

Then:

> "This is a 34-year-old patient involved in a high-speed road traffic collision..."

At the end:

> "The immediate priorities are ongoing haemorrhage surveillance, maintaining oxygenation and blood pressure, and reassessment after the intervention. Do you have any questions?"

The receiving clinician should then acknowledge the handover.

This creates a clear boundary.

---

# 4. Prepare Before Speaking

The best handovers are prepared before the first sentence is spoken.

Before handover, the leader should mentally organise:

### Patient

Who is the patient?

### Mechanism

What happened?

### Injuries

What are the important injuries?

### Physiology

How sick is the patient now?

### Interventions

What has already been done?

### Response

Did the patient improve?

### Current risks

What can kill or deteriorate this patient next?

### Pending information

What is still unknown?

### Plan

What needs to happen next?

### Responsibility

Who is taking over?

This preparation prevents the handover from becoming a chronological story.

---

# 5. Do Not Tell the Story in Chronological Order

A common error is to describe the entire resuscitation from beginning to end.

For example:

> "The ambulance arrived at 09:42. We moved the patient onto the trolley. Then the airway doctor assessed them. Then we put two cannulas in..."

This may be accurate but is inefficient.

The receiving team needs a **clinical summary**, not a transcript.

A better approach is to prioritise:

1. identity;
2. mechanism;
3. major injuries;
4. current physiology;
5. interventions;
6. response;
7. current concerns;
8. next steps.

---

# 6. A Structured Trauma Handover

A practical trauma handover can follow this structure:

## Identification

* age;
* sex;
* relevant background;
* mechanism.

## Major injuries

* confirmed injuries;
* suspected injuries;
* injuries requiring urgent treatment.

## Current physiology

* airway;
* breathing;
* circulation;
* neurological status;
* temperature;
* haemodynamic trend.

## Major interventions

* airway management;
* ventilation;
* chest procedures;
* haemorrhage control;
* blood products;
* surgery;
* analgesia;
* other critical interventions.

## Response

* response to treatment;
* persistent abnormalities;
* deterioration or improvement.

## Investigations

* important imaging findings;
* laboratory results;
* outstanding investigations.

## Current problems

* active haemorrhage;
* airway risk;
* respiratory compromise;
* traumatic brain injury;
* spinal injury;
* hypothermia;
* coagulopathy;
* other immediate threats.

## Plan

* destination;
* definitive treatment;
* monitoring;
* pending decisions.

## Contingencies

* what should happen if the patient deteriorates.

---

# 7. Mechanism Matters

Mechanism provides context for injury patterns.

The receiving team should understand:

* road traffic collision;
* fall from height;
* penetrating trauma;
* crush injury;
* blast injury;
* blunt assault;
* industrial injury;
* or other high-energy mechanism.

Mechanism should not replace clinical findings.

It should provide context.

For example:

> "High-speed frontal collision with significant intrusion and prolonged extrication."

is more useful than:

> "Road traffic accident."

The leader should communicate relevant mechanism details that influence injury risk.

---

# 8. Communicate the Major Injuries First

Do not bury important injuries inside a long narrative.

The receiving team should quickly know:

* what has already been identified;
* what remains suspected;
* and what remains potentially life-threatening.

For example:

> "The major injuries are a severe traumatic brain injury, multiple left-sided rib fractures with pulmonary contusion, and an unstable pelvic fracture with suspected active haemorrhage."

This establishes priorities immediately.

---

# 9. Communicate the Current Physiology

A trauma patient is dynamic.

The receiving team needs to know not only the latest observations but also the **trend**.

For example:

> "Blood pressure was initially 80 systolic, improved to 105 after blood products, but has subsequently fallen to 90."

This is much more informative than:

> "Blood pressure is 90."

Trend communicates trajectory.

Trajectory often predicts what will happen next.

---

# 10. Communicate Treatment Response

Intervention without response is incomplete information.

Instead of:

> "Two units of blood were given."

say:

> "Two units of blood were given; blood pressure improved transiently but subsequently fell again."

This tells the receiving team that the intervention did not provide definitive control.

Similarly:

> "The patient improved following chest decompression."

is more clinically useful than:

> "A chest drain was inserted."

---

# 11. Communicate Uncertainty Explicitly

Trauma care frequently occurs before diagnostic certainty exists.

The leader should distinguish:

* confirmed;
* suspected;
* excluded;
* unknown;
* pending.

For example:

> "There is confirmed splenic injury. Active bleeding has not yet been demonstrated."

or:

> "There is concern for cervical spinal injury; definitive imaging is pending."

Uncertainty should never be hidden.

A receiving team that believes an injury has been excluded may behave differently from a team that understands it remains possible.

---

# 12. The Most Dangerous Problem Should Be Obvious

A useful test is:

> **If the receiving team remembers only one thing, what must it be?**

The leader should make that explicit.

For example:

> "The immediate concern is ongoing haemorrhage."

or:

> "The major concern during transfer is deterioration in airway control."

or:

> "The patient has a severe head injury and cannot tolerate hypotension."

This creates a shared priority.

---

# 13. Closed-Loop Handover

Closed-loop communication should continue into handover.

The receiving clinician should not simply listen silently.

The leader should confirm understanding of critical information.

For example:

> "The patient has active pelvic bleeding and is going directly for haemorrhage control."

Receiving clinician:

> "Understood. Active pelvic bleeding; going directly for haemorrhage control."

Leader:

> "Correct."

This is especially valuable for:

* airway concerns;
* blood products;
* major injuries;
* urgent procedures;
* medication infusions;
* pending investigations;
* and deterioration plans.

---

# 14. Critical Information Should Be Repeated

Not every detail requires repetition.

Critical information does.

Examples include:

* difficult airway;
* cervical spine precautions;
* ongoing haemorrhage;
* massive transfusion;
* anticoagulation;
* severe traumatic brain injury;
* unstable spinal injury;
* active chest pathology;
* vasoactive infusions;
* allergies;
* major medication issues.

A useful principle is:

> **The more catastrophic the consequence of misunderstanding, the stronger the communication should be.**

---

# 15. Handover to CT

CT is often treated as a simple destination.

For an unstable trauma patient, it is not.

The leader should ensure:

* monitoring is available;
* oxygen supply is adequate;
* vascular access is secure;
* infusions can continue;
* blood products can accompany the patient when required;
* airway equipment is available;
* emergency equipment is immediately accessible;
* the team knows who is leading during the scan;
* and the patient can be rapidly moved back to the resuscitation area if necessary.

Before leaving the resuscitation room:

> "Is the patient ready for CT?"

should mean more than:

> "Has the scan been requested?"

It should mean:

> **Can this patient safely survive the transition?**

---

# 16. The CT Handover

Before imaging, the radiology team should understand the relevant clinical question.

For example:

> "This patient has significant blunt abdominal trauma, persistent tachycardia despite resuscitation, and concern for intra-abdominal haemorrhage."

This is more useful than:

> "Polytrauma for CT."

The clinical question helps the receiving team interpret the examination appropriately.

---

# 17. Maintain Leadership During CT

The trauma leader should not automatically disappear when the patient enters the scanner.

Someone must remain responsible for:

* monitoring;
* physiological deterioration;
* communication;
* emergency response;
* and coordination.

If the trauma leader transfers responsibility to another clinician, that transfer should be explicit.

---

# 18. Handover to the Operating Theatre

The operating theatre team needs to know:

* mechanism;
* injuries;
* physiology;
* blood loss;
* blood products administered;
* airway status;
* vascular access;
* medications;
* imaging findings;
* procedures already performed;
* and the reason for surgery.

Most importantly:

> **Why is the patient going to theatre now?**

For example:

> "The patient has ongoing intra-abdominal haemorrhage despite resuscitation and requires emergency laparotomy."

This communicates urgency and purpose.

---

# 19. Blood Products and Haemorrhage

In a bleeding trauma patient, communicate:

* estimated blood loss;
* blood products administered;
* response;
* ongoing bleeding;
* coagulation concerns;
* temperature;
* calcium issues where relevant;
* and whether further blood products are anticipated.

The receiving team should not have to reconstruct the resuscitation from multiple charts.

---

# 20. Handover to ICU

ICU handover should include both current status and anticipated problems.

Important information includes:

* airway;
* ventilator status;
* sedation;
* haemodynamics;
* vasoactive support;
* neurological status;
* injuries;
* procedures;
* blood products;
* laboratory abnormalities;
* imaging;
* lines and drains;
* medications;
* and planned interventions.

But the leader should also communicate:

> **What is most likely to go wrong next?**

For example:

> "The patient is currently stable, but I am concerned about recurrent haemorrhage and worsening acidosis."

That information may influence ICU monitoring intensity and preparation.

---

# 21. Handover to Interventional Radiology

When transferring a patient for interventional radiology, the team should understand:

* the bleeding source or suspected source;
* haemodynamic stability;
* transfusion requirements;
* airway status;
* access;
* anticoagulation issues;
* imaging findings;
* and the intended procedure.

The leader should ensure that the patient can safely reach the procedural area.

---

# 22. Transfer Is a Clinical Procedure

Moving a critically injured patient is itself a clinical intervention.

During transfer:

* the airway can become displaced;
* oxygen can run out;
* monitoring can fail;
* vascular access can be lost;
* blood pressure can deteriorate;
* bleeding can recur;
* hypothermia can worsen;
* and communication can become fragmented.

Therefore:

> **A transfer should be planned with the same seriousness as a procedure.**

---

# 23. The Pre-Transfer Pause

Before leaving the resuscitation area, perform a deliberate pause.

Ask:

### Patient

* Is the patient sufficiently stabilised for the intended transfer?
* What remains immediately life-threatening?

### Airway

* Is the airway secure?
* Is the airway equipment available?

### Breathing

* Is oxygen adequate?
* Is ventilation appropriate?
* Are chest drains functioning?

### Circulation

* Are vascular accesses secure?
* Are infusions running?
* Is blood available if needed?

### Monitoring

* Is continuous monitoring available?
* Is the equipment functioning?

### Medication

* Are analgesia, sedation and vasoactive drugs sufficient for the journey?

### Equipment

* Is emergency equipment available?

### Team

* Who is leading?
* Who is responsible for airway?
* Who is responsible for circulation?
* Who is responsible for monitoring?

### Destination

* Is the receiving team ready?

---

# 29.1 Do Not Transfer to an Unprepared Destination

One of the simplest preventable failures is arriving before the receiving team is ready.

The leader should confirm:

* destination;
* receiving clinician;
* required equipment;
* appropriate bed;
* procedural readiness;
* and communication pathway.

A patient should not arrive in theatre, ICU, or another critical area with the receiving team learning about the patient for the first time.

---

# 29.2 The Transfer Team

The composition of the transfer team should match the patient's risk.

A critically unstable patient may require personnel capable of:

* airway management;
* advanced resuscitation;
* haemodynamic support;
* emergency intervention;
* and recognition of deterioration.

The leader should not assume that the same team composition is appropriate for every patient.

---

# 26. Equipment Readiness

Before transfer, check:

* oxygen supply;
* suction;
* monitoring;
* defibrillation capability where appropriate;
* airway equipment;
* vascular access equipment;
* emergency medications;
* infusion pumps;
* batteries;
* portable ventilator where required;
* chest drain systems;
* blood products where indicated.

A common failure is assuming that equipment will be available at the destination.

The leader should plan for the journey itself.

---

# 27. Maintain Situational Awareness During Transfer

The team should continue to monitor:

* heart rate;
* blood pressure;
* oxygen saturation;
* respiratory status;
* neurological status;
* bleeding;
* temperature;
* infusion delivery;
* and overall trajectory.

Transfer is not a pause in treatment.

It is treatment in a different environment.

---

# 29.3 The Leader Should Continue to Think Ahead

During transfer, ask:

> "What could deteriorate during the next five minutes?"

Possible answers include:

* airway obstruction;
* hypoxia;
* hypotension;
* recurrent haemorrhage;
* tension pneumothorax;
* loss of vascular access;
* equipment failure;
* or worsening neurological status.

Anticipation allows the team to prepare before the problem occurs.

---

# 29. Deterioration During Transfer

If the patient deteriorates, the leader should avoid continuing the planned transfer simply because the team has already started moving.

Stop.

Reassess.

Prioritise.

Treat.

Communicate.

The destination may need to change.

For example:

> "Stop the transfer. The patient has become hypotensive. We are reassessing for haemorrhage."

This is leadership.

The plan is subordinate to the patient's current physiology.

---

# 30. Transfer Should Not Become a Tunnel

Once a destination has been chosen, teams can become psychologically committed to reaching it.

This is a form of fixation.

For example:

> "We are going to CT."

Then the patient becomes hypotensive.

The team may continue because:

> "We are almost there."

That is unsafe.

The correct question is:

> **"Is CT still the safest destination given the patient's current condition?"**

Plans must remain dynamic.

---

# 31. Handover at the Destination

Arrival does not automatically mean successful transfer.

The leader should ensure:

1. the receiving team is present;
2. monitoring is connected;
3. airway and ventilation are maintained;
4. infusions continue;
5. the patient's current status is communicated;
6. critical findings are highlighted;
7. responsibility is transferred;
8. questions are answered;
9. and the transfer is explicitly completed.

---

# 32. The Final Handover

A concise final handover might be:

> "This is a 42-year-old patient after high-speed blunt trauma. Major injuries are severe chest trauma and an unstable pelvic fracture with ongoing haemorrhage. The airway is secured and the patient is ventilated. They have received blood products with only transient haemodynamic improvement. CT demonstrated active pelvic bleeding. The immediate priority is haemorrhage control. They remain high risk for deterioration. The pelvic binder remains in place and blood products are available."

Then:

> "The patient is now under your care."

This makes the transition explicit.

---

# 33. Confirm Acceptance

The leader should not assume that the receiving clinician has accepted responsibility.

Ask:

> "Are you happy to take over?"

or:

> "Can I confirm that you are taking over responsibility for the patient now?"

The receiving clinician should acknowledge.

This is particularly important when several teams are present.

---

# 34. Handover When Multiple Teams Are Present

Multiple clinicians can create communication noise.

Avoid allowing everyone to speak simultaneously.

The trauma leader should control the structure.

For example:

> "I'll give the overall handover first. Then I'll ask anaesthesia to provide the airway details and surgery to confirm the operative plan."

This preserves clarity.

---

# 35. Avoid Parallel Handover

Parallel conversations can produce contradictory information.

For example:

* one clinician tells ICU the patient is stable;
* another tells surgery the patient is deteriorating;
* a third assumes the CT findings have already been communicated.

The leader should establish one shared clinical picture.

---

# 36. Handover of the Airway

Airway information should include:

* current airway;
* method of securing it;
* difficulty encountered;
* tube size;
* depth where relevant;
* ventilation;
* oxygen requirements;
* cervical spine considerations;
* and anticipated airway problems.

If the airway was difficult, say so explicitly.

> "This was a difficult airway. Re-intubation may be challenging."

That statement may change how the receiving team prepares for future deterioration.

---

# 37. Handover of Chest Injuries

Communicate:

* pneumothorax;
* haemothorax;
* pulmonary contusion;
* rib fractures;
* chest drain placement;
* output;
* response;
* and ongoing concerns.

For example:

> "A left chest drain was inserted for haemopneumothorax. There has been ongoing blood-stained output, and the patient remains at risk of significant thoracic haemorrhage."

---

# 38. Handover of Neurological Injury

Communicate:

* initial neurological status;
* deterioration;
* pupil findings;
* imaging;
* airway status;
* sedation;
* seizure activity;
* and major treatment.

The leader should explicitly communicate physiological priorities.

For a patient with severe traumatic brain injury:

> "Avoid hypotension and hypoxia; neurological deterioration remains a major concern."

---

# 39. Handover of Spinal Injury

Communicate:

* suspected or confirmed spinal injury;
* neurological findings;
* immobilisation strategy;
* imaging;
* and precautions.

Do not simply say:

> "Spinal precautions."

Explain why they are required and what remains uncertain.

---

# 40. Handover of Pelvic Injury

Communicate:

* pelvic stability concerns;
* binder use;
* imaging;
* haemodynamic response;
* blood products;
* suspected haemorrhage;
* and definitive plan.

The receiving team should understand whether the pelvis is a major current threat or a secondary injury.

---

# 41. Pending Investigations

A dangerous handover failure is to mention that a test was ordered without identifying who will follow it.

Instead of:

> "Repeat haemoglobin pending."

say:

> "Repeat haemoglobin is pending and the receiving team should review it because the patient remains at risk of ongoing haemorrhage."

The responsibility for follow-up must be explicit.

---

# 42. Pending Decisions

The same applies to clinical decisions.

For example:

> "The neurosurgical plan is not yet finalised."

should be accompanied by:

> "The ICU team will contact neurosurgery after review of the imaging."

A pending decision without an assigned owner is a safety risk.

---

# 43. Medication Handover

Important medication information includes:

* analgesia;
* sedation;
* vasoactive drugs;
* anticoagulants;
* antiplatelet agents;
* antibiotics;
* reversal agents;
* and medications given during resuscitation.

The leader should identify medications that could alter the next clinical decision.

---

# 44. Blood Product Handover

When blood products have been administered, communicate:

* type and approximate quantity;
* ongoing requirement;
* response;
* complications;
* and availability of further products.

If the patient remains at risk of major haemorrhage, the receiving team must know.

---

# 45. Lines, Drains and Devices

The receiving team should know:

* arterial lines;
* central access;
* peripheral access;
* chest drains;
* urinary catheter;
* pelvic devices;
* monitoring devices;
* and other important equipment.

Where appropriate, communicate:

* location;
* function;
* complications;
* and ongoing output.

---

# 46. The Difference Between Information and Action

A handover should distinguish between:

### Information

> "The patient has a pelvic fracture."

and:

### Action

> "The pelvic binder remains in place and should not be removed until haemorrhage control has been addressed."

The second statement tells the receiving team what the information means for management.

---

# 47. The "What Worries Me" Statement

A particularly useful leadership technique is to include a personal clinical concern.

For example:

> "The thing that worries me most is that the patient's blood pressure has become increasingly difficult to maintain despite transfusion."

This communicates clinical judgement.

It also alerts the receiving team to a problem that may not be obvious from the numbers alone.

---

# 48. The "What Happens Next" Statement

Every major handover should answer:

> **What happens next?**

Examples:

> "The patient is going directly to theatre."

> "The patient is going to ICU for ongoing ventilation and neurological monitoring."

> "The patient is returning to resus because they remain unstable."

> "The patient requires urgent interventional radiology."

The receiving team should not have to guess the plan.

---

# 49. The "If Things Go Wrong" Statement

High-risk patients benefit from an explicit contingency plan.

For example:

> "If the blood pressure falls again, treat this as ongoing haemorrhage and activate the major haemorrhage pathway."

Or:

> "If the airway becomes compromised, re-intubation may be difficult, so senior airway support should be called immediately."

This converts anticipation into operational planning.

---

# 50. Common Handover Failures

## Failure 1 — No clear leader

Everyone talks.

Nobody owns the handover.

### Correction

One clinician controls the transition.

---

## Failure 2 — Excessive detail

The receiving team hears a long story but misses the critical information.

### Correction

Prioritise.

---

## Failure 3 — Too little information

Important risks are omitted.

### Correction

Use a structured framework.

---

## Failure 4 — No clinical interpretation

The handover becomes a list of facts.

### Correction

Explain what the findings mean.

---

## Failure 5 — No response to treatment

Interventions are listed without outcomes.

### Correction

State:

> intervention → response → current status.

---

## Failure 6 — No explicit responsibility transfer

Everyone assumes someone else is responsible.

### Correction

State who is taking over.

---

## Failure 7 — Pending tasks have no owner

Tests and decisions are mentioned but nobody is responsible for follow-up.

### Correction

Assign ownership.

---

## Failure 8 — Destination assumed to be ready

The patient arrives before the receiving team.

### Correction

Confirm readiness before departure.

---

## Failure 9 — Transfer continues despite deterioration

The team becomes committed to the original plan.

### Correction

Reassess continuously.

---

## Failure 10 — Critical information is buried

The most important concern appears at the end.

### Correction

Lead with the major threats.

---

# 51. Handover Under Time Pressure

Not every handover can be long.

During an emergency, the leader should compress the structure without abandoning it.

A rapid handover can be:

> **Who — What happened — Major injuries — Current physiology — What has been done — What worries me — What happens next.**

For example:

> "Thirty-year-old after high-speed MVC. Severe chest trauma and pelvic injury. Intubated, currently hypotensive despite blood products. Chest drain inserted with limited improvement. CT shows active pelvic bleeding. Main concern is ongoing haemorrhage. Going directly for definitive haemorrhage control."

This can be delivered rapidly while preserving critical information.

---

# 52. Handover During Ongoing Resuscitation

Sometimes the patient is too unstable to wait for a complete handover.

In this situation:

> **Resuscitation takes priority.**

The leader should communicate essential information while treatment continues.

For example:

> "This patient is actively bleeding. We are continuing transfusion while transferring to theatre. The major concern is haemorrhage."

The full handover can continue once immediate threats are controlled.

---

# 53. Handover During Surgery

If responsibility changes during operative care, the same principles apply.

The receiving clinician should understand:

* preoperative physiology;
* injuries;
* procedures performed;
* blood loss;
* blood products;
* complications;
* findings;
* and postoperative concerns.

A surgical handover should not assume that the receiving ICU team can reconstruct events from the operative note.

---

# 54. Handover From Theatre to ICU

The operating team should communicate:

* indication for surgery;
* findings;
* procedures;
* haemorrhage control;
* contamination;
* drains;
* packing;
* temporary closure where applicable;
* blood products;
* complications;
* remaining concerns;
* and planned re-look or further intervention where applicable.

The receiving ICU team needs to know not only what was done but what remains unresolved.

---

# 55. Handover During Shift Change

Shift change creates another transfer of responsibility.

The outgoing trauma clinician should identify:

* patients still under active management;
* pending investigations;
* unresolved problems;
* likely deterioration;
* planned interventions;
* and outstanding communication.

The incoming clinician should know:

> **What must I watch?**

and:

> **What must I do?**

---

# 56. The High-Risk Patient List

A useful leadership practice is to identify patients who require active follow-up.

Examples:

* unstable haemodynamics;
* ongoing bleeding;
* severe traumatic brain injury;
* difficult airway;
* unresolved diagnostic uncertainty;
* major pending intervention;
* or deterioration risk.

The leader should avoid assuming that a patient becomes "someone else's problem" merely because the shift changes.

---

# 57. Handover and Documentation

Verbal handover should be supported by appropriate documentation.

Documentation should allow the receiving team to verify:

* diagnosis;
* interventions;
* medications;
* blood products;
* investigations;
* and decisions.

However:

> **Documentation does not replace verbal communication when the situation is high risk.**

---

# 58. Handover and Human Factors

Handover is vulnerable to:

* interruptions;
* noise;
* fatigue;
* hierarchy;
* cognitive overload;
* confirmation bias;
* assumptions;
* and competing priorities.

The leader can reduce these risks by creating structure.

For example:

> "Please hold non-urgent questions until I finish the summary."

This protects the critical information pathway.

---

# 59. Protect the Handover

When possible:

* reduce unnecessary interruptions;
* identify the person leading;
* position the relevant clinicians together;
* use clear language;
* avoid ambiguous terminology;
* and confirm critical information.

The handover environment influences handover quality.

---

# 60. Handover Across Professional Boundaries

A trauma handover may involve:

* doctors;
* nurses;
* paramedics;
* radiographers;
* anaesthesia staff;
* operating theatre staff;
* ICU staff;
* and other professionals.

The leader should ensure that all critical information reaches the people who need it.

Not every person needs every detail.

But anyone responsible for a critical component of care must receive the relevant information.

---

# 61. Respectful Handover

Good leadership does not use handover to criticise the previous team.

Avoid:

> "They didn't manage the patient properly."

Instead communicate:

> "The patient remained hypotensive despite initial resuscitation."

Focus on:

* current facts;
* clinical risk;
* and future action.

---

# 62. Handover Should Not Become Blame

The purpose of handover is safe continuation of care.

If an error has occurred, it should be communicated appropriately.

But the immediate objective remains:

> **stabilise the patient and prevent further harm.**

Detailed review can occur later through appropriate governance processes.

---

# 63. When Information Is Missing

Sometimes information genuinely cannot be obtained.

Do not guess.

Say:

> "The exact time of the injury is uncertain."

or:

> "The amount of blood administered before arrival is unclear."

Uncertainty is safer than false precision.

---

# 64. Handover of Unknown Information

Unknown information should be labelled.

Useful phrases include:

> "Not yet known."

> "Not confirmed."

> "Pending."

> "Unable to establish."

This prevents assumptions from becoming clinical facts.

---

# 65. Transfer to a Different Hospital

Inter-hospital transfer introduces additional risks.

The leader should ensure communication regarding:

* diagnosis;
* current stability;
* interventions;
* imaging;
* blood products;
* airway;
* medications;
* reason for transfer;
* destination;
* receiving clinician;
* and anticipated requirements.

The receiving hospital should know what is coming before the patient leaves.

---

# 66. The Receiving Team Should Know Why the Patient Is Being Transferred

For example:

> "Transfer is required because definitive neurosurgical care is not available locally."

This explains the purpose.

It also allows the receiving team to prepare the relevant resources.

---

# 67. Transfer Is Not Complete Until the Receiving Team Has the Patient

Physical arrival is not the same as successful handover.

The transition is complete only when:

* the patient is physically transferred;
* monitoring is established;
* critical treatment continues;
* the receiving team has received the relevant information;
* and responsibility is clearly accepted.

---

# 68. Leadership During the Final Transition

The trauma leader should remain focused until the transition is complete.

Do not mentally "switch off" once the patient reaches the destination.

The final minutes can contain:

* missed information;
* equipment changes;
* loss of access;
* changes in physiology;
* or confusion about responsibility.

Finish deliberately.

---

# 69. A Practical Handover Framework

A useful compact structure is:

## 1. WHO?

Age, sex and relevant background.

## 2. WHAT HAPPENED?

Mechanism and timing.

## 3. WHAT IS INJURED?

Major confirmed and suspected injuries.

## 4. HOW SICK IS THE PATIENT?

Current physiology and trends.

## 5. WHAT HAVE WE DONE?

Major interventions.

## 6. DID IT WORK?

Response to treatment.

## 7. WHAT WORRIES ME?

Immediate threats and uncertainty.

## 8. WHAT HAPPENS NEXT?

Definitive plan.

## 9. WHAT IF THEY DETERIORATE?

Contingency.

## 10. WHO IS RESPONSIBLE NOW?

Explicit transfer of responsibility.

# 71. The Receiving Team Checklist

The receiving team should be able to answer:

* [ ] Who is the patient?
* [ ] What happened?
* [ ] What are the major injuries?
* [ ] What is the current physiology?
* [ ] What interventions have occurred?
* [ ] What was the response?
* [ ] What remains dangerous?
* [ ] What investigations are pending?
* [ ] What is the immediate plan?
* [ ] What could deteriorate?
* [ ] What should we do if deterioration occurs?
* [ ] Who is responsible?

If these questions cannot be answered, the handover is incomplete.

---

# 72. Example: Resuscitation Room to Theatre

### Poor handover

> "This is a 27-year-old MVC. CT shows abdominal injury. He has had blood. He's going to theatre."

### Stronger handover

> "This is a 27-year-old after high-speed MVC with confirmed splenic injury and active intra-abdominal bleeding. He initially presented hypotensive and tachycardic. He has received blood products with only transient improvement and remains haemodynamically unstable. His airway is currently secure and he has two large-bore IVs. The major concern is ongoing haemorrhage. He is going directly to theatre for definitive haemorrhage control. If his pressure deteriorates further during transfer, treat this as ongoing haemorrhage and continue the major haemorrhage response."

The second handover communicates:

* identity;
* mechanism;
* injury;
* physiology;
* treatment;
* response;
* concern;
* destination;
* and contingency.

---

# 73. Example: Resuscitation Room to ICU

> "This is a 58-year-old following a high-speed collision with severe traumatic brain injury and multiple rib fractures. He was intubated after a reduced level of consciousness and is mechanically ventilated. CT demonstrates severe intracranial injury. His blood pressure has been maintained with resuscitation and he currently requires close haemodynamic monitoring. The major priorities are preventing hypoxia and hypotension and monitoring for neurological deterioration. Neurosurgical review is ongoing. He is now transferring to ICU for continued ventilation and neurocritical care."

---

# 74. Example: Transfer to CT

> "This is a 35-year-old following a fall from height. The patient has abdominal tenderness and persistent tachycardia despite initial resuscitation. Airway and breathing are currently stable. Two large-bore IVs are secured. The clinical question is whether there is significant intra-abdominal injury or bleeding. The patient remains physiologically fragile, so continuous monitoring is required during the scan."

---

# 75. Example: Deterioration During Transfer

Imagine a patient being transferred to CT who suddenly becomes hypotensive.

The leader should say:

> "Stop."

Then:

> "Blood pressure has fallen significantly. We are returning to the resuscitation area."

Then:

> "Check airway, breathing and circulation. Prepare blood. Look for ongoing haemorrhage."

The important principle is:

> **The destination is not more important than the patient's physiology.**

---

# 76. The Leader's Mental Model During Transfer

The leader should continuously ask:

### Where are we?

Current location.

### Where are we going?

Destination.

### Why are we going there?

Clinical purpose.

### Is the patient ready?

Physiological readiness.

### Is the destination ready?

Operational readiness.

### What could go wrong?

Anticipation.

### What will we do if it happens?

Contingency.

### Who is responsible?

Ownership.

This simple mental model reduces tunnel vision.

---

# 77. Transfer as a Continuation of Resuscitation

A trauma resuscitation does not stop when the trolley starts moving.

The patient's physiology continues to evolve.

Therefore:

> **The leader must carry the resuscitation mindset into every transition.**

Continue to:

* observe;
* interpret;
* prioritise;
* communicate;
* intervene;
* reassess;
* and anticipate.

---

# 78. The Handover as a Safety Barrier

A strong handover is a safety barrier against:

* information loss;
* duplicated treatment;
* missed investigations;
* delayed intervention;
* unclear responsibility;
* and preventable deterioration.

A weak handover removes that barrier.

This is why handover quality is a leadership issue.

---

# 79. The Leader Should Communicate the Trajectory

The receiving team needs to understand whether the patient is:

* improving;
* stable;
* deteriorating;
* or fluctuating.

For example:

> "The patient is currently stable but has required increasing vasopressor support over the last 20 minutes."

This is more meaningful than a single normal blood pressure reading.

---

# 80. The Importance of Trends

Important trends include:

* blood pressure;
* heart rate;
* oxygenation;
* ventilation;
* neurological status;
* temperature;
* urine output;
* blood loss;
* lactate;
* haemoglobin;
* and transfusion requirements.

The leader should identify which trends matter most for the individual patient.

---

# 81. Avoid False Reassurance

A patient can look stable immediately before deterioration.

Statements such as:

> "Stable."

should be used carefully.

Prefer:

> "Currently haemodynamically stable after resuscitation, but remains at high risk of recurrent haemorrhage."

This distinguishes current status from future risk.

---

# 82. Handover and Anticipation

A strong handover tells the receiving team what to expect.

For example:

> "The patient has a high risk of becoming hypotensive again when sedation is reduced."

or:

> "The chest injury may require escalating ventilatory support."

Anticipation allows the next team to prepare.

---

# 83. Handover and Escalation

The receiving team should know when to escalate.

For example:

> "If vasopressor requirements increase or blood pressure falls despite resuscitation, senior surgical review is required."

This transforms the handover into an operational plan.

---

# 84. Leadership Language

Useful phrases include:

> "The key issue is..."

> "The immediate priority is..."

> "What worries me most is..."

> "The patient responded initially, but..."

> "This remains unresolved..."

> "The next step is..."

> "If the patient deteriorates..."

> "I want to make sure this is clear..."

> "Can you confirm you have that?"

> "Who is taking responsibility for this?"

These phrases improve clarity without unnecessarily increasing handover length.

---

# 85. Handover and Psychological Safety

A receiving clinician should feel able to ask:

> "Can you clarify that?"

The leader should welcome questions.

A handover is not a performance.

It is a safety-critical communication process.

If a junior clinician identifies an inconsistency, the leader should investigate rather than dismiss it.

---

# 86. When the Receiving Team Challenges the Plan

The leader should remain open to reassessment.

For example:

> "Why are you taking the patient to CT rather than directly to theatre?"

The appropriate response is clinical reasoning, not defensiveness.

> "Because the patient is currently stable enough for imaging and we need to identify the source of bleeding before definitive management."

If the patient's physiology changes, the plan should change.

---

# 87. Handover and Shared Mental Models

The goal is for both teams to have approximately the same understanding of:

* patient condition;
* priorities;
* risks;
* uncertainty;
* and plan.

This is a **shared mental model**.

Without it, different team members may make reasonable decisions based on different assumptions.

---

# 88. The Most Important Information Should Survive the Handover

Imagine that the receiving team becomes overwhelmed immediately after the handover.

Which information must they retain?

Usually:

1. major life threats;
2. current physiology;
3. major interventions;
4. unresolved problems;
5. immediate plan;
6. deterioration triggers.

The leader should make these impossible to miss.

---

# 89. The Final Question

At the end of a high-risk handover, ask:

> **"Is there anything you need clarified before I step away?"**

This creates an opportunity to identify missing information.

For critical patients, the leader should also ask:

> **"Who is the senior clinician responsible from this point?"**

This eliminates ambiguity.

---

# 90. Handover Is a Leadership Skill

Technical knowledge alone does not guarantee a safe handover.

The leader must:

* organise information;
* prioritise;
* communicate clearly;
* recognise uncertainty;
* anticipate deterioration;
* establish responsibility;
* and protect the transition from human-factor failures.

This is leadership in action.

---

# 91. Practical Trauma Leadership Prompts

Before transfer:

> **"What is the immediate threat?"**

> **"Is the patient ready to move?"**

> **"Is the destination ready?"**

> **"Who is leading during the transfer?"**

> **"What could deteriorate?"**

At completion:

> **"Has responsibility clearly transferred?"**

# 92. A One-Minute Trauma Handover

After completing a handover, ask:

## Closing Principle

The final responsibility of the trauma team leader is not simply to get the patient to the next destination.

It is to ensure that the **patient, information, priorities, risks and responsibility** all arrive together.

A successful transfer is therefore not:

> **"The patient reached CT."**

It is:

> **"The patient reached CT safely, the receiving team understands the clinical problem, the immediate priorities are clear, the major risks are anticipated, and responsibility has been deliberately transferred."**

That is effective trauma leadership beyond the resuscitation room.

## Purpose

Complex trauma is rarely difficult because of one isolated injury.

It is difficult because multiple injuries compete for attention, physiology may deteriorate faster than anatomy can be defined, information arrives incompletely, and several interventions may be required simultaneously.

The trauma team leader must therefore do more than recognise injuries.

The leader must create a coherent plan from incomplete information, maintain priorities, coordinate parallel work, anticipate deterioration, and repeatedly decide what matters most **now**.

This chapter focuses on the leadership of patients with:

- multiple traumatic injuries
- competing life threats
- major haemorrhage
- traumatic brain injury
- chest trauma
- abdominal or pelvic injury
- major extremity injury
- spinal injury
- complex mechanisms
- prolonged or evolving resuscitation
- conflicting clinical priorities

The central principle is:

> **In complex trauma, the leader must manage the patient as a physiological system rather than as a collection of individual injuries.**

---

# 1. Why Complex Trauma Is a Leadership Problem

A patient with multiple injuries creates a different cognitive environment from a patient with a single obvious injury.

Several problems may occur simultaneously:

- haemorrhage
- airway compromise
- hypoxia
- traumatic brain injury
- obstructive shock
- chest injury
- abdominal injury
- pelvic injury
- long-bone injury
- hypothermia
- coagulopathy
- acidosis
- contamination
- burns
- spinal injury

The danger is not simply that the team may miss one injury.

The greater danger is that the team may correctly identify several injuries but fail to recognise which one is currently determining survival.

The leader must continuously ask:

> **What is killing this patient first?**

Then:

> **What will kill this patient next if we do not act?**

And:

> **What information would change our next decision?**

These questions create a dynamic hierarchy of priorities.

---

# 2. The Complex Trauma Mental Model

A useful model is:

> **Recognise → Prioritise → Control → Reassess → Reprioritise**

The sequence is deliberately cyclical.

It is not:

> Diagnose everything → then treat.

In major trauma, complete diagnosis may occur too late.

The team must often act on:

- mechanism
- physiology
- examination
- focused imaging
- response to intervention
- probability
- trajectory

The leader therefore manages uncertainty rather than waiting for certainty.

---

# 3. The First Leadership Task: Establish Control

When the patient arrives, the leader should immediately establish:

- who is leading
- who is performing airway management
- who is responsible for circulation
- who is obtaining access
- who is documenting
- who is communicating with blood bank
- who is preparing imaging
- who is contacting definitive teams

The team should understand:

1. who is in charge
2. what the immediate priorities are
3. what information is required
4. what interventions are occurring
5. what the next decision point will be

A complex patient requires **more structure**, not less.

---

# 4. Start With Physiology

Before becoming absorbed in anatomical detail, assess the patient's physiological state.

Ask:

- Is the airway threatened?
- Is oxygenation adequate?
- Is ventilation adequate?
- Is the patient shocked?
- Is there uncontrolled haemorrhage?
- Is there evidence of obstructive shock?
- Is neurological function deteriorating?
- Is the patient becoming colder?
- Is the patient responding to resuscitation?

The first assessment should answer:

> **Is the patient stable enough for further assessment?**

If not, definitive intervention may need to occur before complete diagnostic evaluation.

---

# 5. The Concept of the Dominant Threat

In complex trauma, one injury often becomes the **dominant threat**.

Examples include:

- uncontrolled external haemorrhage
- catastrophic pelvic haemorrhage
- tension pneumothorax
- massive haemothorax
- airway obstruction
- severe traumatic brain injury with impending herniation
- major abdominal haemorrhage
- traumatic amputation with uncontrolled bleeding

The dominant threat may change during resuscitation.

Therefore:

> **The leader must repeatedly identify the current dominant threat rather than remain anchored to the original diagnosis.**

---

# 6. Competing Priorities

Complex trauma often creates genuine conflicts.

Examples:

### Severe traumatic brain injury + haemorrhagic shock

The team must protect cerebral perfusion while simultaneously controlling bleeding.

### Chest trauma + shock

Hypotension may represent:

- haemorrhage
- obstructive shock
- cardiac injury
- mixed pathology

### Pelvic fracture + traumatic brain injury

Aggressive haemorrhage control is essential, while avoiding secondary cerebral injury from:

- hypoxia
- hypotension
- hypothermia
- inappropriate ventilation

### Long-bone fracture + haemodynamic instability

The obvious limb injury may distract the team from internal haemorrhage.

The leader must therefore integrate competing physiological priorities.

---

# 7. Parallel Processing

Complex trauma cannot be managed effectively by a purely sequential model.

The team may need to perform simultaneously:

- airway assessment
- oxygenation
- haemorrhage control
- vascular access
- blood sampling
- blood product preparation
- pelvic stabilisation
- chest decompression
- neurological assessment
- exposure
- warming
- imaging preparation
- specialist activation

The leader coordinates these streams.

The question is not:

> "What do we do next?"

It is often:

> **"What needs to happen simultaneously?"**

---

# 8. Assign Tasks Explicitly

Avoid vague instructions such as:

> "Someone get access."

Instead:

> "Sarah, obtain two large-bore IVs."

Or:

> "Ahmed, establish intraosseous access if IV access is unsuccessful."

Then confirm:

> "Sarah, confirm when access is functioning."

Specific delegation reduces ambiguity.

---

# 9. Closed-Loop Communication in Complex Trauma

Every critical instruction should ideally follow:

1. instruction
2. acknowledgement
3. action
4. confirmation

Example:

> "Prepare the massive haemorrhage protocol."

> "Massive haemorrhage protocol activated."

Later:

> "Blood is ready."

This allows the leader to maintain an accurate mental model of what has actually happened.

---

# 10. Control the Information Flow

Complex trauma generates large quantities of information.

Not all information has equal importance.

The leader should distinguish:

### Critical information

Information that changes immediate management.

### Important information

Information that influences near-term decisions.

### Background information

Information that can wait.

Examples of critical information:

- blood pressure
- pulse
- oxygen saturation
- airway status
- consciousness
- major external bleeding
- response to blood products
- chest findings
- imaging demonstrating major haemorrhage

The leader should not allow low-value information to obscure critical information.

---

# 11. The Trauma Pause

At defined points, deliberately pause the process.

A useful trauma pause includes:

- What is the current physiological state?
- What injuries have been identified?
- What remains unknown?
- What has been treated?
- What remains uncontrolled?
- Is the patient improving?
- Is the patient deteriorating?
- What is the next definitive intervention?
- Which team needs to be activated now?

This is not inactivity.

It is **deliberate cognitive control**.

---

# 12. Reassessment After Every Major Intervention

Every major intervention should trigger reassessment.

Examples:

After chest decompression:

- respiratory effort
- oxygenation
- chest movement
- haemodynamics

After blood products:

- blood pressure
- heart rate
- perfusion
- ongoing bleeding
- temperature
- laboratory markers

After intubation:

- oxygenation
- ventilation
- tube position
- haemodynamics
- neurological status

After pelvic stabilisation:

- haemodynamic response
- ongoing blood loss
- need for definitive haemorrhage control

Never assume:

> "The intervention worked."

Ask:

> **"What changed after the intervention?"**

---

# 13. Response Is Diagnostic Information

The patient's response to treatment provides information.

For example:

A patient with hypotension who responds transiently to blood products may still have uncontrolled haemorrhage.

A patient with respiratory compromise who does not improve after oxygen may have:

- pneumothorax
- haemothorax
- airway obstruction
- pulmonary contusion
- severe ventilation failure

The response itself should therefore influence the diagnostic model.

---

# 14. Persistent Shock

Persistent shock despite initial resuscitation should trigger immediate reassessment.

Consider:

- ongoing external haemorrhage
- thoracic haemorrhage
- abdominal haemorrhage
- pelvic haemorrhage
- retroperitoneal bleeding
- major limb bleeding
- missed penetrating injury
- obstructive shock
- cardiac injury
- inadequate vascular access
- inadequate haemorrhage control

The leader should avoid repeatedly giving the same treatment without reconsidering the cause.

---

# 15. The Non-Responder

A patient who fails to respond as expected should trigger a cognitive reset.

Ask:

> **What assumption are we making that may be wrong?**

Then reconsider:

- diagnosis
- severity
- bleeding source
- airway
- ventilation
- circulation
- intervention effectiveness
- occult injury

The leader should say this aloud when appropriate:

> "The patient is not responding as expected. We need to reassess the cause of shock."

This prevents the team from continuing automatically.

---

# 16. Haemorrhage Control as a Leadership Priority

In major trauma, haemorrhage control is often time-critical.

The leader should rapidly determine:

- where the blood is going
- whether bleeding is externally controllable
- whether mechanical control is possible
- whether pelvic stabilisation is required
- whether operative control is required
- whether interventional radiology may be required
- whether the patient can safely undergo further imaging

The key question is:

> **What will definitively stop the bleeding?**

Temporary measures are important, but they should not become substitutes for definitive haemorrhage control.

---

# 17. Damage-Control Thinking

A severely injured patient may not tolerate prolonged definitive surgery.

Damage-control principles aim to:

- control haemorrhage
- control contamination
- restore physiology
- prevent further deterioration
- move toward definitive care when physiologically appropriate

The leader should understand that:

> **The best anatomical operation is not always the best physiological operation at that moment.**

---

# 18. The Lethal Triad

Severe trauma may produce:

- hypothermia
- acidosis
- coagulopathy

These interact and amplify one another.

The leader should therefore actively manage:

### Temperature

- expose only when necessary
- use active warming
- warm appropriate fluids and blood products
- minimise unnecessary environmental heat loss

### Coagulation

- activate appropriate haemorrhage protocols
- provide balanced blood-component resuscitation according to local protocol
- monitor coagulation

### Acidosis

- restore perfusion
- control haemorrhage
- improve oxygen delivery
- avoid prolonged inadequate resuscitation

---

# 19. Traumatic Brain Injury in the Multi-System Patient

Traumatic brain injury creates additional leadership complexity.

Secondary brain injury may be worsened by:

- hypoxia
- hypotension
- abnormal ventilation
- hyperthermia
- hypoglycaemia or hyperglycaemia
- seizures
- raised intracranial pressure

The team must therefore avoid allowing treatment of another injury to produce preventable cerebral injury.

The leader should continuously ask:

> **Are we protecting the brain while treating the rest of the patient?**

---

# 20. Brain and Bleeding: A Common Conflict

The patient with severe brain injury and major haemorrhage requires careful balancing.

The leader should avoid false choices such as:

> "We either treat the bleeding or protect the brain."

Both are required.

The correct approach is coordinated management:

- control haemorrhage
- maintain adequate perfusion
- prevent hypoxia
- maintain appropriate ventilation
- prevent avoidable physiological insults
- obtain definitive neurological assessment when safe

---

# 21. Chest Trauma

Major chest trauma may cause:

- pneumothorax
- tension pneumothorax
- haemothorax
- pulmonary contusion
- flail chest
- cardiac injury
- great-vessel injury

The leader should interpret chest findings in context.

Hypotension with respiratory deterioration should immediately raise concern for causes of obstructive or haemorrhagic shock.

---

# 22. Abdominal Trauma

Abdominal injury may remain clinically occult during the initial assessment.

Potential injuries include:

- solid-organ injury
- hollow-viscus injury
- mesenteric injury
- vascular injury
- retroperitoneal injury

The leader should avoid false reassurance from an initially non-diagnostic examination.

The trajectory matters.

---

# 23. Pelvic Trauma

Pelvic injury may be associated with major haemorrhage.

Leadership priorities include:

- early recognition
- appropriate pelvic stabilisation
- haemorrhage resuscitation
- minimising unnecessary manipulation
- rapid coordination with definitive services

The team should understand that pelvic haemorrhage may continue despite apparently modest external findings.

---

# 29.4 Extremity Trauma

Severe limb injury may be dramatic and visually distracting.

Potential threats include:

- arterial haemorrhage
- compartment syndrome
- open fracture
- crush injury
- traumatic amputation
- nerve injury
- ischaemia

The leader must ensure that dramatic limb pathology does not distract from immediately life-threatening internal injuries.

---

# 29.5 The Attention Trap

Humans naturally focus on:

- visible blood
- obvious deformity
- dramatic wounds
- loud alarms
- the most vocal team member
- the first abnormal finding

This creates a major risk:

> **The most visible problem may not be the most dangerous problem.**

The leader should deliberately ask:

> "What else could explain the physiology?"

---

# 26. Avoiding Anchoring

Anchoring occurs when the team commits prematurely to one explanation.

Examples:

> "The hypotension is from the pelvic fracture."

But the patient may also have:

- chest haemorrhage
- abdominal haemorrhage
- cardiac injury
- tension pneumothorax

Or:

> "The reduced consciousness is the head injury."

But the patient may also have:

- hypoxia
- hypotension
- hypoglycaemia
- intoxication
- seizures

The leader should periodically challenge the working diagnosis.

---

# 27. Diagnostic Time-Out

When the patient does not behave as expected, use a diagnostic time-out.

Ask:

1. What do we think is happening?
2. What evidence supports it?
3. What evidence contradicts it?
4. What have we not assessed?
5. What alternative diagnosis would explain the deterioration?
6. What immediate intervention is required?

This is particularly useful during prolonged resuscitation.

---

# 29.6 Managing Multiple Specialist Teams

Complex trauma may require:

- emergency medicine
- trauma surgery
- orthopaedics
- neurosurgery
- anaesthesia
- radiology
- interventional radiology
- cardiothoracic surgery
- vascular surgery
- intensive care
- nursing
- transfusion services

The trauma team leader should not allow specialist involvement to fragment leadership.

Specialists should contribute expertise while the overall resuscitation remains coordinated.

---

# 29. Specialist Input Must Answer a Question

Avoid calling a specialist with:

> "We have a very sick trauma patient."

Instead provide:

- mechanism
- physiological state
- relevant injuries
- interventions performed
- response
- immediate concern
- specific request

Example:

> "Thirty-five-year-old patient after high-speed collision. Persistent hypotension despite blood-product resuscitation. Pelvic fracture and free intra-abdominal fluid identified. We require urgent surgical assessment for definitive haemorrhage control."

Specific communication accelerates decision-making.

---

# 30. Maintain One Shared Plan

When several specialists are present, the leader should periodically summarise:

> "Our immediate priority is haemorrhage control. Anaesthesia is managing the airway. Blood products are ongoing. Surgery is preparing for operative control. Imaging is limited to information that will change immediate management."

This creates a shared mental model.

---

# 31. The Leader Must Know the Destination

A complex trauma resuscitation should not continue indefinitely without a destination.

Possible destinations include:

- operating theatre
- interventional radiology
- CT
- angiography
- intensive care
- specialist trauma centre
- transfer facility

The destination should be anticipated early.

---

# 32. Imaging Strategy

Imaging should answer clinical questions.

The leader should ask:

> **Will this scan change what we do now?**

A stable patient may undergo extensive imaging.

An unstable patient with an obvious need for definitive intervention may require immediate treatment rather than prolonged diagnostic evaluation.

The key is not:

> "Can we obtain more information?"

It is:

> **"Will obtaining this information improve the next decision enough to justify the delay and risk?"**

---

# 33. The Stable but High-Risk Patient

Not every complex trauma patient is immediately unstable.

A patient may initially have:

- normal blood pressure
- normal oxygen saturation
- relatively reassuring examination

yet still have serious injuries.

The leader should avoid equating initial stability with low risk.

Mechanism, injury pattern, age, comorbidity and evolving physiology all influence risk.

---

# 34. The Deteriorating Patient

Deterioration should be treated as new information.

Possible changes include:

- falling blood pressure
- rising heart rate
- worsening oxygenation
- increasing respiratory effort
- decreasing consciousness
- increasing lactate
- worsening metabolic acidosis
- increasing transfusion requirement
- increasing drain output
- new neurological deficit

The leader should identify deterioration early rather than waiting for collapse.

---

# 35. Trend More Than Snapshot

A single physiological value may be misleading.

The trajectory is often more informative.

For example:

- BP 100 → 90 → 80
- HR 100 → 115 → 130
- oxygen saturation 97% → 94% → 89%
- consciousness progressively declining

The leader should ask:

> **"Where is the patient heading?"**

not merely:

> **"Where are they now?"**

---

# 36. Anticipatory Leadership

In complex trauma, waiting for deterioration is often too late.

If the leader believes the patient is likely to deteriorate, preparations should begin early.

Examples:

- activate blood products
- prepare airway equipment
- notify theatre
- contact specialist teams
- prepare warming measures
- arrange transfer
- prepare additional vascular access
- alert radiology
- prepare for massive haemorrhage

The principle is:

> **Prepare before the physiological crash.**

---

# 37. The Next-Problem Question

At every stage ask:

> **"If nothing changes, what is most likely to go wrong next?"**

Then prepare for it.

Examples:

If bleeding continues:

> Prepare definitive haemorrhage control.

If airway swelling is progressing:

> Prepare advanced airway management.

If ventilation is worsening:

> Prepare for chest intervention and ventilatory support.

If consciousness is deteriorating:

> Prepare for airway and neurological management.

This converts anticipation into action.

---

# 38. Managing the Team During Prolonged Resuscitation

Long trauma resuscitations create fatigue.

Fatigue increases the risk of:

- communication failure
- omission
- tunnel vision
- delayed reassessment
- poor situational awareness

The leader should rotate tasks where possible and maintain regular summaries.

A prolonged resuscitation should become **more structured**, not less structured.

---

# 39. The Periodic Team Reset

During prolonged cases, perform a structured reset:

> "Team, let's pause for a brief summary."

Then state:

- mechanism
- current physiology
- known injuries
- major interventions
- response
- unresolved threats
- immediate plan
- destination

This can restore shared situational awareness.

---

# 40. Managing Uncertainty

Complex trauma frequently involves incomplete information.

The leader should be comfortable saying:

> "We do not yet know the source."

But uncertainty should be followed by action:

> "The patient remains unstable. We will treat the most likely life-threatening causes while continuing targeted assessment."

This is better than either:

- false certainty
- diagnostic paralysis

---

# 41. Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

A useful framework is:

### What do we know?

Established facts.

### What do we think?

Working diagnoses.

### What do we fear?

Potential catastrophic conditions.

### What can we do now?

Immediate interventions.

### What information do we need?

Information that will alter management.

This separates facts from assumptions.

---

# 42. The Red-Flag List

During complex trauma, the leader should maintain awareness of major deterioration signals:

- persistent hypotension
- increasing transfusion requirement
- worsening oxygenation
- escalating ventilatory support
- decreasing consciousness
- new neurological findings
- increasing abdominal distension
- worsening metabolic acidosis
- hypothermia
- persistent bleeding
- failure to respond to intervention

Any of these should prompt reassessment.

---

# 43. When the Plan Is Failing

A failing plan should not be protected simply because it was the original plan.

Ask:

> "Is our current strategy achieving its intended physiological goal?"

If not:

- stop
- reassess
- identify why
- modify the plan
- communicate the change

Leadership includes the ability to abandon an ineffective strategy.

---

# 44. Escalation

Escalation should occur early when:

- resources are insufficient
- definitive intervention is unavailable
- the patient's condition exceeds local capability
- specialist input is required
- transfer is likely to be necessary

Early escalation creates options.

Late escalation creates emergencies.

---

# 45. Transfer of the Critically Injured Patient

A complex trauma transfer is itself a high-risk procedure.

Before transfer, confirm:

- airway secured if required
- ventilation adequate
- haemorrhage controlled as far as possible
- blood products available
- monitoring established
- medications available
- analgesia/sedation addressed
- drains secured
- lines secured
- documentation complete
- receiving team informed
- destination confirmed

The leader should not assume that transfer is merely a transport problem.

It is a continuation of resuscitation.

---

# 46. Handover in Complex Trauma

The receiving team needs a concise but complete picture.

A useful structure includes:

### Patient

Age, sex and relevant background.

### Mechanism

How the injury occurred.

### Injuries

Known major injuries.

### Physiology

Current and worst physiological state.

### Interventions

What has been done.

### Response

How the patient responded.

### Blood products

What has been administered and what remains available.

### Outstanding threats

What remains uncontrolled or uncertain.

### Plan

What is expected to happen next.

---

# 47. The "What Are You Worried About?" Question

One of the most important elements of handover is explicitly stating:

> **"My main concern is..."**

For example:

> "My main concern is ongoing pelvic haemorrhage despite transfusion."

This allows the receiving team to immediately understand the leader's risk assessment.

---

# 48. Complex Trauma and Cognitive Load

The leader's cognitive capacity is limited.

Complex cases produce:

- multiple conversations
- alarms
- laboratory results
- imaging reports
- procedures
- specialist opinions
- changing physiology

The leader should reduce cognitive load by externalising information.

Useful methods include:

- whiteboard
- trauma record
- structured checklist
- verbal summaries
- named task allocation
- explicit priorities

---

# 49. The Leader's Internal Checklist

At regular intervals ask:

### Airway
Is it secure?

### Breathing
Is oxygenation and ventilation adequate?

### Circulation
Is bleeding controlled?

### Brain
Is neurological status stable?

### Exposure
Have important injuries been identified?

### Physiology
Are temperature, acid-base status and coagulation being addressed?

### Destination
Where does this patient need to go?

### Team
Does everyone understand the plan?

---

# 50. The Three-Level Trauma Plan

A useful leadership model is:

## Now

What must happen in the next few minutes?

## Next

What is likely to be required after the immediate threat is controlled?

## Later

What definitive management, monitoring or transfer will be required?

This prevents the team from becoming trapped in the immediate moment.

---

# 51. Avoiding Premature Closure

A complex trauma patient may have more injuries than the initial assessment reveals.

Therefore:

> **A normal finding in one system does not exclude serious pathology elsewhere.**

The leader should continue reassessment throughout the resuscitation.

---

# 52. The Second Primary Survey

After major interventions and once immediate threats are controlled, repeat the primary assessment.

Ask again:

- airway
- breathing
- circulation
- disability
- exposure

Then compare with the initial assessment.

This identifies changes that may have developed during resuscitation.

---

# 53. The Secondary Survey Must Remain Dynamic

The secondary survey is not simply a one-time examination.

New findings may emerge after:

- analgesia
- sedation
- warming
- haemodynamic stabilisation
- imaging
- neurological recovery
- reduction of distracting pain

The patient should therefore be reassessed repeatedly.

---

# 54. Common Leadership Failures in Complex Trauma

### Failure 1: Treating the most obvious injury first

The obvious injury may not be the dominant threat.

### Failure 2: Sequential rather than parallel management

Multiple life threats may require simultaneous treatment.

### Failure 3: Waiting for certainty

Definitive diagnosis may come too late.

### Failure 4: Failure to anticipate deterioration

The team reacts rather than prepares.

### Failure 5: Poor communication

Critical information becomes fragmented.

### Failure 6: Specialist fragmentation

Multiple specialists manage individual injuries without a shared plan.

### Failure 7: Failure to reassess

The team assumes that interventions have worked.

### Failure 8: Continuing a failing strategy

The original plan becomes difficult to abandon.

### Failure 9: Delayed escalation

Help is requested only after deterioration.

### Failure 10: Losing sight of destination

Resuscitation continues without a clear definitive pathway.

---

# 55. The Complex Trauma Leadership Algorithm

A practical sequence is:

1. **Establish leadership**
2. **Assess physiology**
3. **Identify immediate threats**
4. **Control immediately reversible causes**
5. **Identify the dominant threat**
6. **Run parallel workstreams**
7. **Allocate named tasks**
8. **Activate definitive resources early**
9. **Reassess response**
10. **Challenge the working diagnosis**
11. **Anticipate the next deterioration**
12. **Repeat the primary survey**
13. **Define destination**
14. **Prepare transfer or definitive intervention**
15. **Handover clearly**
16. **Debrief and learn**

---

# 56. Leadership Language During Complex Trauma

Useful phrases include:

> "Our immediate priority is..."

> "The patient is deteriorating because..."

> "I want two things happening simultaneously."

> "What is the most likely source of shock?"

> "What else could explain this?"

> "The patient has not responded as expected."

> "We need definitive haemorrhage control."

> "Please prepare for deterioration."

> "What is our destination?"

> "I am concerned about..."

> "Before we proceed, let's summarise."

> "Has that task been completed?"

These phrases make the leader's cognitive process visible to the team.

---

# 57. When the Team Becomes Overwhelmed

Signs include:

- multiple people speaking simultaneously
- repeated instructions
- unclear task ownership
- procedures being duplicated
- critical tasks forgotten
- specialists giving conflicting plans
- no one knowing the destination

The leader should deliberately slow the communication.

For example:

> "Stop for a moment. I am going to reset the plan."

Then:

1. state the current problem
2. state the priority
3. assign tasks
4. confirm tasks
5. state the next decision point

A short reset can restore control.

---

# 58. When the Leader Becomes Overwhelmed

Leadership does not require the leader to know everything personally.

The leader should use the team.

Ask:

> "What am I missing?"

> "What is your biggest concern?"

> "What has changed?"

> "What would you do next?"

This can reveal problems that the leader has not recognised.

A strong leader actively seeks disconfirming information.

---

# 59. The Specialist as an Information Source

Specialists should not only be asked to perform procedures.

They can provide important interpretation.

For example:

> "From the surgical perspective, what is the most likely source of ongoing bleeding?"

Or:

> "From an anaesthetic perspective, what is currently limiting our resuscitation?"

The leader integrates these perspectives into one plan.

---

# 60. The Team Must Know the Why

Compliance improves when team members understand the reason for an action.

Instead of:

> "Move to theatre."

Use:

> "The patient remains unstable with suspected uncontrolled intra-abdominal haemorrhage, so definitive operative control is now our priority."

The explanation creates shared understanding.

---

# 61. Complexity Does Not Justify Chaos

A complex patient does not require a complicated leadership system.

The principles remain simple:

- identify threats
- prioritise
- act
- communicate
- reassess
- anticipate
- escalate
- transfer or treat definitively

Complexity should increase discipline rather than reduce it.

---

# 62. The Leader's Role in Physiological Rescue

The leader should constantly connect interventions with physiology.

Instead of asking only:

> "Have we done the procedure?"

Ask:

> "Did the procedure improve the patient's physiology?"

This distinction is fundamental.

A technically successful procedure may still fail to solve the patient's dominant problem.

---

# 63. The Difference Between Activity and Progress

A resuscitation can be extremely busy without being effective.

Signs of activity include:

- multiple lines
- repeated blood tests
- many conversations
- multiple procedures
- extensive imaging

Progress means:

- haemorrhage is controlled
- oxygenation improves
- perfusion improves
- neurological status is protected
- definitive treatment is underway
- the patient is moving toward an appropriate destination

The leader must distinguish activity from progress.

---

# 64. A Useful Leadership Question

At any point during the resuscitation ask:

> **"What are we doing that is actually changing the patient's outcome?"**

This question can expose low-value activity and redirect attention toward definitive treatment.

---

# 65. Complex Trauma Is a Dynamic System

The patient changes.

The team changes.

The information changes.

The available resources change.

The leader's plan must therefore change when the situation changes.

A plan that was correct five minutes ago may become inappropriate now.

Good leadership is adaptive.

---

# 66. The Final Decision Point

Before leaving the resuscitation area, the leader should be able to state:

- what happened
- what injuries are known
- what threats remain
- what has been done
- whether the patient responded
- what definitive treatment is required
- where the patient is going
- who is responsible next

If these cannot be clearly stated, the team may not yet have a sufficiently shared mental model.

---

# 67. Practical Case: Multiple Trauma With Haemorrhagic Shock

A patient arrives after a high-speed road traffic collision.

Findings include:

- hypotension
- tachycardia
- pelvic instability
- reduced consciousness
- chest wall injury
- abdominal tenderness
- obvious femoral deformity

The inexperienced leader may attempt to manage each injury individually.

The effective leader recognises:

> This is a physiological emergency with multiple potential sources of deterioration.

Immediate priorities include:

- airway and oxygenation
- haemorrhage control
- blood-product resuscitation
- pelvic stabilisation where indicated
- assessment for thoracic causes of shock
- neurological protection
- rapid definitive pathway

The leader does not wait for every injury to be fully characterised.

---

# 68. Case: Persistent Shock Despite Blood Products

The patient receives blood products but remains hypotensive.

The leader should not simply continue transfusion without reassessment.

Ask:

- Is bleeding continuing?
- Where?
- Has pelvic haemorrhage been addressed?
- Is there thoracic haemorrhage?
- Is there abdominal haemorrhage?
- Could this be obstructive shock?
- Is cardiac injury possible?
- Is the vascular access adequate?
- What definitive intervention is required?

The patient's lack of response is new diagnostic information.

---

# 69. Case: Severe Head Injury With Abdominal Haemorrhage

The patient has:

- reduced consciousness
- suspected intracranial injury
- hypotension
- abdominal free fluid

The leader must prevent secondary brain injury while controlling life-threatening haemorrhage.

The plan should integrate:

- airway management when indicated
- oxygenation
- appropriate ventilation
- perfusion support
- haemorrhage control
- neurological protection
- early definitive specialist involvement

The leader should avoid treating the head and abdomen as separate patients.

---

# 70. Case: Dramatic Limb Injury

A patient arrives with traumatic amputation and severe external bleeding.

The limb dominates everyone's attention.

The leader must simultaneously ask:

- Is the airway secure?
- Is there another source of haemorrhage?
- Is there chest trauma?
- Is there abdominal trauma?
- Is there pelvic injury?
- Is the patient hypothermic?
- What is the definitive destination?

The dramatic injury must not become a cognitive trap.

---

# 71. Case: Deterioration During Imaging

A patient initially appears stable enough for imaging but develops:

- hypotension
- tachycardia
- worsening consciousness

The leader should immediately reconsider whether continuing imaging remains appropriate.

The priority may shift from:

> "Complete the scan."

to:

> **"Resuscitate and move toward definitive treatment."**

The plan must follow the patient, not the schedule.

---

# 72. Case: Multiple Specialist Opinions

One specialist recommends CT.

Another recommends theatre.

A third recommends further resuscitation.

The leader should not allow the team to become paralysed by competing recommendations.

Return to:

1. current physiology
2. known injuries
3. dominant threat
4. immediate risk
5. definitive treatment pathway

Then make the decision explicit.

---

# 73. The Leader Owns the Decision

Consultation does not remove leadership responsibility.

The leader should gather appropriate expert input and then communicate:

> "Based on the current physiology and suspected ongoing haemorrhage, we are proceeding to definitive haemorrhage control."

Clear decisions prevent drift.

---

# 74. Team Performance Under Complexity

The best-performing trauma teams usually demonstrate:

- clear leadership
- defined roles
- concise communication
- closed-loop communication
- anticipation
- situational awareness
- early escalation
- regular reassessment
- shared mental models

These behaviours become even more important as complexity increases.

---

# 75. Complexity Should Trigger More Structure

When the patient becomes more complex:

Do not:

- speak faster
- give vague instructions
- abandon reassessment
- allow everyone to act independently

Instead:

- slow the communication
- simplify priorities
- assign roles
- state the plan
- confirm actions
- reassess frequently

---

# 76. The Complex Trauma Checklist

Before definitive transfer or intervention, ask:

### Patient

- Is the airway adequate?
- Is oxygenation adequate?
- Is ventilation adequate?
- Is circulation adequate?
- Is haemorrhage controlled as far as possible?
- Is neurological status documented?
- Is the patient warm enough?

### Injuries

- Major thoracic injury?
- Abdominal injury?
- Pelvic injury?
- Brain injury?
- Spinal injury?
- Major limb injury?
- Other major injury?

### Resuscitation

- Access established?
- Blood products available?
- Ongoing transfusion requirements?
- Analgesia and sedation addressed?
- Temperature management underway?

### Team

- Roles clear?
- Specialist teams activated?
- Communication clear?
- Receiving team informed?

### Destination

- Theatre?
- Interventional radiology?
- CT?
- ICU?
- Transfer centre?

### Contingency

- What could deteriorate during the next phase?
- What will we do if it happens?

---

# 77. The Final Leadership Summary

The leader of a complex trauma team should continually perform five functions:

### 1. See

Understand the whole patient.

### 2. Prioritise

Identify what matters most now.

### 3. Coordinate

Make multiple actions occur together.

### 4. Anticipate

Prepare for the next deterioration.

### 5. Reassess

Determine whether the strategy is working.

This cycle continues until the patient reaches definitive care.

---

# 78. Key Principles

> **Treat physiology before becoming distracted by anatomy.**

> **The most obvious injury is not necessarily the most dangerous.**

> **Multiple life threats may require simultaneous treatment.**

> **The dominant threat can change during resuscitation.**

> **Response to treatment is diagnostic information.**

> **Persistent deterioration should trigger a cognitive reset.**

> **Definitive haemorrhage control should be anticipated early.**

> **Specialists should contribute to one shared plan.**

> **Complexity requires more structure, not less.**

> **Prepare for deterioration before it occurs.**

> **Activity is not the same as progress.**

> **The patient's trajectory matters more than a single snapshot.**

> **A plan must change when the patient's condition changes.**

> **The leader must always know the next destination.**

---

# 79. The Trauma Team Leader's Final Mental Model

When faced with a complex trauma patient, think:

> **What is happening?**

> **What is killing the patient?**

> **What can I reverse immediately?**

> **What needs to happen in parallel?**

> **What am I missing?**

> **Is the patient responding?**

> **What is likely to happen next?**

> **What definitive treatment is required?**

> **Where does this patient need to go?**

> **Does everyone know the plan?**

Then repeat.

# Conclusion

Complex and multi-system trauma tests the trauma team leader more than almost any other clinical situation.

The challenge is not simply recognising individual injuries.

The challenge is maintaining a coherent model of the entire patient while the situation changes.

The effective leader:

- recognises the dominant threat
- protects physiology
- controls haemorrhage
- coordinates parallel interventions
- prevents cognitive fixation
- uses the expertise of the team
- communicates clearly
- anticipates deterioration
- reassesses continuously
- escalates early
- and moves the patient toward definitive care

The central lesson is simple:

> **In complex trauma, leadership is the discipline of turning multiple simultaneous threats into one coordinated plan.**

And the final question remains:

> **"What does this patient need next, and what must I do now to make that happen?"**

## Introduction

Trauma leadership is often taught in environments where the necessary resources are assumed to be available:

- a fully staffed trauma team
- immediate access to anaesthesia
- blood products
- advanced airway equipment
- CT
- interventional radiology
- operating theatre access
- intensive care capacity
- specialist surgical support
- multiple experienced clinicians
- reliable communication systems
- functioning transport pathways

Real trauma care is not always delivered under these conditions.

A trauma team may instead be working with:

- limited personnel
- delayed specialist support
- restricted blood availability
- incomplete equipment
- limited monitoring
- no immediate CT
- no interventional radiology
- delayed theatre access
- long transfer times
- overcrowding
- simultaneous major incidents
- unreliable communication
- constrained oxygen supply
- limited critical-care beds
- staff with variable trauma experience

The principles of trauma leadership do not disappear when resources are limited.

They become more important.

The leader's task changes from:

> "How do we deliver the ideal trauma pathway?"

to:

> "Given the resources actually available, what is the safest achievable pathway for this patient?"

This chapter focuses on that problem.

Resource limitation must never become an excuse for disorganised care.

It should trigger:

- earlier prioritisation
- clearer communication
- more deliberate resource allocation
- earlier escalation
- earlier transfer planning
- stronger anticipation
- tighter reassessment
- explicit contingency planning

The resource-limited trauma leader must understand both **clinical priorities** and **system constraints**.

The leader is therefore managing two simultaneous problems:

1. the patient's physiological threat
2. the environment's ability to respond to that threat

The best leaders recognise the second problem before it becomes the reason the first problem becomes fatal.

---

# 29.7 The Central Principle: Work With Reality, Not Assumptions

A trauma leader must begin with an accurate understanding of what is actually available.

Do not lead according to what the guideline assumes should be available.

Lead according to:

> what is available now, what can be obtained quickly, and what cannot be obtained in time.

This distinction is critical.

A resource-limited environment can create dangerous cognitive errors.

For example:

> "The patient needs CT."

That statement may be clinically correct.

But it is incomplete.

The leader must immediately ask:

- Is CT available?
- Is the scanner functioning?
- Is radiology available?
- Can the patient safely leave the resuscitation area?
- Is the patient stable enough?
- Is CT likely to change management?
- How long will the scan take?
- What treatment cannot wait?
- Is transfer to another centre more appropriate?
- Can the diagnosis be established sufficiently without CT?

The correct question is therefore not simply:

> "What would be ideal?"

It is:

> "What is the safest effective action available now?"

---

# 29.8 Resource Awareness Is Part of Situational Awareness

Situational awareness includes more than physiology.

The trauma leader should maintain awareness of:

### Patient state

- airway
- breathing
- circulation
- neurological status
- temperature
- response to treatment
- trajectory

### Team state

- number of clinicians
- experience level
- workload
- fatigue
- role clarity
- communication quality

### Equipment state

- airway equipment
- suction
- oxygen
- monitoring
- vascular access equipment
- blood warmer
- haemorrhage-control equipment
- ultrasound
- transport equipment

### System state

- theatre availability
- blood bank capacity
- imaging availability
- ICU capacity
- specialist availability
- transfer capability
- ambulance availability

### Environmental state

- overcrowding
- multiple casualties
- infection-control restrictions
- access limitations
- security issues
- communication limitations
- power or equipment failure

The leader should continuously ask:

> "What limitation is most likely to affect this patient next?"

That question should be asked before the limitation becomes critical.

---

# 29.9 The Resource Hierarchy

When resources are constrained, not every intervention can be performed simultaneously.

The leader must prioritise resources according to clinical consequence.

A useful hierarchy is:

1. life-threatening physiological problems
2. immediate haemorrhage control
3. airway and ventilation support
4. essential monitoring
5. definitive intervention
6. diagnostic clarification
7. optimisation
8. documentation and secondary tasks

This does not mean diagnostics are unimportant.

It means that diagnostic activity must not displace immediately lifesaving treatment.

A CT scan cannot compensate for failure to control catastrophic haemorrhage.

A perfect blood result cannot compensate for an untreated airway obstruction.

A complete history cannot compensate for delayed treatment of tension physiology.

---

# 29.10 The Minimum Safe Team

In constrained environments, the leader should define the minimum team required to deliver safe care.

This may include:

- team leader
- airway clinician
- circulation/resuscitation clinician
- procedure clinician
- nurse responsible for medications and monitoring
- runner or coordinator

The exact structure will vary.

The principle does not.

Every critical task should have an owner.

If there are insufficient personnel, the leader must identify which tasks can safely be combined and which cannot.

For example:

One clinician may be able to:

- assess
- perform a procedure
- communicate findings

But that clinician may not be able to simultaneously:

- perform a complex procedure
- monitor physiology
- administer medication
- document accurately
- coordinate transfer

The leader must recognise these limits.

---

# 29.11 Never Create a Single-Point-of-Failure Team

A major risk in small teams is dependence on one individual.

Examples include:

- one clinician knows how to perform a critical procedure
- one person controls communication
- one nurse knows the medication process
- one doctor knows how to activate transfer
- one person knows where blood products are stored

If that individual becomes unavailable, the system becomes fragile.

The leader should therefore distribute critical knowledge.

For every essential process, ask:

> "Who can perform this if the primary person becomes unavailable?"

If the answer is:

> "Nobody."

then the team has identified a safety vulnerability.

---

# 29.12 Role Allocation Under Constraint

When staff numbers are low, role allocation should become more explicit, not less.

A useful approach is:

> **Person + Task + Expected Output**

For example:

> "Sara, manage airway equipment and tell me immediately if the airway becomes difficult."

> "Ahmed, establish IV access and start the blood protocol. Tell me when blood is running."

> "Khalid, contact the receiving trauma centre and give me an update within five minutes."

This prevents vague delegation.

---

# 29.13 The Leader Must Protect Cognitive Capacity

Resource limitation increases cognitive load.

The leader may simultaneously be:

- assessing the patient
- coordinating the team
- making clinical decisions
- arranging transfer
- managing equipment limitations
- communicating with specialists
- responding to relatives
- documenting
- supervising junior clinicians

This creates a major risk.

The leader can become the bottleneck.

A strong leader therefore delegates aggressively but safely.

The principle is:

> Delegate tasks, retain responsibility.

The leader does not need to perform every task.

The leader needs to ensure that every critical task is being performed correctly.

---

# 29.14 When Equipment Is Missing

Missing equipment should trigger an immediate alternative plan.

The leader should avoid spending excessive time searching for equipment without simultaneously maintaining patient care.

Use a simple sequence:

1. identify what is missing
2. determine whether it is essential
3. identify an acceptable alternative
4. assign someone to obtain the missing item
5. continue parallel management
6. escalate if the limitation changes the safety of care

For example:

> "We do not have the usual airway device. Continue oxygenation and prepare the alternative airway plan while someone obtains the backup equipment."

The key principle is parallel processing.

---

# 29.15 Equipment Failure

Equipment failure can rapidly become a clinical emergency.

Examples include:

- monitor failure
- oxygen supply failure
- suction failure
- ventilator failure
- infusion pump failure
- blood warmer failure
- ultrasound failure

The leader should immediately determine:

### Is the patient currently dependent on this equipment?

### Is there a manual alternative?

### Is another device available?

### Does the failure require escalation?

### Does the failure change the patient's transfer or operative plan?

The leader should communicate clearly:

> "The monitor has failed. We are switching to the backup monitor. Continue clinical observation while we replace it."

Avoid allowing equipment troubleshooting to consume the entire team's attention.

---

# 29.16 Oxygen as a Critical Resource

Oxygen is often treated as an unlimited resource.

In constrained environments, it may not be.

The leader should understand:

- available oxygen sources
- cylinder capacity
- wall supply
- transport oxygen
- expected duration
- backup availability

Before transporting a critically ill patient, ask:

> "Do we have enough oxygen for the journey, including delay?"

Transport planning must include contingency.

The question is not:

> "How long should the journey take?"

It is:

> "How much oxygen do we need if the journey takes longer than expected?"

---

# 29.17 Blood Product Constraints

Blood availability can fundamentally alter trauma management.

The leader should establish early:

- what blood products are available
- how quickly additional products can be obtained
- whether massive haemorrhage protocols are functional
- whether emergency blood is available
- whether blood products can accompany the patient during transfer
- what alternatives are appropriate if blood is limited

The absence of blood must trigger early escalation.

Do not wait until the final unit is being administered before contacting the next level of support.

---

# 29.18 Major Haemorrhage When Blood Is Limited

The principles remain:

- recognise haemorrhage early
- control bleeding
- activate the appropriate pathway
- minimise unnecessary crystalloid
- prevent hypothermia
- correct coagulopathy where possible
- reassess continuously
- obtain definitive haemorrhage control

But resource limitation requires additional questions.

> "How much blood do we have?"

> "How much blood can we obtain?"

> "How long will it take?"

> "Can the patient reach definitive haemorrhage control before our resources are exhausted?"

These questions may determine the transfer threshold.

---

# 29.19 The Transfer Threshold

One of the most important leadership decisions in a constrained environment is recognising when local capability is insufficient.

Transfer should not be viewed as failure.

Appropriate transfer is a clinical intervention.

The leader should ask:

> "Can this patient receive the definitive care they need here?"

If the answer is no, the transfer process should begin early.

Do not wait until the patient deteriorates beyond the point of safe transfer.

---

# 29.20 The Four-Question Transfer Test

Before deciding to remain locally, ask:

### 1. What does the patient need?

For example:

- surgery
- interventional radiology
- neurosurgery
- vascular surgery
- paediatric trauma care
- burns care
- critical care

### 2. Can we provide it here?

Not theoretically.

Actually.

### 3. If not, how long until it becomes available?

### 4. Can the patient safely wait?

If the answer to question 4 is no:

> initiate transfer.

---

# 29.21 Early Transfer Beats Late Rescue

A common failure pattern is:

1. patient arrives
2. local team attempts definitive care
3. limitations become apparent
4. patient deteriorates
5. transfer is discussed
6. transfer becomes urgent
7. transport is delayed
8. physiological reserve is exhausted

The better pattern is:

1. recognise capability limitation
2. stabilise immediately reversible threats
3. activate transfer early
4. continue definitive local stabilisation
5. reassess transfer readiness
6. transfer before deterioration becomes irreversible

The leader should think:

> "What will this patient need in the next one to two hours that we cannot provide?"

That question often identifies the transfer requirement earlier.

---

# 29.22 Stabilisation Versus Definitive Treatment

In resource-limited environments, stabilisation and definitive treatment must be distinguished.

Stabilisation aims to:

- maintain oxygenation
- maintain ventilation
- control haemorrhage
- support circulation
- prevent secondary injury
- control contamination where necessary
- prevent hypothermia
- prepare for definitive care

Definitive treatment aims to:

- surgically control bleeding
- repair major vascular injury
- treat intracranial pathology
- provide specialist intervention
- provide definitive burns management
- provide specialist paediatric trauma care

The leader must know when stabilisation has reached its limit.

---

# 29.23 The "Good Enough to Transfer" Concept

The goal before transfer is not necessarily to make the patient completely stable.

Some patients cannot be made fully stable before definitive treatment.

The goal is:

> achieve the safest physiological state realistically possible for transfer without delaying definitive care unnecessarily.

This may mean:

- airway secured
- haemorrhage temporarily controlled
- adequate vascular access
- blood products started
- analgesia provided
- temperature supported
- monitoring established
- receiving team informed

The leader should avoid pursuing low-value optimisation while the definitive intervention is waiting elsewhere.

---

# 29.24 Diagnostic Constraints

Resource-limited environments may have limited imaging.

The leader must avoid two opposite errors.

### Error 1: Diagnostic paralysis

> "We cannot confirm the diagnosis, therefore we cannot act."

### Error 2: Diagnostic overconfidence

> "We do not have imaging, therefore our provisional diagnosis must be correct."

Instead:

> act on the clinical probability of immediately life-threatening conditions while recognising diagnostic uncertainty.

Clinical examination, physiology, mechanism, bedside ultrasound where available, and serial reassessment become increasingly important.

---

# 29.25 The Value of Serial Assessment

When advanced diagnostics are unavailable, time becomes a diagnostic tool.

A patient's trajectory can provide important information.

Ask:

- Is blood pressure improving?
- Is heart rate changing?
- Is mental status changing?
- Is oxygenation improving?
- Is respiratory effort increasing?
- Is perfusion improving?
- Is pain changing?
- Is bleeding continuing?
- Is the response to intervention sustained?

The key question is:

> "Is the patient getting better, worse, or failing to respond?"

Failure to improve is itself information.

---

# 29.26 Clinical Examination Becomes More Important

When diagnostics are limited, structured examination becomes critical.

The leader should repeatedly assess:

### Airway

- patency
- voice
- secretions
- obstruction
- facial injury

### Breathing

- respiratory rate
- work of breathing
- chest movement
- oxygen saturation
- breath sounds
- tracheal position where relevant

### Circulation

- pulse
- blood pressure
- skin perfusion
- capillary refill
- mental status
- external bleeding
- abdominal or pelvic clues

### Disability

- consciousness
- pupils
- glucose
- neurological change

### Exposure

- missed wounds
- bleeding
- burns
- temperature

Serial examination should be deliberate.

---

# 29.27 The Importance of Time

Time is a clinical resource.

In a constrained environment, time may compensate for unavailable technology through:

- repeated examination
- serial physiology
- response-to-treatment assessment
- early consultation
- early transfer
- repeated team review

However:

> Time is only useful if the patient has enough physiological reserve to spend it.

The leader must therefore distinguish between:

- time that provides useful information
- time that merely delays definitive treatment

---

# 29.28 Escalation Is a Resource

Escalation should occur before local resources are exhausted.

Potential escalation targets include:

- senior emergency clinician
- trauma surgeon
- anaesthesia
- intensive care
- blood bank
- specialist centre
- retrieval service
- regional trauma network
- hospital administration

Escalation should be explicit.

For example:

> "This patient has ongoing haemorrhage despite temporary control. We do not have definitive surgical capability here. I am activating transfer now."

---

# 29.29 Do Not Hide Resource Limitations

A dangerous leadership behaviour is attempting to make the system appear more capable than it is.

This can lead to:

- delayed transfer
- false reassurance
- inappropriate intervention
- failure to escalate
- unrealistic expectations

A professional leader should communicate limitations clearly.

For example:

> "We can stabilise the airway and control the bleeding temporarily, but we cannot provide definitive vascular repair here."

That statement is not weakness.

It is clinical clarity.

---

# 29.30 Resource Limitation and Cognitive Bias

Resource constraints can produce predictable biases.

### Normalisation of deviance

Repeatedly functioning with inadequate resources can make unsafe practice feel normal.

### Anchoring

The team may become committed to the first diagnosis because further diagnostic options are unavailable.

### Sunk-cost bias

The team may continue a local strategy because significant effort has already been invested.

### Optimism bias

The team may assume:

> "The patient will probably remain stable until transfer."

### Authority gradient

Junior clinicians may hesitate to challenge an apparently unrealistic plan.

The leader should actively counter these biases.

---

# 29.31 The Leader Should Invite Challenge

A useful phrase is:

> "If anyone thinks our current plan is unsafe, say so now."

This is particularly important when:

- resources are limited
- transfer is delayed
- the diagnosis is uncertain
- the patient is deteriorating
- the team is inexperienced

Psychological safety is a clinical safety intervention.

---

# 29.32 When the Team Is Small

A small team can still function effectively if the leader protects priorities.

The leader should:

1. identify the immediate threat
2. assign essential tasks
3. eliminate non-essential activity
4. reassess continuously
5. escalate early

Do not attempt to reproduce the workflow of a large trauma centre with a small team.

Simplify.

---

# 29.33 Simplification Under Pressure

When resources are limited, reduce unnecessary complexity.

Ask:

> "What absolutely must happen next?"

Then:

> "Who will do it?"

Then:

> "How will we know it is complete?"

This creates a simple control loop.

For example:

> "Control the external haemorrhage."

> "Ahmed, apply the tourniquet."

> "Tell me when it is applied and whether bleeding has stopped."

---

# 29.34 Avoid Parallel Tasks That Compete for the Same Person

In a small team, competing tasks create hidden delays.

For example, asking one clinician to:

- perform ultrasound
- obtain IV access
- call the consultant
- document
- administer medication

is not true parallel processing.

It is task switching.

The leader should recognise this.

A task that is "assigned" but cannot realistically be completed is not an assigned task.

---

# 29.35 Communication Becomes More Structured

Resource-limited environments require concise communication.

Use:

> **Problem → Action → Owner → Time → Escalation trigger**

Example:

> "Ongoing haemorrhage despite pressure. Ahmed, apply the tourniquet now. Tell me when bleeding is controlled. If it remains uncontrolled, we escalate immediately."

This reduces ambiguity.

---

# 29.36 Closed-Loop Communication Is Even More Important

In a constrained environment, the leader cannot afford silent assumptions.

The sequence should remain:

1. instruction
2. acknowledgement
3. task completion
4. confirmation

For example:

> Leader: "Start emergency blood."

> Nurse: "Starting emergency blood."

> Leader: "Tell me when it is running."

> Nurse: "Blood is running."

This creates visibility.

---

# 29.37 The Team Should Know the Resource Ceiling

The team should understand important limitations.

For example:

> "We have limited blood available."

> "CT is unavailable."

> "The operating theatre is occupied."

> "The retrieval team will take approximately two hours."

This shared mental model helps everyone anticipate the next problem.

---

# 29.38 Contingency Planning

Every major limitation should have a contingency.

Ask:

> "If this fails, what will we do?"

Examples:

### Airway

If first airway strategy fails:

> What is the backup?

### Oxygen

If oxygen supply fails:

> What is the alternative?

### Blood

If blood runs out:

> What is the next escalation?

### Transfer

If the ambulance is delayed:

> What is the interim plan?

### Theatre

If theatre becomes unavailable:

> What is the alternative destination?

The leader should define these plans early.

---

# 29.39 The "If–Then" Leadership Model

A simple framework is:

> **If X happens, then we will do Y.**

Examples:

> If the patient becomes hypotensive again, we will reassess for ongoing haemorrhage.

> If airway control deteriorates, we will escalate immediately to the advanced airway plan.

> If transfer is delayed beyond the agreed timeframe, we will escalate to the regional trauma service.

> If blood availability becomes critical, we will activate the next level of support.

This reduces hesitation during deterioration.

---

# 29.40 Resource Allocation During Multiple Casualties

Resource limitation becomes especially important during multiple casualty incidents.

The leader must shift from:

> "What does this patient need?"

to:

> "What does each patient need, and where will limited resources produce the greatest clinical benefit?"

This requires triage.

Resources may include:

- airway expertise
- blood
- theatre capacity
- imaging
- transport
- ICU beds
- senior clinicians

The leader must avoid allowing one patient to consume all available resources when several patients require urgent care.

---

# 29.41 Triage and Leadership

Triage is not abandonment.

It is structured prioritisation.

The leader should consider:

- immediate threat
- likelihood of benefit
- time sensitivity
- available intervention
- resource requirements
- transfer options

In major incidents, the leader should ensure that triage decisions are coordinated with the wider incident command structure.

---

# 29.42 Resource Scarcity and Ethical Decision-Making

Severe scarcity can create ethical tension.

Examples include:

- insufficient blood
- limited ICU beds
- one available operating theatre
- one transport vehicle
- multiple critically injured patients

The trauma leader should not make isolated decisions outside the relevant institutional or major-incident framework.

Instead:

- use established protocols
- involve appropriate senior leadership
- document major decisions
- communicate rationale
- maintain fairness
- avoid arbitrary allocation

The principle is:

> scarce resources require transparent prioritisation.

---

# 29.43 The Leader's Relationship With External Services

When local resources are limited, external communication becomes part of resuscitation.

Potential contacts include:

- tertiary trauma centre
- retrieval service
- specialist surgeon
- anaesthetic service
- blood bank
- paediatric centre
- burns centre
- vascular service
- neurosurgical service

The leader should not delegate the communication without ensuring that the critical information is transferred accurately.

---

# 29.44 What the Receiving Team Needs to Know

A transfer call should answer:

### Who?

Patient identity and age.

### What happened?

Mechanism and relevant context.

### What is happening now?

Current physiology.

### What injuries are suspected or confirmed?

Key findings.

### What has been done?

Interventions and response.

### What remains uncontrolled?

Current threats.

### What do we need?

Specific definitive capability.

### When can the patient arrive?

Estimated transfer timing.

A concise structured handover is safer than a long narrative.

---

# 29.45 The Resource-Limited Handover

A useful structure is:

> **Situation → Threats → Interventions → Limitations → Need → Plan**

Example:

> "Adult male with high-energy blunt trauma. Persistent shock with suspected intra-abdominal haemorrhage. Airway currently controlled and blood products started. We do not have operative capability immediately available. He requires definitive haemorrhage control. Transfer is being arranged now."

The receiving team immediately understands the problem.

---

# 29.46 The Leader Must Maintain Ownership Until Transfer

Calling another hospital does not transfer responsibility.

Until the patient physically leaves the department:

> the current team remains responsible for ongoing care.

The leader must continue:

- reassessment
- haemorrhage control
- airway management
- physiological support
- temperature management
- communication
- documentation
- transfer preparation

Do not mentally hand over the patient before the physical handover occurs.

---

# 29.47 Transport Is a Clinical Procedure

Transfer should be treated like any other high-risk intervention.

Before departure, confirm:

### Patient

- airway
- breathing
- circulation
- neurological status
- pain control
- temperature

### Equipment

- oxygen
- suction
- monitoring
- vascular access supplies
- emergency drugs
- backup equipment

### Team

- appropriately skilled escort
- clear responsibilities
- communication capability

### Destination

- receiving clinician identified
- receiving facility prepared
- estimated arrival communicated

### Contingency

- deterioration plan
- equipment failure plan
- oxygen contingency
- communication backup

---

# 29.48 The Transport Risk Assessment

Before transfer ask:

> "What is most likely to deteriorate during the journey?"

Then:

> "What will we do if it happens?"

Examples:

### Airway deterioration

Backup airway equipment and skilled clinician available.

### Hypotension

Blood or appropriate resuscitation resources available.

### Respiratory deterioration

Oxygen and ventilation capability available.

### Bleeding

Temporary haemorrhage-control measures secure.

### Agitation

Adequate analgesia and sedation strategy.

The transport plan should match the patient's risk.

---

# 29.49 The Leader Should Anticipate Delay

Transfer rarely occurs exactly according to the theoretical timeline.

Potential delays include:

- ambulance availability
- traffic
- weather
- bed availability
- receiving-team delay
- equipment preparation
- imaging
- documentation
- communication failure

Therefore:

> plan for the delayed transfer, not only the ideal transfer.

---

# 29.50 The "Time-to-Capability" Concept

A useful leadership concept is:

> **How long until the patient can access the capability they need?**

This may be more important than distance.

A centre ten kilometres away may be less useful than a centre fifty kilometres away if the second centre can provide definitive treatment immediately.

Therefore consider:

- transport time
- acceptance time
- mobilisation time
- specialist availability
- theatre availability
- intervention readiness

The relevant variable is:

> time to definitive capability.

---

# 29.51 When Local Treatment Is Appropriate

Local treatment may be appropriate when:

- the problem can be definitively managed locally
- the patient is stable enough
- transfer would introduce greater immediate risk
- local expertise is adequate
- required resources are available
- escalation pathways remain available

The decision should be deliberate.

---

# 29.52 When Transfer Should Be Favoured

Transfer should be strongly considered when:

- definitive treatment is unavailable
- specialist expertise is required
- ongoing haemorrhage cannot be controlled
- advanced airway or ventilation support is unavailable
- neurosurgical intervention is required
- vascular surgery is required
- paediatric trauma capability is inadequate
- burns care exceeds local capability
- critical care capacity is inadequate
- the patient's trajectory is worsening

The threshold should be lower when the patient is deteriorating.

---

# 29.53 The Deteriorating Patient in a Constrained Environment

When the patient deteriorates, the leader should avoid repeating the same interventions without reassessment.

Use:

> **Recognise → Reassess → Reprioritise → Escalate**

### Recognise

Something has changed.

### Reassess

Return to cABCDE.

### Reprioritise

Identify the new immediate threat.

### Escalate

Obtain resources or transfer.

This prevents diagnostic inertia.

---

# 29.54 Avoiding the "More of the Same" Trap

A common failure pattern is:

> hypotension → more fluid → hypotension → more fluid → hypotension → more fluid

The correct response is not automatically more of the same.

The leader should ask:

> "Why has the patient not responded?"

Possible explanations include:

- ongoing haemorrhage
- inadequate haemorrhage control
- tension physiology
- cardiac injury
- inadequate resuscitation
- missed injury
- deterioration in another system

Resource limitation increases the importance of this question.

---

# 29.55 The Leader as a Systems Thinker

A trauma team leader must think beyond the immediate patient.

For example:

The patient requires surgery.

But:

- theatre is occupied
- blood is limited
- anaesthesia is managing another emergency
- ICU has no bed
- transfer takes two hours

The clinical problem is therefore also a systems problem.

The leader must coordinate the system around the patient.

---

# 29.56 The Constraint Map

A useful mental model is to identify five domains:

1. **People**
2. **Equipment**
3. **Blood**
4. **Definitive treatment**
5. **Transport**

For each domain ask:

> Available now?

> Available soon?

> Not available?

This rapidly exposes the major constraints.

---

# 29.57 Example Constraint Map

| Domain | Current status | Risk | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| People | Limited | High | Call additional staff |
| Equipment | Adequate | Low | Continue |
| Blood | Limited | High | Escalate blood supply |
| Definitive treatment | Unavailable | Critical | Activate transfer |
| Transport | Delayed | High | Escalate retrieval |

The leader can then prioritise the highest-risk constraint.

---

# 29.58 The Bottleneck Principle

In constrained systems, one bottleneck can determine the entire pathway.

Examples:

- no blood
- no surgeon
- no anaesthesia
- no theatre
- no transport
- no oxygen
- no ICU bed

The leader should identify the bottleneck early.

Ask:

> "What single limitation is most likely to delay definitive care?"

Then act on that limitation.

---

# 29.59 Do Not Waste Scarce Expertise

Senior clinicians are limited resources.

Use them where their expertise changes outcome.

A senior surgeon may be more valuable:

- controlling haemorrhage
- deciding operative strategy
- coordinating transfer

than:

- completing routine documentation
- performing low-complexity tasks
- searching for equipment

The leader should allocate expertise according to consequence.

---

# 29.60 Use Junior Staff Safely

Resource limitation may require junior clinicians to perform important tasks.

This is appropriate when:

- the task is within competence
- supervision is available
- escalation criteria are clear
- the patient is monitored
- the risk is understood

The leader should avoid both extremes:

### Over-delegation

Giving a clinician a task beyond competence.

### Under-delegation

Keeping all tasks with the senior clinician and creating a bottleneck.

The correct approach is:

> task matched to competence, complexity and supervision.

---

# 29.61 When Expertise Is Unavailable

If the required expertise is unavailable locally:

1. recognise the gap
2. use the best available immediate stabilisation
3. contact remote expertise if possible
4. activate transfer
5. document the limitation
6. continue reassessment

Remote consultation can be valuable when direct specialist access is delayed.

---

# 29.62 Telecommunication as a Clinical Resource

In constrained environments, communication technology can function as a clinical resource.

Potential uses include:

- remote specialist consultation
- image sharing where appropriate
- transfer coordination
- advice regarding stabilisation
- retrieval planning

However:

> remote advice does not replace local clinical responsibility.

The leader must integrate external advice into the actual clinical context.

---

# 29.63 Documentation Under Constraint

Documentation remains important.

But during active resuscitation:

> patient care takes priority over perfect documentation.

The leader should ensure that essential information is captured:

- major interventions
- times
- medications
- blood products
- physiological changes
- key decisions
- transfer communication
- receiving clinician

Detailed documentation can be completed when immediate threats are controlled.

---

# 29.64 Resource Limitation and Human Factors

Scarcity increases:

- workload
- interruptions
- frustration
- fatigue
- communication failures
- cognitive overload

The leader should therefore deliberately create structure.

Useful behaviours include:

- clear role allocation
- verbal summaries
- closed-loop communication
- explicit priorities
- regular reassessment
- planned pauses
- early escalation

Structure protects performance.

---

# 29.65 The Micro-Pause

Even in severe resource limitation, the leader should periodically pause for several seconds.

Ask:

> "What is the biggest threat now?"

> "What has changed?"

> "What are we waiting for?"

> "What resource are we running out of?"

> "What happens next?"

This is not wasted time.

It is cognitive control.

---

# 29.66 The Resource Checkpoint

At major transitions, perform a rapid resource checkpoint.

For example:

> "Before we move, confirm oxygen, blood, monitoring, escort, destination and contingency plan."

This prevents predictable transfer failures.

---

# 29.67 Crisis Leadership With Inadequate Staffing

When staffing is inadequate, the leader should immediately identify:

- what must be done
- what can be delayed
- what can be delegated
- what can be stopped
- what requires escalation

Stopping low-value work is often as important as starting high-value work.

---

# 29.68 The Leader Must Be Willing to Stop Tasks

Examples of tasks that may become temporarily secondary:

- non-essential history
- non-urgent documentation
- unnecessary imaging
- low-value procedures
- administrative work

The leader should communicate:

> "Stop non-essential tasks. We are focusing on haemorrhage control and transfer."

This creates alignment.

---

# 29.69 Avoiding Task Saturation

Task saturation occurs when the number of demands exceeds the team's capacity.

Warning signs include:

- repeated instructions
- missed tasks
- unanswered questions
- multiple people doing the same task
- no one monitoring the patient
- unclear priorities
- escalating frustration

The leader should simplify the task list.

---

# 29.70 The Three-Priority Rule

At any moment, the team should know the top three priorities.

For example:

1. control haemorrhage
2. maintain airway
3. activate transfer

If the team cannot state the priorities, the leader should restate them.

---

# 29.71 Resource-Limited Resuscitation Is Still Structured Resuscitation

Even with limited resources, the sequence remains:

> **Recognise → Resuscitate → Reassess → Escalate → Transfer/Definitive care**

The equipment may change.

The structure should not.

---

# 29.72 Common Leadership Errors in Resource-Limited Trauma

### Error 1: Pretending resources are adequate

This delays escalation.

### Error 2: Waiting for deterioration before arranging transfer

This wastes physiological reserve.

### Error 3: Over-investing in diagnosis

The patient needs treatment, not diagnostic certainty.

### Error 4: Failing to define task ownership

Tasks become assumed rather than completed.

### Error 5: Using the most senior person for every task

The leader becomes the bottleneck.

### Error 6: Ignoring transport limitations

The patient may become unstable en route.

### Error 7: Failing to communicate limitations

Receiving teams cannot prepare appropriately.

### Error 8: Normalising unsafe conditions

Repeated scarcity becomes accepted as normal.

### Error 9: Continuing ineffective treatment

Failure to respond is not ignored.

### Error 10: Delaying escalation

The system is asked to solve a problem after its capacity has already been exceeded.

---

# 29.73 A Practical Leadership Algorithm

When resources are constrained:

## Step 1 — Identify the immediate threat

What can kill the patient first?

## Step 2 — Identify the available capability

What can we actually do now?

## Step 3 — Identify the missing capability

What can we not provide?

## Step 4 — Stabilise what is immediately reversible

Do not delay lifesaving interventions.

## Step 5 — Activate additional resources early

Call the people or services required.

## Step 6 — Decide whether definitive care is local or external

Do not wait until the patient is exhausted.

## Step 7 — Define contingencies

If the patient deteriorates, what happens?

## Step 8 — Reassess

Is the patient improving?

## Step 9 — Reassess the system

Are our resources still adequate?

## Step 10 — Transfer or proceed to definitive care

Move decisively once the pathway is ready.

---

# 29.74 The Resource-Limited Trauma Leader's Mental Model

A useful mental model is:

> **Patient + Team + Resources + Time + Destination**

The leader continuously asks:

### Patient

What is happening physiologically?

### Team

Who is available and what can they safely do?

### Resources

What do we have and what are we running out of?

### Time

How long can the patient safely wait?

### Destination

Where can the definitive problem actually be solved?

The interaction between these five domains determines the plan.

---

# 29.75 The "Can We Solve This Here?" Question

One of the most powerful leadership questions is:

> **"Can we solve this problem here?"**

If yes:

> proceed with local definitive management.

If no:

> stabilise and transfer.

If uncertain:

> seek senior/external advice and establish the transfer pathway early.

This question should be asked repeatedly as the patient's condition evolves.

---

# 29.76 The "What Will Kill the Patient Before Help Arrives?" Question

When transfer is delayed, ask:

> "What is most likely to kill the patient before definitive help arrives?"

Then build the interim plan around that threat.

For example:

If haemorrhage is the threat:

- maximise haemorrhage control
- optimise blood availability
- monitor physiology
- prepare for deterioration

If airway failure is the threat:

- secure airway
- prepare backup strategy
- ensure oxygen
- ensure skilled airway support

If neurological deterioration is the threat:

- prevent hypoxia and hypotension
- monitor neurological status
- expedite specialist transfer

---

# 29.77 The "Resource Runway"

A useful concept is the patient's **resource runway**.

This means:

> How long can the current resources sustain safe care?

Examples:

- blood supply may last 20 minutes
- oxygen may last 45 minutes
- ICU capacity may be unavailable
- specialist support may arrive in 60 minutes

The leader should compare:

> **resource runway**

against:

> **time to definitive capability**

If the resource runway is shorter:

> escalation must occur immediately.

---

# 29.78 The Resource Runway Example

A patient has ongoing haemorrhage.

Available blood may last approximately 30 minutes.

Definitive surgery is available at another centre in approximately 90 minutes.

This creates a critical mismatch.

The correct response is not:

> "We have 30 minutes of blood."

The correct response is:

> "Our current resource runway is shorter than the time to definitive control. We need escalation now."

---

# 29.79 When the System Is Overloaded

Overcrowding can create hidden risk.

The leader should identify:

- how many critically ill patients are present
- which patients require immediate attention
- which resources are shared
- which staff are overloaded
- whether additional support can be activated

The leader should communicate with the wider department or incident command structure.

---

# 29.80 Protecting the Resuscitation Area

The trauma leader should protect the resuscitation environment from unnecessary interruptions.

This includes controlling:

- non-essential staff
- unnecessary procedures
- excessive conversation
- unrelated administrative demands
- repeated requests for information

The leader may need to state:

> "Please keep the room clear. We are managing an unstable trauma patient."

Environmental control supports cognitive performance.

---

# 29.81 When Multiple Teams Compete for Resources

If two patients require the same limited resource:

- identify urgency
- assess potential benefit
- use triage principles
- escalate to appropriate senior leadership
- communicate the decision

Do not allow resource competition to become an unstructured argument in the resuscitation room.

---

# 29.82 Leadership During Infrastructure Failure

Infrastructure problems may include:

- power failure
- oxygen failure
- IT failure
- communication failure
- water supply interruption
- equipment network failure

The leader should:

1. protect immediate patient safety
2. activate backup systems
3. communicate the problem
4. identify affected patients
5. escalate
6. maintain clinical priorities

The leader should not become the infrastructure technician.

Assign someone else to coordinate the technical problem where possible.

---

# 29.83 Communication Failure

If normal communication systems fail:

- use alternative communication methods
- identify a communication runner
- maintain face-to-face confirmation
- document key communications
- escalate through available channels

Never assume that an unconfirmed message has been received.

---

# 29.84 The Leader and Institutional Policy

Resource limitation does not eliminate governance.

The leader should know:

- local escalation policies
- transfer pathways
- major haemorrhage protocols
- emergency blood procedures
- major incident procedures
- equipment failure procedures
- critical care escalation pathways

When the system is stressed, familiarity with policy becomes a safety advantage.

---

# 29.85 Training for Resource-Limited Trauma

Teams should practise resource constraints deliberately.

Simulation scenarios can include:

- no CT
- limited blood
- failed suction
- failed monitor
- delayed specialist
- limited staffing
- delayed transfer
- multiple casualties
- oxygen shortage

The objective is not to simulate inconvenience.

It is to develop adaptive leadership.

---

# 29.86 Adaptive Expertise

Adaptive expertise means being able to preserve core principles while changing implementation.

For example:

The principle:

> maintain oxygenation.

The implementation may differ depending on available equipment.

The principle:

> control haemorrhage.

The exact technique may differ depending on resources.

The principle:

> obtain definitive care.

The pathway may involve local treatment or transfer.

Strong trauma leaders preserve the **principle** while adapting the **method**.

---

# 29.87 The Difference Between Adaptation and Unsafe Improvisation

Improvisation is not automatically good.

Safe adaptation:

- preserves core safety principles
- remains within competence
- considers consequences
- includes contingency
- is communicated

Unsafe improvisation:

- ignores established safety principles
- exceeds competence
- creates new hazards
- is not communicated
- becomes permanent practice without review

The leader should ask:

> "Are we adapting safely, or are we simply improvising because we have no plan?"

---

# 29.88 Resource Limitation and Professionalism

Professionalism includes recognising limitations.

A strong leader can say:

> "We cannot safely provide definitive treatment here."

This is not failure.

It demonstrates:

- insight
- accountability
- patient advocacy
- situational awareness

The unsafe leader is the one who refuses to acknowledge the limitation.

---

# 29.89 The Trauma Leader as Patient Advocate

In a constrained environment, the leader must advocate for the patient.

This may require:

- challenging delays
- escalating transfer
- requesting additional blood
- contacting senior clinicians
- requesting retrieval
- challenging inappropriate waiting
- escalating system barriers

The leader should be persistent without becoming disruptive.

---

# 29.90 Escalation Language

Useful language is specific.

Instead of:

> "We need help."

Say:

> "This patient has ongoing haemorrhage despite temporary control. We do not have definitive surgical capability. I need senior surgical review and transfer activation now."

Specific escalation produces specific action.

---

# 29.91 The Safety-Net Statement

When a decision is made to continue local care despite limitations, define the safety net.

For example:

> "We will continue local stabilisation while awaiting transfer. If blood pressure falls despite resuscitation, mental status deteriorates, or bleeding increases, we will escalate immediately."

This converts uncertainty into an active monitoring plan.

---

# 29.92 The Leader's End-of-Resuscitation Review

Before leaving the resuscitation phase, ask:

### Patient

- What injuries remain untreated?
- What remains unstable?
- What could deteriorate next?

### Team

- Does everyone know the plan?
- Are responsibilities clear?

### Resources

- Do we have enough blood?
- oxygen?
- monitoring?
- medication?
- equipment?

### Destination

- Where is definitive care?
- Has it accepted the patient?

### Transfer

- Is the escort appropriate?
- Is equipment ready?
- Is contingency planned?

This prevents premature closure.

---

# 29.93 Practical Scenario 1: No Immediate CT

A patient has major blunt trauma and neurological symptoms.

CT is unavailable.

The patient is clinically deteriorating.

The leader should not allow the absence of CT to create diagnostic paralysis.

Priorities include:

- airway and oxygenation
- avoidance of hypotension
- neurological reassessment
- identification of immediately reversible threats
- senior consultation
- early transfer to a centre with definitive imaging and specialist capability

The absence of CT changes the pathway.

It does not remove the need for decisive action.

---

# 29.94 Practical Scenario 2: Limited Blood

A patient has severe haemorrhage.

Only a limited amount of blood is available.

The leader should:

1. control haemorrhage immediately
2. activate the appropriate blood pathway
3. determine remaining blood availability
4. contact definitive surgical capability
5. assess whether local resources can sustain the patient
6. initiate transfer if required
7. reassess continuously

The key question is:

> "Can our current resources sustain this patient until definitive haemorrhage control?"

---

# 29.95 Practical Scenario 3: No Immediate Surgeon

A patient has suspected major intra-abdominal bleeding.

No surgeon is immediately available.

The patient is deteriorating.

The leader should not wait passively.

Actions include:

- haemorrhage control measures where possible
- resuscitation
- senior escalation
- specialist consultation
- transfer activation
- communication with receiving centre
- preparation for transport

The absence of a surgeon is itself a reason to escalate.

---

# 29.96 Practical Scenario 4: Small Rural Team

A rural emergency department has:

- one doctor
- two nurses
- limited blood
- no CT
- no surgery
- long transfer distance

The leader should simplify the plan.

The priorities become:

1. immediate life threats
2. stabilisation
3. early transfer activation
4. resource preservation
5. structured reassessment
6. safe transport preparation

The team should not attempt to replicate a tertiary trauma centre.

It should deliver the highest-value care it can provide while moving the patient toward definitive capability.

---

# 29.97 Practical Scenario 5: Multiple Casualties

Three major trauma patients arrive simultaneously.

Resources include:

- one senior doctor
- limited nursing staff
- one operating theatre
- limited blood

The leader must shift into a triage mindset.

The team should establish:

- immediate threats
- resource requirements
- likely benefit
- definitive treatment needs
- transfer options

The leader must coordinate with the wider incident structure rather than allowing the first patient encountered to consume all resources.

---

# 29.98 Practical Scenario 6: Transfer Delayed

A critically injured patient has been accepted by a tertiary centre.

The retrieval ambulance is delayed.

The leader should ask:

> "What is the patient's resource runway?"

Then:

- continue definitive local stabilisation
- reassess physiology
- protect airway and breathing
- maintain haemorrhage control
- monitor blood availability
- escalate delay
- prepare for deterioration

The transfer is not complete simply because the receiving centre has accepted the patient.

---

# 29.99 Practical Scenario 7: Equipment Failure

During resuscitation, suction fails.

The leader should:

- identify immediate patient risk
- obtain backup suction
- maintain manual alternatives where appropriate
- assign equipment troubleshooting to another person
- continue monitoring
- reassess airway risk

The leader should avoid allowing the entire team to focus on the equipment.

---

# 29.100 Practical Scenario 8: Limited ICU Capacity

A patient requires critical care but no ICU bed is immediately available.

The leader should:

- identify immediate stabilisation requirements
- escalate to critical care leadership
- determine alternative monitored location if appropriate
- initiate transfer if required
- continue close reassessment
- document the escalation and plan

The absence of an ICU bed does not remove the clinical need.

It creates a systems problem requiring escalation.

---

# 29.101 Practical Scenario 9: Paediatric Trauma With Limited Expertise

A severely injured child presents to an adult-focused facility.

The leader should recognise the expertise gap early.

Immediate priorities:

- stabilise life threats
- use available paediatric expertise
- contact specialist paediatric trauma support
- activate transfer
- ensure appropriate equipment and medication
- prepare safe transport

The leader should not delay escalation because the child is temporarily stable.

---

# 29.102 Practical Scenario 10: Major Incident With Scarce Resources

During a major incident:

- multiple casualties
- limited blood
- limited theatre capacity
- limited ambulances

The leader must work within the incident command system.

The key principles are:

- triage
- prioritisation
- resource allocation
- communication
- escalation
- documentation
- reassessment

The leader should avoid making isolated decisions outside the wider operational structure.

---

# 29.104 The One-Minute Resource Brief

When the team is under pressure, the leader can use a rapid resource brief:

> **"Our patient has [problem]. We have [critical resources]. We do not have [critical limitation]. Our immediate priorities are [1], [2], and [3]. [Person] will manage [task]. [Person] will activate [resource]. If [deterioration] occurs, we will [contingency]. Our definitive destination is [destination]."**

This creates a shared operational picture.

---

# 29.105 The One-Minute Transfer Brief

Before transfer:

> **"This is a [patient] with [mechanism/injury]. Current major threats are [threats]. We have performed [interventions]. The patient remains [physiological status]. Our major limitation is [limitation]. Definitive treatment required is [treatment]. Transfer is occurring because [reason]. During transfer we are specifically concerned about [risk]."**

This is concise and clinically meaningful.

---

# 29.106 Leadership Principles to Remember

When resources are limited:

1. **Know what you actually have.**
2. **Know what you cannot provide.**
3. **Prioritise life threats.**
4. **Control haemorrhage early.**
5. **Protect oxygenation and ventilation.**
6. **Use serial assessment.**
7. **Delegate according to competence.**
8. **Avoid making the leader the bottleneck.**
9. **Identify the system bottleneck early.**
10. **Escalate before resources are exhausted.**
11. **Think in terms of time to definitive capability.**
12. **Treat transfer as a clinical intervention.**
13. **Plan for delay, not the ideal timeline.**
14. **Define contingencies.**
15. **Communicate limitations honestly.**
16. **Maintain ownership until physical transfer.**
17. **Protect the team's cognitive capacity.**
18. **Simplify when overwhelmed.**
19. **Use structured communication.**
20. **Adapt safely without abandoning core principles.**

---

# 29.107 Final Leadership Principle

The best trauma leader is not the person who can create a perfect trauma system from imperfect resources.

The best trauma leader is the person who can recognise the limitations of the system, protect the patient from those limitations, mobilise the resources that are available, and move the patient toward definitive care before the window of opportunity closes.

Resource limitation changes the method.

It does not change the responsibility.

> **Know your capability.**
>
> **Know your limitations.**
>
> **Control what you can.**
>
> **Escalate what you cannot.**
>
> **Transfer before the patient runs out of physiological reserve.**

The safest trauma leader does not ask:

> "How can we make this system look adequate?"

The leader asks:

> **"What does this patient need, what can we provide now, what can we obtain next, and what must happen before time runs out?"**

That is adaptive trauma leadership.

That is safe leadership under constraint.
