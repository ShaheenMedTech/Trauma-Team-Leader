# Chapter 7 — Team Briefing Before Patient Arrival

## The Trauma Team Leader's Pre-arrival Briefing

> A trauma resuscitation should not begin when the patient arrives.
> It should begin when the team receives the pre-arrival information.

---

# 1. Clinical Scenario

A major trauma patient is being transported to the emergency department.

The pre-hospital team reports:

- Adult patient
- High-speed road traffic collision
- Significant vehicle damage
- Suspected multiple injuries
- Hypotension in the field
- Reduced level of consciousness
- Ongoing external haemorrhage
- Estimated arrival in 8 minutes

The trauma team assembles in the resuscitation room.

Several people begin preparing equipment simultaneously.

The airway clinician asks what airway strategy is expected.

The nursing team asks whether blood products should be prepared.

Radiology asks whether CT is likely to be required.

The surgical team is uncertain who will perform the initial assessment.

No one has clearly identified who is leading the resuscitation.

The patient arrives.

The room becomes busy immediately.

Everyone is working.

But not everyone is working toward the same plan.

This is exactly the situation that a structured pre-arrival briefing is designed to prevent.

---

# 2. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain the purpose of a trauma team pre-arrival briefing.
2. Identify the essential information that should be shared before patient arrival.
3. Allocate team roles clearly.
4. Anticipate likely clinical problems before the patient enters the resuscitation room.
5. Prepare equipment, blood products, medications and investigations appropriately.
6. Establish an initial airway, breathing and circulation strategy.
7. Develop contingency plans for anticipated deterioration.
8. Communicate using clear and closed-loop communication principles.
9. Maintain a shared mental model within the trauma team.
10. Lead a concise and effective trauma team briefing.

---

# 3. Why the Pre-arrival Briefing Matters

The period immediately before patient arrival is one of the most valuable opportunities for the Trauma Team Leader to influence the quality of the resuscitation.

Once the patient arrives, attention is rapidly consumed by:

- Airway assessment
- Breathing assessment
- Haemorrhage control
- Monitoring
- Vascular access
- Blood sampling
- Procedures
- Imaging
- Communication with multiple teams
- Reassessment

There may be little time to clarify responsibilities or develop a strategy.

The pre-arrival briefing creates that opportunity.

It allows the team to establish:

> **Who is doing what, what we expect to find, what we are going to do first, and what we will do if the patient deteriorates.**

A good briefing therefore creates a shared mental model before the first critical decision is required.

---

# 4. The Trauma Team Leader's Objective

The purpose of the briefing is not to give a long lecture.

It is to prepare the team for the first minutes of resuscitation.

The Trauma Team Leader should aim to establish five things:

### 1. Situation

What is happening?

### 2. Threats

What are we most worried about?

### 3. Roles

Who is responsible for each task?

### 4. Plan

What are we going to do initially?

### 5. Contingencies

What will we do if the patient is worse than expected?

A useful mental model is:

> **Situation → Threats → Roles → Plan → Contingencies**

---

# 5. Information Available Before Arrival

The quality of the briefing depends on the quality of the information received.

The Trauma Team Leader should rapidly identify:

## Mechanism

- Road traffic collision
- Motorcycle collision
- Pedestrian injury
- Fall from height
- Crush injury
- Penetrating trauma
- Blast injury
- Industrial injury
- Sporting injury
- Assault

## Patient Factors

- Age
- Sex
- Pregnancy status when relevant
- Major comorbidities if known
- Anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy if known

## Physiological Status

Ask for the latest:

- Heart rate
- Blood pressure
- Respiratory rate
- Oxygen saturation
- Glasgow Coma Scale
- Temperature when relevant

More importantly, ask:

> **Are the observations improving, stable or deteriorating?**

A single blood pressure reading is less informative than the trajectory.

---

# 6. Mechanism Is a Predictor, Not a Diagnosis

The mechanism should influence preparation but should not determine the diagnosis.

For example:

A high-speed road traffic collision may raise concern for:

- Major haemorrhage
- Thoracic injury
- Traumatic brain injury
- Cervical spine injury
- Pelvic injury
- Long-bone injury

But the team must remain prepared to identify unexpected injuries.

The Trauma Team Leader should therefore avoid statements such as:

> "This is a chest trauma."

Instead:

> "The mechanism makes significant thoracic injury possible. We will assess and treat immediately according to our primary survey."

---

# 7. Establish the Team Structure

Before arrival, every important role should have an identifiable person.

Depending on local staffing and resources, the team may include:

- Trauma Team Leader
- Airway clinician
- Primary survey clinician
- Procedure clinician
- Nurses
- Healthcare assistants
- Scribe
- Radiographer
- Emergency department support staff
- Surgical team
- Anaesthetic team
- Orthopaedic team
- Neurosurgical team
- Blood bank support
- Other specialty teams as required

The exact composition varies between institutions.

The principle does not:

> **Every critical task should have a clearly identified owner.**

---

# 8. Identify the Trauma Team Leader

The team should know who is leading before the patient arrives.

The leader should be explicitly identified.

For example:

> "I will be the Trauma Team Leader for this resuscitation."

This simple statement prevents ambiguity.

If another clinician is leading:

> "Dr Smith will lead the resuscitation. I will manage the airway."

Leadership should be visible and unambiguous.

---

# 9. Role Allocation

Role allocation should occur before the patient arrives whenever possible.

A simple briefing may include:

> "Airway: Dr Ahmed."

> "Primary survey: Dr Sara."

> "Circulation and vascular access: Nurse A and Nurse B."

> "Procedure support: Dr Ali."

> "Scribe: Nurse C."

> "I will lead and coordinate the resuscitation."

The team should not need to determine these roles while simultaneously treating a critically injured patient.

---

# 10. The Airway Plan

The airway clinician should know what information is available and what problems are anticipated.

The briefing should consider:

- Current GCS
- Facial trauma
- Neck injury
- Airway contamination
- Burns
- Inhalational injury
- Vomiting
- Hypoxia
- Expected difficult airway
- Need for cervical spine precautions
- Potential need for advanced airway management

The leader should ask:

> "Do you anticipate any airway difficulty?"

If the answer is yes, the team should identify:

- Primary airway strategy
- Backup strategy
- Required equipment
- Additional expertise
- Location for airway management
- Plan if intubation fails

The exact airway technique depends on the clinical situation, expertise and local protocol.

---

# 11. Cervical Spine Considerations

The team should not automatically assume that every trauma patient requires the same immobilisation strategy.

Instead, the mechanism, clinical findings and applicable trauma guidance should be considered.

If cervical spine injury is suspected, the team should establish:

- Who will maintain appropriate spinal precautions
- How airway management will be performed
- Whether manual in-line stabilisation or another strategy is appropriate
- How the patient will be moved
- Whether imaging is anticipated

The goal is coordinated spinal protection without allowing immobilisation concerns to distract from life-threatening problems.

---

# 12. Breathing Plan

The team should anticipate possible thoracic life threats.

Depending on the mechanism, consider:

- Tension pneumothorax
- Open pneumothorax
- Massive haemothorax
- Flail chest
- Pulmonary contusion
- Airway injury
- Diaphragmatic injury

The leader should establish:

> "If there is evidence of an immediately life-threatening chest injury, we will treat it during the primary survey."

The team should know where the relevant equipment is located.

---

# 13. Circulation and Haemorrhage Plan

Major trauma should be approached with early recognition of haemorrhage.

Before arrival, consider potential bleeding sources:

- External haemorrhage
- Chest
- Abdomen
- Pelvis
- Retroperitoneum
- Long bones
- Soft tissues

The briefing should establish:

- Vascular access strategy
- Blood sampling
- Blood product availability
- Haemorrhage control equipment
- Pelvic binder availability when indicated
- Tourniquets
- Pressure dressings
- Rapid infusion equipment
- Calcium availability when relevant
- Tranexamic acid according to applicable protocol and timing
- Massive haemorrhage pathway activation if indicated

---

# 14. Blood Product Preparation

If major haemorrhage is suspected, preparation should begin before the patient arrives.

Depending on local protocol, this may include:

- Emergency-release blood
- Plasma
- Platelets
- Cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen replacement
- Major haemorrhage pack
- Blood warmer
- Rapid infuser

The Trauma Team Leader should establish:

> "Is blood available?"

and:

> "Has the massive haemorrhage protocol been activated if required?"

The precise transfusion strategy should follow the institution's major haemorrhage protocol and current evidence.

---

# 15. External Haemorrhage Preparation

External haemorrhage should be anticipated whenever the mechanism suggests it.

The team should have immediate access to:

- Tourniquets
- Haemostatic dressings
- Pressure dressings
- Gauze
- Pelvic binder when indicated
- Appropriate wound packing equipment

The principle is:

> **Prepare the equipment before it is needed.**

---

# 16. Disability and Neurological Plan

If traumatic brain injury is suspected, the briefing should identify:

- Current GCS
- Pupillary findings if known
- Seizure activity
- Anticoagulant use if known
- Evidence of deterioration
- Need for airway protection
- Need for urgent imaging

The team should remember that neurological deterioration may reflect:

- Intracranial injury
- Hypoxia
- Hypotension
- Hypercapnia
- Hypoglycaemia
- Seizure
- Intoxication
- Other medical causes

The primary survey remains the priority.

---

# 17. Exposure and Environmental Control

The patient may need complete exposure to identify injuries.

Before arrival, ensure:

- Adequate lighting
- Appropriate clothing removal equipment
- Warm blankets
- Active warming equipment when appropriate
- Temperature monitoring
- Privacy considerations

The team should remember:

> **Exposure without temperature control can worsen trauma physiology.**

Hypothermia is particularly concerning in patients with major haemorrhage.

---

# 18. Anticipate the Need for Imaging

Imaging should be guided by the patient's physiology and clinical findings.

Before arrival, the team should consider:

- Portable chest radiography
- Pelvic radiography when indicated
- Ultrasound
- CT
- Angiography or interventional radiology

The leader should avoid making imaging the default next step.

The correct question is:

> **What information do we need, and is the patient physiologically stable enough to obtain it?**

---

# 19. CT Planning

If CT is likely to be required, preparation should consider:

- Patient stability
- Airway status
- Ventilation
- Monitoring
- Intravenous access
- Contrast requirements
- Renal function when relevant
- Need for escort
- Availability of the CT scanner
- Ability to continue resuscitation during transfer

A patient who requires immediate life-saving intervention should not be delayed unnecessarily for imaging.

---

# 20. Operating Theatre and Interventional Radiology

For patients with suspected major haemorrhage, the team should anticipate definitive haemorrhage control.

Potential destinations may include:

- Operating theatre
- Interventional radiology
- Hybrid theatre
- Angioembolisation pathway

The Trauma Team Leader should ask:

> "If the patient remains unstable despite initial resuscitation, where is definitive haemorrhage control going to occur?"

This question should be considered before the patient arrives.

---

# 21. Medication Preparation

Depending on the case, relevant medications may include:

- Analgesia
- Sedation
- Induction agents
- Neuromuscular blockade
- Vasopressors when indicated
- Anticonvulsants when indicated
- Antibiotics when indicated
- Reversal agents when appropriate
- Tranexamic acid when indicated

The exact medications and doses depend on the patient, clinical circumstances, local protocols and clinician expertise.

The briefing should focus on preparation rather than premature treatment decisions.

---

- Airway adjuncts

- Endotracheal tubes
- Difficult-airway equipment
- Capnography

## Breathing

- Chest decompression equipment
- Chest drain equipment
- Occlusive dressings
- Ventilation equipment

## Circulation

- Intravenous cannulas
- Intraosseous access equipment
- Blood sampling tubes
- Pressure bags
- Rapid infuser
- Blood warmer
- Haemorrhage control equipment

## Monitoring

- ECG
- Blood pressure
- Pulse oximetry
- Temperature
- Capnography where appropriate

# 23. Establish the Resuscitation Room Environment

The room should be prepared for the anticipated level of care.

Consider:

- Adequate space
- Working monitors
- Oxygen supply
- Suction
- Procedure lighting
- Ultrasound
- Blood products
- Warming equipment
- Imaging equipment
- Documentation system

A useful principle is:

> **If an item is likely to be needed in the first five minutes, it should be immediately accessible.**

---

# 24. The Scribe

The scribe has a critical role.

The scribe should document:

- Arrival time
- Vital signs
- GCS
- Interventions
- Medications
- Blood products
- Procedures
- Imaging
- Clinical response
- Important decisions
- Time-critical events

The scribe should not be expected to remember events retrospectively.

The record should be contemporaneous whenever possible.

---

# 25. Communication With the Pre-hospital Team

The Trauma Team Leader should identify who will receive the handover.

The team should avoid multiple people asking questions simultaneously.

A useful approach is:

> One clinician receives the handover while the rest of the team listens and prepares.

The leader should ensure that the handover includes:

- Mechanism
- Injuries suspected
- Signs and symptoms
- Vital signs
- Interventions
- Response to treatment
- Medications
- Blood loss
- Relevant medical history
- Estimated time of arrival

The exact handover framework may vary by system.

---

# 26. Anticipate Deterioration

A strong briefing includes contingency planning.

The leader should ask:

> "What are we most worried about?"

and:

> "What will we do if the patient arrives worse than expected?"

Examples:

### If the patient becomes profoundly hypotensive

Consider:

- Immediate haemorrhage control
- Blood products
- Major haemorrhage protocol
- Identification of bleeding source
- Surgical or interventional haemorrhage control

### If the patient becomes hypoxic

Consider:

- Airway obstruction
- Pneumothorax
- Haemothorax
- Pulmonary injury
- Equipment failure

### If the patient deteriorates neurologically

Consider:

- Hypoxia
- Hypotension
- Intracranial injury
- Seizure
- Hypoglycaemia
- Other reversible causes

---

# 27. The Shared Mental Model

One of the most important outcomes of the briefing is the creation of a shared mental model.

Every member should understand:

- What is happening
- What is expected
- What the priorities are
- What their role is
- What the likely next steps are

Without a shared mental model, different clinicians may act on different assumptions.

For example:

The surgeon may expect immediate transfer to theatre.

The emergency physician may expect CT.

The anaesthetist may expect immediate intubation.

The nursing team may be preparing for a major haemorrhage.

All may be reasonable.

But the team must agree on the sequence.

---

# 28. Sequence Matters

The Trauma Team Leader should establish the likely sequence of priorities.

A typical sequence may be:

1. Receive handover.
2. Confirm immediate threats.
3. Perform the primary survey.
4. Treat life-threatening problems immediately.
5. Establish haemorrhage control and resuscitation.
6. Reassess response.
7. Decide whether further imaging is appropriate.
8. Determine definitive destination.
9. Continue reassessment.

The exact sequence must remain dynamic.

The patient may force the team to change the plan.

---

# 29. The Briefing Should Not Become a Rigid Script

A briefing is a framework, not a ritual.

The leader should avoid spending several minutes discussing low-probability details while a critically unstable patient is approaching.

Priorities are:

> **Critical information first.**

A short briefing that establishes the essential plan is better than a detailed briefing that delays preparation.

---

# 30. The 60-Second Briefing

For many trauma activations, a concise briefing may be enough.

Example:

> "We have a 35-year-old male after a high-speed road traffic collision, approximately five minutes away. Pre-hospital observations show tachycardia and hypotension with reduced GCS and suspected pelvic injury. Major haemorrhage is a concern."

> "I will lead. Dr Ahmed will manage the airway. Dr Sara will perform the primary survey. Nurses A and B will establish access, obtain bloods and prepare blood products. Nurse C will document."

> "Please have suction, difficult-airway equipment, chest decompression equipment, haemorrhage-control equipment and the pelvic binder immediately available."

> "We will assess and treat using the primary survey. If he remains unstable despite initial haemorrhage control and resuscitation, we will activate the major haemorrhage pathway and move toward definitive haemorrhage control."

> "Any concerns before arrival?"

This briefing is short, structured and actionable.

---

# 31. The 30-Second Version

When time is extremely limited:

> "High-speed MVC, hypotensive, reduced GCS, suspected major haemorrhage. I am leading. Airway: Dr Ahmed. Primary survey: Dr Sara. Nursing team: access, bloods and haemorrhage preparation. Have airway, chest and haemorrhage equipment ready. We will treat immediate threats on arrival and reassess continuously. Any immediate concerns?"

---

# 32. Closed-Loop Communication Before Arrival

Important instructions should be acknowledged.

For example:

Leader:

> "Please prepare the major haemorrhage equipment."

Nurse:

> "Major haemorrhage equipment prepared."

Leader:

> "Thank you."

This confirms that the instruction was heard and completed.

---

# 33. Speaking Up

Every member of the trauma team should be able to raise a concern.

The leader should actively encourage this.

For example:

> "If you see something that concerns you, say it immediately."

This is particularly important when:

- The patient's condition changes
- A procedure is delayed
- Equipment is unavailable
- The planned strategy appears unsafe
- A team member notices an unexpected finding

The leader should create an environment in which speaking up is expected rather than discouraged.

---

# 34. Psychological Safety

A high-performing trauma team should allow members to communicate concerns without fear of humiliation or retaliation.

The leader can model this behaviour by saying:

> "If anyone disagrees with the plan or sees a safety issue, speak up."

This is not a sign of weak leadership.

It is a component of safe leadership.

---

# 35. Briefing the Team About Expected Problems

The leader should identify the most likely threats without anchoring the team to a single diagnosis.

For example:

> "The mechanism suggests possible pelvic and thoracic injury. However, we will not assume the source of shock until we assess the patient."

This creates vigilance without premature closure.

---

# 36. Avoiding Anchoring

Pre-arrival information is often incomplete.

The team may receive a statement such as:

> "Likely pelvic fracture."

The leader should not allow this statement to dominate the assessment.

Instead:

> "Pelvic bleeding is a concern. We will also actively assess the chest, abdomen, external bleeding and long bones."

This maintains diagnostic breadth.

---

# 37. Anticipating Special Circumstances

The briefing should change according to the patient.

## Paediatric trauma

Consider:

- Weight
- Equipment sizes
- Drug dosing
- Vascular access
- Temperature control
- Specialist paediatric support

## Pregnancy

Consider:

- Gestational age
- Maternal resuscitation priorities
- Obstetric involvement
- Fetal assessment when appropriate
- Positioning considerations
- Rh status and haemorrhage management according to local protocols

## Burns

Consider:

- Airway oedema
- Inhalational injury
- Burn size
- Temperature control
- Fluid strategy
- Specialist burns referral

## Penetrating trauma

Consider:

- Immediate haemorrhage control
- Chest injury
- Abdominal injury
- Vascular injury
- Need for urgent operative management

---

# 38. The Trauma Team Leader Should Think Ahead

The leader should be approximately one step ahead of the team.

While the team is preparing the patient for arrival, the leader should already be considering:

> "What will we do if the first intervention does not work?"

For example:

- What if the airway cannot be secured?
- What if the patient remains hypotensive?
- What if there is no peripheral IV access?
- What if ultrasound is positive?
- What if the patient deteriorates during CT?
- What if the operating theatre is unavailable?

This is anticipatory leadership.

---

# 39. Contingency Planning

A useful structure is:

### Plan A

What we expect to do.

### Plan B

What we will do if Plan A fails.

### Escalation

Who will be called if additional expertise is required?

### Destination

Where will definitive care occur?

This structure can be applied to:

- Airway
- Haemorrhage
- Imaging
- Operative management
- Transfer

---

# 40. Example: Difficult Airway

### Plan A

Prepare for standard advanced airway management.

### Plan B

Use an alternative airway strategy according to the difficult-airway plan.

### Escalation

Call an experienced airway clinician early.

### Rescue

Follow the applicable difficult-airway algorithm if oxygenation or ventilation cannot be maintained.

The team should know the plan before the first attempt.

---

# 41. Example: Major Haemorrhage

### Plan A

Immediate haemorrhage control and blood-product resuscitation.

### Plan B

Escalate to definitive operative or interventional haemorrhage control.

### Escalation

Activate relevant surgical, anaesthetic, interventional and transfusion support.

### Destination

Proceed directly toward definitive haemorrhage control when clinically indicated.

---

# 42. The Importance of Time

The leader should identify time-critical interventions.

Examples include:

- Control of catastrophic external haemorrhage
- Treatment of life-threatening airway obstruction
- Treatment of tension pneumothorax
- Control of major haemorrhage
- Prevention of severe hypoxia
- Prevention of severe hypothermia

The team should understand that not every intervention can wait for a complete diagnosis.

---

# 43. Preparation of the Resuscitation Room

The physical environment influences performance.

The leader should ensure:

- Clear access to the patient
- Equipment arranged logically
- Monitoring visible
- Suction functioning
- Oxygen available
- Procedures can be performed without obstruction
- Blood products can reach the bedside rapidly
- Appropriate imaging equipment is accessible

A cluttered room can become a clinical hazard.

---

# 44. Human Factors During Preparation

The pre-arrival period can still contain human-factor hazards.

Common problems include:

- Assumptions
- Poor role clarity
- Information overload
- Noise
- Multiple simultaneous conversations
- Confirmation bias
- Failure to escalate
- Equipment unfamiliarity

The leader should simplify communication.

---

# 45. The Leader Should Not Perform Every Task

One of the most important principles of trauma leadership is delegation.

The leader should not:

- Prepare every piece of equipment
- Perform every procedure
- Write every instruction
- Obtain every piece of information

Instead:

> **The leader coordinates the system.**

The leader should preserve cognitive capacity for:

- Prioritisation
- Reassessment
- Decision-making
- Anticipation
- Communication

---

# 46. Cognitive Bandwidth

The leader's attention is a limited resource.

If the leader becomes deeply involved in a technical procedure, situational awareness may deteriorate.

This does not mean that the leader can never perform a procedure.

It means that the leader should recognise when doing so compromises leadership.

A useful question is:

> "Can someone else safely perform this task?"

If yes, delegate.

---

# 47. Briefing the Team About Leadership Behaviour

The leader can establish expectations before arrival:

> "I will coordinate the resuscitation. Please tell me immediately about significant changes."

> "I may ask for confirmation of important instructions."

> "If you think we are missing something, speak up."

These statements establish a team culture of active communication.

# 49. A Practical Pre-arrival Briefing Template

The Trauma Team Leader can use the following structure:

## Situation

> "We are receiving a [age]-year-old [sex] following [mechanism]."

## Current physiology

> "Current observations are [key findings], with a trend of [improving/stable/deteriorating]."

## Major concerns

> "Our main concerns are [airway/respiratory/haemorrhage/neurological/other]."

## Roles

> "I will lead. Airway is [name]. Primary survey is [name]. Nursing roles are [names]. Scribe is [name]."

## Preparation

> "Please prepare [critical equipment/blood/imaging]."

## Initial plan

> "We will perform a structured primary survey and treat life-threatening problems immediately."

## Contingency

> "If the patient remains unstable, we will [escalation plan]."

## Destination

> "If definitive intervention is required, our anticipated destination is [theatre/IR/other]."

## Safety check

> "Does anyone have any concerns before the patient arrives?"

---

# 50. When the Briefing Should Change

The briefing is not a one-time event.

If new information arrives, the leader should update the team.

For example:

Initial information:

> "Hypotensive after road traffic collision."

New information:

> "Pre-hospital team reports ongoing external arterial bleeding from the lower limb."

The leader should immediately update the plan:

> "New information: significant external limb haemorrhage. Please have tourniquet and haemostatic dressing ready. We will prioritise haemorrhage control immediately on arrival."

---

# 51. Re-briefing After Arrival

After the initial primary survey, the leader may need to briefly re-orientate the team.

For example:

> "We have controlled the external haemorrhage. The patient remains hypotensive. Chest examination is concerning for a left-sided injury. We need immediate chest intervention and continued blood-product resuscitation."

This maintains the shared mental model.

---

# 52. Common Pitfalls

## Pitfall 1 — No identified leader

Everyone assumes someone else is leading.

### Correction

Explicitly identify the leader.

---

## Pitfall 2 — Roles are unclear

Multiple clinicians attempt the same task while another critical task is neglected.

### Correction

Assign clear roles before arrival.

---

## Pitfall 3 — Overly long briefing

The team spends several minutes discussing unlikely possibilities.

### Correction

Focus on information that changes immediate preparation or management.

---

## Pitfall 4 — Anchoring on the pre-hospital diagnosis

The team assumes the suspected injury is the confirmed diagnosis.

### Correction

Use pre-arrival information to anticipate threats, not to replace assessment.

---

## Pitfall 5 — No contingency plan

The team knows what to do if everything goes normally but not if the patient deteriorates.

### Correction

Ask:

> "What will we do if the patient is worse than expected?"

---

## Pitfall 6 — Equipment is assumed to be available

A critical device is discovered to be missing or non-functional.

### Correction

Check essential equipment before arrival.

---

## Pitfall 7 — Poor communication

Several clinicians talk simultaneously during handover.

### Correction

Nominate one person to receive the handover while the team listens.

---

## Pitfall 8 — Leader becomes task-focused

The leader becomes occupied with a procedure and loses situational awareness.

### Correction

Delegate whenever appropriate.

---

## Pitfall 9 — Failure to speak up

Team members notice a problem but remain silent.

### Correction

Explicitly invite concerns.

---

# 53. Case-Based Discussion

## Case

A 42-year-old patient is five minutes away after a motorcycle collision.

The pre-hospital team reports:

- GCS 12
- Heart rate 132/min
- Systolic blood pressure 78 mmHg
- Oxygen saturation 91%
- Suspected pelvic injury
- Suspected left chest injury
- Ongoing blood loss from the leg

The patient has received initial haemorrhage-control measures.

---

## Question 1

What should the Trauma Team Leader do first?

### Answer

Establish the team structure and identify the immediate priorities.

The briefing should recognise:

- Potential airway compromise
- Respiratory compromise
- Major haemorrhage
- Possible pelvic injury
- Significant limb haemorrhage

---

## Question 2

What equipment should be prepared?

### Answer

At minimum:

- Airway equipment
- Suction
- Oxygen
- Capnography
- Chest decompression and drainage equipment
- Large-bore vascular access equipment
- Intraosseous equipment
- Blood sampling equipment
- Haemorrhage-control equipment
- Pelvic binder if indicated
- Blood products according to local major haemorrhage protocol
- Appropriate monitoring

---

## Question 3

Should CT be the immediate destination?

### Answer

Not automatically.

The patient is profoundly physiologically abnormal.

The team should first identify and treat immediate life threats and determine whether the patient is stable enough for imaging.

If definitive haemorrhage control is required, the appropriate destination may be the operating theatre, interventional radiology or another definitive treatment pathway depending on the source of bleeding and local resources.

---

# 54. Case-Based Leadership Analysis

The important issue is not simply what injuries the patient has.

The leadership challenge is:

> **Can the team recognise the threats, assign responsibilities and move rapidly toward definitive control?**

The leader should maintain awareness of:

- Airway
- Oxygenation
- Ventilation
- Haemorrhage
- Neurological status
- Response to treatment
- Need for definitive intervention

The leader should continuously update the team's mental model.

---

# 55. The First Minute After Arrival

The pre-arrival briefing should prepare the team for the transition from planning to action.

On arrival:

1. Receive structured handover.
2. Confirm patient identity where possible.
3. Connect monitoring.
4. Begin the primary survey.
5. Treat life-threatening findings immediately.
6. Communicate significant findings aloud.
7. Reassess after interventions.
8. Update the plan.

The briefing ends when the patient arrives.

Leadership does not.

---

# 56. The Trauma Team Leader's Cognitive Model

Before arrival, the leader should be thinking in three parallel domains:

### What do I know?

Facts from the pre-hospital team.

### What do I expect?

Likely injuries based on mechanism and physiology.

### What must I not miss?

Life-threatening diagnoses that require immediate assessment and treatment.

This prevents the leader from confusing expectation with diagnosis.

---

# 57. The Golden Rule of the Pre-arrival Briefing

The purpose of the briefing is not to predict the exact injuries.

It is to make the team ready for the important possibilities.

> **Prepare for what you expect, but remain ready for what you did not expect.**

---

# 58. Key Takeaways

1. Trauma resuscitation begins before the patient arrives.
2. The Trauma Team Leader should conduct a concise pre-arrival briefing.
3. The leader must be explicitly identified.
4. Critical roles should be allocated before arrival.
5. The team should anticipate the most important immediate threats.
6. Airway, breathing and haemorrhage plans should be considered in advance.
7. Essential equipment should be checked and immediately accessible.
8. Blood products and major haemorrhage resources should be prepared when indicated.
9. Imaging and definitive destination should be considered early.
10. A contingency plan should exist for deterioration.
11. The briefing should create a shared mental model.
12. Team members should be encouraged to speak up.
13. The leader should delegate rather than perform every task.
14. Pre-arrival information should guide preparation without causing diagnostic anchoring.
15. The briefing should be concise, structured and clinically useful.
16. The plan must remain dynamic once the patient arrives.
