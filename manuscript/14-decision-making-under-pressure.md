# Chapter 14 — Decision-Making Under Pressure

## Decision-Making Under Pressure

> In major trauma, the leader rarely has the luxury of complete information.
> The task is not to eliminate uncertainty. The task is to make the safest
> decision possible with the information available — and to remain ready to
> change it when the situation changes.

---

## Clinical Scenario

A 34-year-old man is brought to the resuscitation room following a high-speed
motor vehicle collision.

The pre-hospital team reports:

- Prolonged extrication
- Significant passenger-compartment intrusion
- Transient loss of consciousness
- Suspected pelvic injury
- One unit of blood given pre-hospital
- Persistent tachycardia
- Systolic blood pressure of 82 mmHg
- GCS 13

On arrival:

- Airway is patent
- Respiratory rate is 30/min
- Oxygen saturation is 94%
- Heart rate is 138/min
- Blood pressure is 78/48 mmHg
- Peripheral pulses are weak
- The patient is confused and increasingly agitated
- The abdomen is difficult to assess
- There is pelvic tenderness
- FAST demonstrates free fluid in the right upper quadrant

The trauma team leader now faces several simultaneous questions:

- Does the patient require immediate operative control?
- Should the patient undergo CT?
- Is the airway currently adequate?
- Is the hypotension primarily haemorrhagic?
- Should blood products be escalated immediately?
- Should the pelvis be mechanically stabilised?
- Does the patient need transfer directly to theatre?
- What information is still required?
- Which decisions cannot safely wait?

The leader does not have all the answers.

The leader must still decide.

---

# Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Describe the nature of decision-making during major trauma resuscitation.
2. Recognise why trauma decisions are frequently made with incomplete information.
3. Distinguish time-critical decisions from decisions that can safely wait.
4. Use structured decision-making during rapidly evolving resuscitation.
5. Recognise the difference between reversible and irreversible decisions.
6. Avoid cognitive traps associated with anchoring, premature closure and
   confirmation bias.
7. Maintain situational awareness while making immediate clinical decisions.
8. Reassess decisions as new information becomes available.
9. Communicate decisions clearly to the trauma team.
10. Escalate appropriately when the available resources or expertise are
    insufficient.
11. Use explicit decision points to prevent diagnostic drift.
12. Integrate clinical findings, mechanism, physiology and response to
    treatment.
13. Recognise when the safest decision is to act before complete diagnostic
    certainty is available.
14. Maintain flexibility when the patient's trajectory changes.
15. Apply a practical decision-making framework to the trauma resuscitation room.

---

# 1. Why Trauma Decision-Making Is Different

Major trauma is an environment of uncertainty.

The patient may be:

- critically ill,
- unable to provide a history,
- deteriorating rapidly,
- affected by multiple simultaneous injuries,
- physiologically altered by pre-hospital treatment,
- difficult to examine,
- surrounded by incomplete information,
- and subject to competing priorities.

The trauma team leader must therefore make decisions under conditions that
differ substantially from routine clinical practice.

In many clinical environments, the clinician can:

1. obtain a history,
2. perform a complete examination,
3. order investigations,
4. review the results,
5. formulate a differential diagnosis,
6. and then make a management decision.

Major trauma frequently reverses this sequence.

The patient may require treatment before the diagnosis is fully established.

The leader may need to decide:

> "What must happen now?"

before being able to answer:

> "Exactly what has happened?"

This distinction is fundamental.

---

# 2. The Trauma Leader Is Managing Uncertainty

The trauma leader should not expect certainty before acting.

Instead, the leader should continuously ask:

### What do we know?

For example:

- mechanism,
- physiological state,
- obvious injuries,
- response to treatment,
- available imaging,
- laboratory results,
- pre-hospital interventions.

### What do we strongly suspect?

For example:

- major haemorrhage,
- tension pneumothorax,
- traumatic brain injury,
- pelvic haemorrhage,
- airway deterioration.

### What could kill the patient if missed?

This question is often more important than constructing an exhaustive
differential diagnosis.

### What information would change our immediate management?

This prevents unnecessary investigation that does not affect the next decision.

---

# 3. The First Decision: Is There a Time-Critical Threat?

The first question in major trauma is not:

> "What is the diagnosis?"

It is:

> "Is there a threat that requires immediate intervention?"

Examples include:

- catastrophic external haemorrhage,
- airway obstruction,
- tension pneumothorax,
- massive haemothorax,
- severe haemorrhagic shock,
- traumatic cardiac arrest,
- impending airway failure,
- critical traumatic brain injury with herniation risk.

When such a threat is identified, definitive action should not be unnecessarily
delayed by investigations that will not alter the immediate management.

---

# 4. Time-Critical Versus Non-Time-Critical Decisions

A useful mental model is to divide decisions into two broad categories.

## Time-critical decisions

These may include:

- haemorrhage control,
- airway intervention,
- chest decompression,
- activation of massive haemorrhage protocols,
- urgent operative intervention,
- urgent transfer to an appropriate definitive-care environment.

These decisions often have a narrow therapeutic window.

## Non-time-critical decisions

These may include:

- detailed secondary examination,
- completion of the full medical history,
- definitive classification of some injuries,
- long-term rehabilitation planning,
- non-urgent specialist consultations.

The leader should actively protect time-critical decisions from being delayed
by lower-priority tasks.

---

# 5. The Principle of "What Cannot Wait?"

During a complex resuscitation, the leader should repeatedly ask:

> What cannot wait?

This question simplifies an overwhelming situation.

For example:

If the patient is hypotensive with suspected major haemorrhage:

The exact fracture classification can wait.

The decision to control haemorrhage cannot.

If the airway is progressively failing:

The complete neurological examination can wait.

The airway decision cannot.

If the patient is physiologically unstable:

A detailed radiological characterisation of every injury can wait.

The decision about the next definitive destination may not.

---

# 6. Decision-Making Should Follow the Patient's Physiology

Anatomy is important.

Physiology is often more urgent.

A patient with a dramatic radiological injury but stable physiology may have
time for controlled investigation.

A patient with apparently limited visible injury but profound shock may have
a life-threatening occult injury.

The leader should therefore continuously integrate:

- blood pressure,
- heart rate,
- mental status,
- respiratory status,
- peripheral perfusion,
- temperature,
- response to treatment,
- ongoing blood loss,
- and overall trajectory.

---

# 7. Trajectory Matters More Than a Single Number

A single blood pressure is a snapshot.

The trend may be more informative.

For example:

### Patient A

BP:

- 88/52
- 94/58
- 102/64

after appropriate resuscitation.

The patient may be responding.

### Patient B

BP:

- 104/68
- 92/54
- 78/46

despite blood products and haemorrhage-control measures.

This is a deteriorating trajectory.

The leader must recognise the difference.

The question is not simply:

> "What is the blood pressure?"

It is:

> "What is happening to the patient over time?"

---

# 8. The Concept of the Physiological Trajectory

The trauma leader should continuously classify the patient as:

### Improving

Evidence suggests that current interventions are working.

### Stable

The patient is maintaining an acceptable physiological state but remains
at risk.

### Deteriorating

The patient is moving towards physiological failure despite intervention.

### Temporarily responding

The patient improves briefly but deteriorates again.

The last category is particularly important.

A transient response should not be mistaken for definitive haemorrhage control.

---

# 9. Decision Points During the Primary Survey

The primary survey should not be viewed as a passive sequence.

It contains repeated decision points.

For each major domain, the leader should ask:

1. Is there an immediate life threat?
2. Has it been treated?
3. Did the patient respond?
4. What remains unresolved?
5. What is the next priority?

This creates a dynamic process rather than a checklist performed mechanically.

---

# 10. The Decision Loop

A practical decision loop is:

## Recognise

Identify the abnormality.

## Interpret

Determine its likely significance.

## Prioritise

Decide whether it requires immediate action.

## Act

Assign the intervention.

## Reassess

Determine whether the intervention worked.

## Escalate

If the problem persists, change the strategy or increase the level of
intervention.

This cycle should repeat throughout the resuscitation.

---

# 11. Recognise

Recognition begins with noticing that something is abnormal.

Examples:

- falling blood pressure,
- increasing heart rate,
- worsening mental status,
- increasing work of breathing,
- unilateral absent breath sounds,
- ongoing external haemorrhage,
- reduced urine output,
- worsening peripheral perfusion.

The leader should not rely exclusively on automated monitoring.

Clinical observation remains essential.

---

# 12. Interpret

Recognition alone is insufficient.

The leader must ask:

> What does this finding mean in this patient?

For example:

Tachycardia may represent:

- haemorrhage,
- pain,
- hypoxia,
- hypothermia,
- anxiety,
- medication effect,
- or another physiological stressor.

The context determines the interpretation.

---

# 13. Prioritise

Not every abnormality deserves immediate attention.

The leader should prioritise based on:

1. threat to life,
2. time sensitivity,
3. reversibility,
4. likelihood,
5. severity,
6. available intervention.

A minor laceration should not compete with uncontrolled internal haemorrhage
for the team's attention.

---

# 14. Act

Once a decision is made, it should be translated into a clear action.

Avoid vague commands.

Instead of:

> "Someone should probably get some blood."

Say:

> "Activate the massive haemorrhage protocol now."

Instead of:

> "The airway doesn't look great."

Say:

> "Prepare for definitive airway management. Anaesthesia, please assess now."

The team must understand what has been decided.

---

# 15. Reassess

Every major intervention should create a reassessment point.

Examples:

After haemorrhage control:

> Has bleeding decreased?

After chest decompression:

> Has ventilation and haemodynamics improved?

After airway intervention:

> Is oxygenation and ventilation adequate?

After blood product administration:

> Is perfusion improving?

The absence of improvement is information.

---

# 16. Escalate

Failure to respond should trigger escalation.

Escalation may involve:

- additional expertise,
- additional equipment,
- additional blood products,
- advanced imaging,
- operative intervention,
- interventional radiology,
- transfer to another centre,
- or a change in the overall treatment strategy.

The leader should not repeatedly perform an ineffective intervention without
changing the plan.

---

# 17. Reversible and Irreversible Decisions

Not all decisions carry the same consequences.

Some decisions are easily reversed.

Others are difficult or impossible to reverse.

### More reversible

Examples may include:

- changing monitoring strategy,
- requesting another assessment,
- obtaining additional laboratory tests,
- changing fluid or blood-product strategy,
- reassessing a provisional diagnosis.

### Less reversible

Examples may include:

- proceeding to major surgery,
- transferring a patient away from definitive trauma capability,
- committing to a specific treatment pathway,
- delaying definitive intervention until deterioration occurs.

The more irreversible the decision, the more important it becomes to confirm the
critical assumptions supporting it.

However, reversibility must not become an excuse for dangerous delay.

---

# 18. Decision Thresholds

The leader should identify thresholds that trigger action.

For example:

> "If the patient remains unstable despite initial haemorrhage-control
> measures, we will proceed directly to definitive haemorrhage control."

The exact threshold depends on:

- injury pattern,
- physiology,
- response to resuscitation,
- resources,
- local protocols,
- and specialist availability.

The principle is to avoid repeatedly asking the same question while the patient
deteriorates.

---

# 19. Pre-Commitment

Pre-commitment means deciding in advance what will trigger escalation.

Examples:

> "If blood pressure continues to fall despite blood products, we will not
> delay definitive haemorrhage control for additional imaging."

Or:

> "If the patient becomes unable to maintain the airway, we will proceed to
> definitive airway management."

Pre-commitment reduces hesitation during deterioration.

---

# 20. The Danger of "One More Test"

A common cognitive trap is:

> "Let's just get one more test."

Additional information can be valuable.

But every test has a cost:

- time,
- movement,
- distraction,
- resource use,
- and potential delay to definitive treatment.

The correct question is:

> "Will the result change what we do next?"

If the answer is no, the test may not be justified at that moment.

---

# 21. CT Is a Decision Tool, Not a Destination

CT provides valuable anatomical information.

However, the trauma team leader must decide whether the patient is appropriate
for CT at that time.

Before sending an unstable patient to CT, consider:

- current physiology,
- suspected injuries,
- response to resuscitation,
- ability to monitor and treat deterioration,
- availability of definitive treatment,
- and whether CT findings will change immediate management.

The CT scanner should never become the default destination simply because it
provides more information.

---

# 22. "Stable Enough for CT"

The phrase "stable enough for CT" should have clinical meaning.

It should not simply mean:

> "The blood pressure is above a particular number."

The leader should consider the entire clinical picture.

Questions include:

- Is the patient maintaining perfusion?
- Is the airway secure?
- Is ventilation adequate?
- Is haemorrhage controlled or controllable?
- Is the patient responding to resuscitation?
- Can the patient be safely monitored during transfer?
- Is the required treatment available immediately if the patient deteriorates?

---

# 23. The CT Decision

Before leaving the resuscitation room, the leader should be able to state:

> "We are going to CT because..."

and complete the sentence.

For example:

> "We are going to CT because the patient is responding to resuscitation,
> there is no immediate indication for operative intervention, and the
> additional anatomical information will guide definitive management."

If the leader cannot explain why CT is necessary now, the decision deserves
reconsideration.

---

# 24. The Operating Theatre Decision

Similarly, the leader should be able to state:

> "We are going to theatre because..."

Possible reasons include:

- uncontrolled haemorrhage,
- injury requiring immediate operative control,
- failure of resuscitation,
- or another time-critical surgical indication.

The decision should be communicated early enough to allow the receiving team
to prepare.

---

# 25. Interventional Radiology

In selected patients, interventional radiology may provide definitive
haemorrhage control.

The trauma leader must understand that this decision involves more than
identifying an anatomical lesion.

It requires consideration of:

- physiological status,
- bleeding source,
- availability,
- time to treatment,
- expertise,
- competing injuries,
- and the patient's overall trajectory.

The decision should be coordinated with the relevant specialists.

---

# 26. Avoiding False Certainty

Trauma decisions are frequently probabilistic.

The leader may think:

> "This is most likely pelvic haemorrhage."

But the correct mental model is:

> "Pelvic haemorrhage is currently the leading explanation, but other
> significant bleeding sources remain possible."

This preserves diagnostic flexibility.

---

# 27. Working Diagnosis Versus Final Diagnosis

A working diagnosis is sufficient to initiate many emergency interventions.

Examples:

- suspected tension pneumothorax,
- suspected major haemorrhage,
- suspected unstable pelvic injury,
- suspected severe traumatic brain injury.

The team does not always need definitive confirmation before acting.

The working diagnosis should remain explicitly provisional when appropriate.

---

# 28. The Leader Should Say What Is Known and Unknown

Useful language includes:

> "We have evidence of major haemorrhage."

> "The exact source is not yet established."

> "The patient is responding poorly to resuscitation."

> "We need definitive haemorrhage control."

This is clearer than pretending that the diagnosis is already certain.

---

# 29. Diagnostic Momentum

Once a diagnosis has been proposed, it can acquire momentum.

For example:

> "This is probably a pelvic bleed."

The team may then interpret every subsequent finding through that lens.

The leader should periodically ask:

> "What else could explain this physiology?"

This is particularly important when the patient is not responding as expected.

---

# 30. Anchoring

Anchoring occurs when the clinician becomes excessively influenced by the first
piece of information received.

Examples include:

- the pre-hospital diagnosis,
- the mechanism of injury,
- an early ultrasound finding,
- an apparently obvious injury,
- or a previous medical history.

The mechanism is useful.

It should not become a substitute for reassessment.

---

# 31. Confirmation Bias

Confirmation bias occurs when information supporting an existing belief receives
greater attention than information contradicting it.

Example:

The team believes the patient has isolated abdominal haemorrhage.

The patient develops worsening respiratory distress.

If the team continues focusing exclusively on the abdomen, a thoracic problem
may be missed.

The leader should actively seek disconfirming evidence.

---

# 32. Premature Closure

Premature closure occurs when the team stops searching for explanations after
finding one plausible diagnosis.

A patient may have:

- a pelvic fracture,
- a chest injury,
- and an abdominal haemorrhage.

Finding one significant injury does not exclude another.

The trauma leader should remain alert to multiple simultaneous injuries.

---

# 33. Search Satisficing

Search satisficing occurs when the team stops searching after finding one
abnormality.

Examples:

- finding one bleeding vessel,
- identifying one fracture,
- detecting one chest injury.

In major trauma:

> Finding an injury is not the same as finding all life-threatening injuries.

---

# 34. The "Obvious Injury" Trap

An obvious injury can dominate attention.

Examples:

- an open limb fracture,
- severe facial injury,
- a large scalp laceration,
- an obvious pelvic deformity.

The leader must maintain the primary survey.

The dramatic injury is not necessarily the injury that will kill the patient
first.

---

# 35. Mechanism Should Inform, Not Dictate

Mechanism is an important predictor of injury pattern.

However:

> Mechanism is a hypothesis generator, not a diagnosis.

The leader should use mechanism to anticipate injuries while allowing the
patient's physiology and examination to modify the working model.

---

# 36. Base Rates and Probability

Decision-making is improved when the leader considers:

- what is common,
- what is dangerous,
- what is plausible in this mechanism,
- and what is supported by the current findings.

A rare diagnosis should not automatically outrank a common life-threatening
diagnosis.

However, a rare condition should not be ignored when the clinical pattern
strongly supports it.

---

# 37. The "Can Kill the Patient" Test

When uncertain, ask:

> "What diagnosis, if missed now, could kill this patient?"

This question helps prioritise dangerous possibilities.

Examples:

- airway obstruction,
- tension pneumothorax,
- massive haemorrhage,
- traumatic brain herniation,
- cardiac tamponade,
- ongoing uncontrolled bleeding.

---

# 38. The "What Would Change My Mind?" Question

A powerful anti-bias question is:

> "What finding would make me change this plan?"

For example:

> "If the patient remains hypotensive despite blood products and external
> haemorrhage control, we will assume that major internal bleeding remains
> uncontrolled and escalate definitive haemorrhage control."

This keeps the decision dynamic.

---

# 39. The "Stop Point"

At major transitions, the leader should pause briefly.

Examples:

- after the primary survey,
- before CT,
- before leaving the resuscitation room,
- after major intervention,
- before transfer,
- after unexpected deterioration.

A 10-second pause can prevent a major error.

The pause is not inactivity.

It is deliberate reassessment.

---

# 40. The 10-Second Leadership Pause

A practical pause can involve five questions:

1. What is killing the patient?
2. What have we done?
3. Did it work?
4. What remains unresolved?
5. Where does the patient need to go next?

This can be performed rapidly without stopping the resuscitation.

---

# 41. Situation Awareness

Effective decision-making depends on situational awareness.

Situational awareness can be considered in three stages:

### Perception

What is happening?

### Understanding

What does it mean?

### Projection

What is likely to happen next?

The leader should continuously move through these stages.

---

# 42. Projection Is Particularly Important

The leader should ask:

> "If nothing changes over the next five minutes, what will happen?"

For a deteriorating patient, the answer may be:

- worsening shock,
- cardiac arrest,
- airway loss,
- worsening hypoxia,
- or irreversible organ injury.

This makes the cost of delay explicit.

---

# 43. Anticipatory Decision-Making

The best trauma leaders do not wait for deterioration before preparing for it.

If airway failure appears likely:

- equipment should be prepared,
- appropriate expertise should be present,
- a backup plan should be identified.

If haemorrhage is likely:

- blood products should be available,
- haemorrhage-control strategies should be considered,
- definitive treatment should be alerted.

Anticipation reduces the time between recognition and intervention.

---

# 44. The Next Five Minutes

At any point, the leader should be able to answer:

> "What are the three most important things that need to happen in the next
> five minutes?"

This forces prioritisation.

For example:

1. Continue blood-product resuscitation.
2. Obtain definitive haemorrhage-control plan.
3. Prepare for immediate transfer to theatre.

The exact priorities depend on the patient.

---

# 45. The Next Thirty Minutes

The leader should also maintain a broader horizon.

Ask:

> "Where does this patient need to be in 30 minutes?"

Potential destinations include:

- CT,
- operating theatre,
- interventional radiology,
- ICU,
- another trauma centre,
- or another definitive treatment area.

This prevents the team from becoming trapped in the immediate task.

---

# 46. Immediate Plan and Definitive Plan

Every major resuscitation should have two simultaneous plans.

## Immediate plan

What needs to happen now?

## Definitive plan

What needs to happen next?

For example:

### Immediate

- haemorrhage control,
- blood products,
- warming,
- monitoring.

### Definitive

- operative haemorrhage control.

The two plans should not be confused.

---

# 47. The Leader Should Avoid Task Saturation

Task saturation occurs when the leader attempts to personally manage too many
details.

This reduces cognitive capacity for:

- prioritisation,
- anticipation,
- communication,
- and decision-making.

The leader should delegate tasks while retaining responsibility for the overall
plan.

---

# 48. Information Overload

More information does not always produce better decisions.

During trauma resuscitation, the leader may receive:

- monitor readings,
- laboratory results,
- imaging findings,
- medication requests,
- nursing updates,
- specialist opinions,
- blood-product information,
- and pre-hospital information.

The leader must filter information according to relevance.

Ask:

> "Does this information change what we do next?"

---

# 49. Information Hierarchy

A useful hierarchy is:

### Immediate physiological threat

What could kill the patient now?

### Response to intervention

Is what we are doing working?

### Definitive treatment requirement

Where must the patient go?

### Supporting information

What additional information will improve the plan?

This hierarchy helps prevent low-value information from dominating attention.

---

# 50. The Leader Should Request Information Selectively

Instead of asking:

> "What are all the results?"

Ask:

> "What is the latest haemoglobin?"

> "Has the blood pressure responded?"

> "What did the FAST show?"

> "How much blood has been given?"

> "Is there ongoing external bleeding?"

Specific questions produce usable information.

---

# 51. Closed-Loop Decision Communication

A decision should have:

1. decision,
2. responsible person,
3. action,
4. confirmation,
5. reassessment.

Example:

> "Activate massive haemorrhage protocol."

Team member:

> "Activating massive haemorrhage protocol."

Leader:

> "Thank you. Tell me when the first pack arrives."

This creates a visible decision pathway.

---

# 52. Decision Ownership

The leader should avoid ambiguous ownership.

Poor:

> "Someone call theatre."

Better:

> "Ahmed, call theatre now and tell them we have an unstable patient with
> suspected uncontrolled intra-abdominal haemorrhage."

Specific ownership reduces omission.

---

# 53. Communicating Uncertainty

Uncertainty should be communicated explicitly.

For example:

> "We suspect major abdominal haemorrhage, but the source is not confirmed."

This allows the team to understand:

- what is known,
- what is suspected,
- and what remains uncertain.

---

# 54. Avoiding Ambiguous Language

Avoid phrases such as:

- "Maybe..."
- "Perhaps..."
- "Let's see..."
- "Someone should..."
- "We might need..."

when a decision is actually required.

Use:

> "Our working diagnosis is..."

> "Our priority is..."

> "The next step is..."

> "If this occurs, we will..."

---

# 55. When Experts Disagree

Trauma care involves multiple specialties.

Different clinicians may interpret the same information differently.

The leader should:

1. hear the concern,
2. clarify the disagreement,
3. identify the immediate risk,
4. make the decision,
5. communicate the plan.

Disagreement should not be suppressed.

It should be converted into useful information.

---

# 56. The Challenge-Response Model

A team member may say:

> "I am concerned that the patient is deteriorating."

The leader should respond:

> "What are you seeing?"

This invites useful information.

If the concern is significant:

> "Thank you. Let's reassess the patient now."

A leader who welcomes challenge is more likely to detect deterioration early.

---

# 57. The Importance of Speaking Up

All team members should be able to raise concerns.

The leader should establish this expectation before or during the resuscitation.

A simple statement is:

> "If anyone sees something that concerns them, say it immediately."

This creates psychological safety.

---

# 58. Avoiding Authority Gradient

Junior staff may hesitate to challenge senior clinicians.

This is particularly dangerous in trauma because the leader may have incomplete
information.

The leader should actively invite observations from:

- nurses,
- junior doctors,
- anaesthesia,
- surgery,
- radiology,
- pre-hospital staff,
- and other team members.

---

# 59. Decision-Making During Deterioration

When the patient deteriorates suddenly:

1. recognise the change,
2. announce it,
3. stop non-essential activity,
4. reassess the primary survey,
5. identify reversible causes,
6. intervene,
7. reassess response,
8. escalate if necessary.

The team should not simply continue the previous plan automatically.

---

# 60. The "Reset" Principle

Unexpected deterioration should trigger a mental reset.

The leader should consider:

> "We may no longer be dealing with the same clinical situation."

This prevents the previous plan from continuing by inertia.

---

# 61. Cognitive Inertia

Cognitive inertia occurs when a plan continues simply because it was already
started.

Examples:

- continuing fluid administration despite inadequate response,
- continuing preparation for CT despite deterioration,
- continuing a diagnostic pathway after the patient's physiology changes.

The correct question is:

> "If we were starting again right now, would we make the same decision?"

---

# 62. Dynamic Reassessment

The trauma plan is a living plan.

It should change when:

- physiology changes,
- new injuries are identified,
- treatment fails,
- new imaging becomes available,
- specialist input changes,
- or resources change.

Changing the plan is not failure.

Refusing to change an ineffective plan is failure.

---

# 63. Decision-Making and Resuscitation Response

Response to treatment provides diagnostic information.

For example:

A patient with suspected haemorrhagic shock who improves after blood products
provides evidence supporting significant blood loss.

A patient who does not improve should trigger reconsideration.

Possible explanations include:

- ongoing haemorrhage,
- incorrect diagnosis,
- inadequate intervention,
- another simultaneous life threat,
- or irreversible physiological injury.

---

# 64. The Non-Responder

A non-responder should trigger a structured reassessment.

Ask:

### Is bleeding still occurring?

### Has haemorrhage control been achieved?

### Is there a thoracic life threat?

### Is there an airway or ventilation problem?

### Is there another major injury?

### Is the resuscitation adequate?

### Is the patient becoming hypothermic?

### Has the treatment strategy failed?

The answer should drive escalation.

---

# 65. Temporary Response

A patient who initially improves but subsequently deteriorates should be treated
as a warning.

Possible explanations include:

- ongoing internal bleeding,
- loss of temporary haemorrhage control,
- inadequate resuscitation,
- evolving injury,
- or another emerging problem.

Temporary improvement should not automatically justify de-escalation.

---

# 66. Decision-Making in Haemorrhage

When major haemorrhage is suspected, the leader should focus on three parallel
objectives:

1. recognise haemorrhage,
2. resuscitate appropriately,
3. obtain definitive haemorrhage control.

Resuscitation should not become a substitute for haemorrhage control.

---

# 67. The Definitive-Control Question

At regular intervals ask:

> "What is controlling the bleeding?"

If the answer is:

> "We are giving blood."

that is not necessarily definitive haemorrhage control.

The leader should identify the actual strategy:

- direct pressure,
- tourniquet,
- pelvic stabilisation where indicated,
- surgery,
- interventional radiology,
- or another appropriate intervention.

---

# 68. Decision-Making in Airway Problems

Airway decisions should consider:

- current patency,
- ability to maintain protection,
- ventilation,
- oxygenation,
- anticipated deterioration,
- injury pattern,
- and available expertise.

The question is not only:

> "Can the patient maintain the airway right now?"

It is also:

> "Will the patient continue to maintain it?"

---

# 69. Decision-Making in Chest Trauma

The leader should avoid delaying treatment of an immediately life-threatening
thoracic injury while pursuing diagnostic certainty.

When the clinical picture strongly indicates an immediately dangerous condition,
the priority is timely treatment and reassessment.

---

# 70. Decision-Making in Traumatic Brain Injury

Neurological deterioration should be treated as a change in clinical state.

The leader should consider:

- oxygenation,
- ventilation,
- blood pressure and perfusion,
- airway protection,
- neurological examination,
- pupil findings,
- and the possibility of intracranial deterioration.

The trauma leader should avoid allowing other injuries to obscure a rapidly
worsening neurological problem.

---

# 71. Competing Priorities

Trauma often creates competing priorities.

For example:

- airway versus haemorrhage,
- imaging versus intervention,
- pelvic stabilisation versus transport,
- neurological assessment versus airway control.

The leader must determine which threat is:

1. most immediate,
2. most reversible,
3. most time-sensitive.

---

# 72. Parallel Processing

Major trauma should not always be managed sequentially.

Different team members can simultaneously:

- establish vascular access,
- control external bleeding,
- prepare blood products,
- assess the airway,
- obtain monitoring,
- perform ultrasound,
- and contact definitive services.

The leader coordinates these parallel processes.

---

# 73. Avoiding Serial Thinking

Serial thinking sounds like:

> "First we finish the airway, then we will think about bleeding."

In major trauma, multiple threats may need simultaneous management.

The leader should ask:

> "What can safely happen in parallel?"

---

# 74. The Two-Track Mental Model

A useful approach is to maintain:

### Track 1 — Resuscitation

What needs to happen now to keep the patient alive?

### Track 2 — Definitive care

What needs to happen next to treat the underlying injury?

This prevents the team from becoming trapped in the resuscitation room.

---

# 75. The Destination Decision

Every critically injured patient has a destination.

Possible destinations include:

- CT,
- operating theatre,
- interventional radiology,
- ICU,
- ward,
- another trauma centre.

The leader should begin considering destination early.

---

# 76. Destination Should Influence Preparation

If theatre is likely:

- theatre should be contacted early,
- appropriate specialists should prepare,
- blood products should continue,
- transfer logistics should be organised.

If CT is appropriate:

- the patient should be prepared for safe transport,
- monitoring should continue,
- required staff should accompany the patient,
- and deterioration contingencies should be considered.

---

# 77. Decision-Making Under Resource Constraints

Not every trauma system has:

- immediate CT,
- 24-hour interventional radiology,
- specialist trauma surgery,
- immediate anaesthesia,
- or advanced blood-product availability.

The decision must therefore account for the actual system.

The leader should ask:

> "What can we provide here, and what cannot we provide?"

---

# 78. Escalation and Transfer

If definitive treatment is unavailable locally:

> "Can this patient safely remain here?"

If not:

> "What is required to move the patient to definitive care?"

Transfer decisions should begin early.

Waiting until the patient is critically unstable can make transfer more
dangerous.

---

# 79. Local Protocols Matter

Decision-making should follow:

- local trauma pathways,
- massive haemorrhage protocols,
- transfer arrangements,
- imaging pathways,
- operative pathways,
- and specialist availability.

This chapter provides a framework.

Local protocols determine the operational details.

---

# 80. When the Leader Does Not Know

A leader does not need to know everything.

A safe leader recognises the limits of their expertise.

Appropriate actions include:

- calling a senior clinician,
- requesting specialist input,
- consulting the trauma service,
- contacting a tertiary centre,
- or activating transfer pathways.

Seeking help early is a strength.

---

# 81. Escalation Should Be Early

Do not wait until:

- the patient arrests,
- the airway becomes impossible,
- the blood pressure collapses,
- or the team runs out of options.

Escalation should occur when the trajectory suggests that current resources or
expertise may be insufficient.

---

# 82. Decision-Making and Human Factors

Decision quality is influenced by:

- fatigue,
- stress,
- noise,
- interruptions,
- hierarchy,
- workload,
- time pressure,
- team conflict,
- and information overload.

The leader cannot eliminate these factors.

The leader can design the team response to reduce their effect.

---

# 83. Noise Reduction

During critical decisions, reduce unnecessary conversation.

For example:

> "Quiet for a moment. I need the latest physiology."

This is not about creating silence throughout the resuscitation.

It is about protecting critical information.

---

# 84. Avoiding Interruptions During Critical Decisions

If the leader is making a major decision, unnecessary interruptions should be
minimised.

The team can continue essential clinical tasks while the leader receives the
information required for the decision.

---

# 85. The Leadership Pause Before Major Transitions

Before:

- CT,
- theatre,
- interventional radiology,
- transfer,
- or major procedural intervention,

perform a brief pause.

Confirm:

- diagnosis or working diagnosis,
- current physiology,
- major unresolved threats,
- destination,
- personnel,
- equipment,
- blood products,
- and contingency plan.

---

# 86. The "What If?" Question

Before a major transition, ask:

> "What are we going to do if the patient deteriorates?"

Examples:

> "If the patient becomes hypotensive in CT, who is leading the resuscitation?"

> "If the airway becomes compromised during transfer, what is our plan?"

> "If bleeding continues despite current measures, where is definitive
> control available?"

---

# 87. Decision-Making and Documentation

Important decisions should be documented.

Record:

- major findings,
- physiological deterioration,
- interventions,
- response,
- consultations,
- decision rationale,
- destination,
- and major changes in management.

Good documentation supports:

- continuity,
- handover,
- audit,
- quality improvement,
- and medicolegal clarity.

---

# 88. Decision Log

For complex cases, the team may benefit from explicitly documenting:

| Time | Finding | Decision | Intervention | Response |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 14:05 | BP 78/48 | Major haemorrhage suspected | MHP activated | Ongoing shock |
| 14:10 | FAST positive | Definitive haemorrhage control required | Theatre alerted | Preparing |
| 14:15 | BP remains low | CT deferred | Direct transfer to theatre | Ongoing resuscitation |

The exact format can be adapted to local documentation systems.

---

# 89. The Leader's Mental Checklist

During major decisions, consider:

### Patient

- What is the immediate threat?
- What is the physiology?
- Is the patient improving or deteriorating?

### Problem

- What is my working diagnosis?
- What else could explain the findings?
- What remains unknown?

### Priority

- What cannot wait?
- What can safely wait?
- What can happen in parallel?

### Plan

- What needs to happen now?
- What needs to happen next?
- What is the definitive treatment?

### People

- Who needs to act?
- Who needs to know?
- Who has additional expertise?

### Place

- Where does the patient need to go?

### Contingency

- What if the patient deteriorates?

---

# 90. The Three-Question Reset

When the situation becomes confusing, ask:

> 1. What is killing the patient?

> 2. What are we doing about it?

> 3. Is it working?

If the answer to the third question is no:

> Change the plan.

---

# 91. The Decision Ladder

A practical decision ladder is:

### Level 1

Recognise the threat.

### Level 2

Determine whether immediate action is required.

### Level 3

Choose the simplest effective intervention.

### Level 4

Assign responsibility.

### Level 5

Reassess response.

### Level 6

Escalate if unsuccessful.

### Level 7

Move towards definitive care.

This structure can be applied repeatedly throughout the resuscitation.

---

# 92. The Simplest Effective Decision

Under pressure, complexity can become dangerous.

When two approaches are similarly effective, the simpler approach may be safer
because it:

- reduces delay,
- reduces communication burden,
- reduces opportunities for error,
- and is easier for the team to execute.

The leader should avoid unnecessary complexity.

---

# 93. Avoiding Overanalysis

Overanalysis can be as dangerous as impulsive decision-making.

A leader may continue considering possibilities while the patient deteriorates.

The goal is not perfect certainty.

The goal is an appropriate decision within the available time.

---

# 94. Decision Quality Versus Outcome

A poor outcome does not automatically mean that the decision was poor.

Trauma patients may die despite appropriate management.

The leader should distinguish:

### Decision quality

Was the decision reasonable based on the information available at the time?

from:

### Outcome

What ultimately happened to the patient?

This distinction is important during clinical review and debriefing.

---

# 95. Hindsight Bias

After an outcome is known, it can appear obvious.

For example:

> "It was obvious that the patient had aortic injury."

But was it obvious before the imaging was performed?

Clinical review should reconstruct what was known at each decision point.

---

# 96. Counterfactual Thinking

After the resuscitation, ask:

> "What information was available at the time?"

> "What reasonable alternatives existed?"

> "What would have changed the decision?"

This produces a more useful review than simply asking:

> "Why didn't we know?"

---

# 97. Decision-Making During Team Debrief

The team should review:

- key decisions,
- decision timing,
- information available,
- communication,
- escalation,
- unexpected deterioration,
- and opportunities for improvement.

The purpose is learning, not blame.

---

# 98. Case-Based Discussion

## Case 1 — Unstable Patient With Positive FAST

A 45-year-old patient arrives after blunt abdominal trauma.

Observations:

- HR 132/min
- BP 76/42 mmHg
- altered mental status
- positive FAST
- ongoing blood loss suspected

### Decision

The patient should be treated as having life-threatening haemorrhage until
proven otherwise.

The team should activate appropriate haemorrhage resuscitation and pursue
definitive haemorrhage control according to the patient's condition and local
trauma pathway.

The key leadership decision is not to delay definitive management merely to
obtain additional anatomical information that will not change the immediate
course.

### Leadership lesson

The leader must distinguish:

> information that is useful

from:

> information that is necessary before acting.

---

# 99. Case 2 — Stable Patient With Multiple Injuries

A 28-year-old patient has:

- normal blood pressure,
- normal mental status,
- stable respiratory status,
- multiple areas of tenderness,
- and a mechanism suggesting significant injury.

### Decision

The patient may have time for structured assessment and appropriate imaging,
provided there is no emerging physiological instability.

### Leadership lesson

Not every major trauma patient requires immediate operative intervention.

The leader must avoid both:

- dangerous delay,
- and unnecessary escalation.

---

# 100. Case 3 — Temporary Response

A patient arrives hypotensive after blunt trauma.

Following initial blood products, the blood pressure rises from:

78/44 to 102/64 mmHg.

Ten minutes later:

- BP falls to 82/48 mmHg
- HR rises
- peripheral perfusion worsens

### Interpretation

The initial response was temporary.

The leader should assume that the underlying problem remains unresolved until
proven otherwise.

### Leadership lesson

A transient improvement is not the same as haemorrhage control.

---

# 101. Case 4 — Deterioration Before CT

A patient initially considered suitable for CT becomes increasingly hypotensive
while preparations are underway.

### Decision

The previous CT decision must be reconsidered.

The patient has changed.

The leader should reassess:

- airway,
- breathing,
- circulation,
- haemorrhage,
- response to treatment,
- and destination.

### Leadership lesson

A decision is valid only while its underlying assumptions remain valid.

---

# 102. Case 5 — The Dramatic Limb Injury

A patient has a severe open lower-limb injury with obvious deformity.

The team is focused on the limb.

The patient becomes progressively tachycardic and hypotensive.

### Leadership response

The leader should redirect the team:

> "The limb injury is important, but the patient is becoming shocked.
> We are returning to the primary survey."

### Leadership lesson

The most visible injury is not necessarily the most immediately dangerous.

---

# 103. Case 6 — Conflicting Opinions

The surgeon believes the patient requires immediate theatre.

Another clinician believes CT should be obtained first.

The patient is deteriorating.

### Leadership response

The leader should clarify:

- current physiology,
- what information CT would provide,
- whether that information would change immediate management,
- and whether definitive haemorrhage control is time-critical.

Then make the decision and communicate it.

### Leadership lesson

Disagreement should trigger clarification, not paralysis.

---

# 104. Case 7 — The Junior Team Member Speaks Up

A junior nurse says:

> "Doctor, I am worried the patient's blood pressure is falling."

The monitor shows a modest change.

### Appropriate response

> "Thank you. Let's look at the trend and reassess the patient."

The concern should be investigated.

### Leadership lesson

The team leader does not need to accept every concern as correct.

The leader does need to make it safe to raise concerns.

---

## 15. Confusing a good outcome with a good decision

# 106. The Trauma Leader's Decision Rules

The following rules are useful under pressure:

### Rule 1

Treat immediate life threats before pursuing diagnostic completeness.

### Rule 2

Use physiology and trajectory to guide urgency.

### Rule 3

Do not allow one injury to explain everything.

### Rule 4

Ask whether a test will change immediate management.

### Rule 5

Reassess after major interventions.

### Rule 6

A failed intervention requires a change in strategy.

### Rule 7

Communicate decisions explicitly.

### Rule 8

Escalate early when resources or expertise are insufficient.

### Rule 9

Prepare for the next deterioration before it occurs.

### Rule 10

Reconsider decisions whenever the patient's condition changes.

---

# 107. Practical Framework for Decision-Making Under Pressure

A compact framework for the trauma team leader is:

## STOP

### S — See the situation

What is happening now?

### T — Threat

What is the immediate life threat?

### O — Options

What can we do now?

### P — Plan

What is the next action, who will perform it, and where is the patient going?

Then:

## ACT

### A — Assign

Give clear responsibility.

### C — Communicate

State the plan and contingency.

### T — Test the response

Reassess whether the intervention worked.

This is not a replacement for clinical judgement.

It is a cognitive aid.

---

# 108. The Leader's Internal Monologue

An effective internal dialogue may sound like:

> "This patient is unstable."

> "The most likely immediate threat is major haemorrhage."

> "There may be more than one bleeding source."

> "We are giving blood, but this is not definitive control."

> "The patient is not responding adequately."

> "CT will not provide information that changes the immediate need for
> haemorrhage control."

> "I need definitive treatment mobilised now."

> "If the patient deteriorates further, we will escalate immediately."

This keeps the reasoning structured and visible.

---

# 109. Decision-Making Is a Team Activity

Although the trauma team leader owns the overall decision, useful information
comes from the entire team.

The leader should actively use:

- nursing observations,
- anaesthetic assessment,
- surgical assessment,
- ultrasound findings,
- laboratory results,
- radiology,
- pre-hospital information,
- and the response to interventions.

The leader's role is to integrate these inputs into a coherent plan.

---

# 110. The Leader Should Think Ahead

The strongest leaders continuously ask:

> "What will I need to know in five minutes?"

> "What will I need ready in ten minutes?"

> "Where will the patient need to go?"

> "Who needs to be contacted now?"

Anticipation creates time.

---

# 111. The Cost of Delay

Delay can occur because of:

- waiting for investigations,
- waiting for a specialist,
- unclear responsibility,
- repeated discussion,
- equipment problems,
- poor communication,
- or failure to recognise deterioration.

The leader should identify avoidable delay.

---

# 112. Decision Latency

Decision latency is the time between:

> recognition of a problem

and

> implementation of the required action.

The goal is not to eliminate all deliberation.

The goal is to minimise unnecessary delay in time-critical situations.

---

# 113. The Decision Is Not Finished Until the Action Begins

Saying:

> "We need theatre."

is not the same as:

- theatre being contacted,
- theatre accepting the patient,
- blood products being prepared,
- staff being assembled,
- and transfer being organised.

The leader should follow the decision through to execution.

---

# 114. Decision Execution

For major decisions, confirm:

1. Who is responsible?
2. Has the action started?
3. What is the expected timeframe?
4. What could prevent completion?
5. What is the backup plan?

This converts intention into action.

---

# 115. The Decision-to-Destination Chain

A major trauma decision can be represented as:

> Recognition
> ↓
> Prioritisation
> ↓
> Intervention
> ↓
> Response
> ↓
> Definitive treatment
> ↓
> Destination
> ↓
> Handover

A break anywhere in this chain can compromise care.

---

# 116. The Trauma Leader's Final Questions Before Leaving the Resuscitation Room

Before transferring the patient, ask:

- What are the known major injuries?
- What remains suspected?
- What remains untreated?
- Is the airway secure?
- Is ventilation adequate?
- Is haemorrhage controlled?
- What blood products have been given?
- What interventions are currently running?
- What could deteriorate during transfer?
- Who is receiving the patient?
- What is the contingency plan?

---

# 117. Key Takeaways

1. Major trauma decisions are frequently made with incomplete information.
2. The leader should prioritise life-threatening and time-critical problems.
3. The goal is not perfect diagnostic certainty before treatment.
4. Physiology and trajectory should influence urgency.
5. Every major intervention should be followed by reassessment.
6. Failure to respond should trigger reconsideration and escalation.
7. Working diagnoses should remain flexible.
8. Anchoring, confirmation bias and premature closure are important threats.
9. A test is valuable when its result can change management.
10. CT should not become an automatic destination for an unstable patient.
11. Immediate and definitive management should be considered simultaneously.
12. Parallel processing is often necessary.
13. Clear communication converts decisions into action.
14. The team should be encouraged to challenge concerning findings.
15. Escalation should occur before the patient's situation becomes unrecoverable.
16. Major transitions should include a brief leadership pause.
17. A decision must be followed through to execution.
18. The patient's changing condition may invalidate a previous decision.
19. Good decision-making should be judged using the information available at
    the time, not only by the eventual outcome.
20. The trauma team leader's central task is to maintain direction despite
    uncertainty.

---

# 118. Trauma Team Leader Cognitive Aid

When under significant pressure:

> **WHAT IS HAPPENING?**

What has changed?

> **WHAT IS KILLING THE PATIENT?**

Identify the immediate threat.

> **WHAT HAVE WE DONE?**

List the major interventions.

> **DID IT WORK?**

Assess the response.

> **WHAT REMAINS UNRESOLVED?**

Identify untreated threats.

> **WHAT HAPPENS NEXT?**

Choose the next priority.

> **WHO IS DOING IT?**

Assign responsibility.

> **WHERE IS THE PATIENT GOING?**

Establish definitive destination.

> **WHAT IF THE PATIENT DETERIORATES?**

State the contingency plan.

# 119. Case-Based Discussion — Complete Scenario

A 52-year-old patient is brought after a high-speed collision.

On arrival:

- HR 142/min
- BP 74/42 mmHg
- RR 32/min
- GCS 12
- cool peripheries
- pelvic tenderness
- abdominal tenderness
- reduced air entry on the left

The leader initially suspects major haemorrhage.

Blood products are started.

The patient remains unstable.

A team member reports worsening respiratory distress.

The leader pauses.

### Question 1

What is the immediate problem?

Answer:

The patient remains in severe physiological instability with more than one
possible life-threatening process.

### Question 2

Should the leader continue focusing exclusively on abdominal or pelvic
haemorrhage?

No.

The new respiratory findings require immediate reassessment of breathing and
the chest.

### Question 3

What cognitive error could occur?

Anchoring on the initial diagnosis.

### Question 4

What should the leader do?

Reassess the primary survey and identify whether a treatable thoracic
life-threatening injury is present.

### Question 5

What happens after intervention?

The leader reassesses:

- respiratory status,
- oxygenation,
- circulation,
- and overall trajectory.

### Question 6

If the patient remains unstable?

The leader should reconsider the entire working model, continue appropriate
haemorrhage resuscitation, and escalate towards definitive treatment.

### Lesson

The patient's condition is dynamic.

The trauma leader must be dynamic as well.

---

# 120. Final Leadership Principle

The trauma team leader is not expected to predict the future.

The leader is expected to:

- recognise danger,
- make timely decisions,
- communicate clearly,
- use the team's information,
- reassess continuously,
- anticipate deterioration,
- and change direction when the evidence changes.

The safest trauma leader is not the person who is always certain.

It is the person who can act decisively while remaining willing to reconsider.

> **Decide. Act. Reassess. Adapt.**

That cycle is at the heart of effective trauma leadership.

# Chapter Summary

The trauma team leader works in an environment where:

- information is incomplete,
- time is limited,
- physiology changes rapidly,
- multiple threats may coexist,
- and decisions often have to be made before diagnostic certainty exists.

Effective decision-making therefore depends on a disciplined cycle:

> **Recognise → Interpret → Prioritise → Act → Reassess → Escalate**

The leader should continuously ask:

> **What is killing the patient?**

> **Is what we are doing working?**

> **What needs to happen next?**

The ability to make a timely decision is important.

The ability to recognise when that decision is no longer appropriate is equally
important.

> **The best trauma decision is not necessarily the most certain decision.
> It is the decision that best protects the patient with the information
> available at that moment — followed by continuous reassessment.**
