# Chapter 12 — Breathing and Chest Trauma

## Clinical Scenario

A 28-year-old man is brought to the resuscitation room following a high-speed road traffic collision.

He has increasing respiratory distress and severe right-sided chest pain.

The pre-hospital team reports progressive tachypnoea, reduced air entry on the right, and increasing oxygen requirements during transport.

On arrival, the patient is anxious and speaking only in short sentences.

Initial observations are:

- respiratory rate 34/min;
- oxygen saturation 88% despite supplemental oxygen;
- heart rate 128/min;
- systolic blood pressure 102 mmHg;
- Glasgow Coma Scale 14/15;
- cool peripheries.

The Trauma Team Leader recognises that the breathing problem may be immediately life-threatening.

The team must rapidly determine whether the patient has a reversible thoracic emergency, while continuing assessment for other causes of respiratory and circulatory compromise.

The leader must also recognise that chest trauma is dynamic. A patient who initially appears relatively stable may deteriorate rapidly.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain the purpose of the breathing assessment during trauma resuscitation.
2. Recognise immediately life-threatening thoracic injuries.
3. Integrate clinical findings rather than relying on a single examination sign.
4. Identify tension pneumothorax and recognise when immediate treatment is required.
5. Recognise massive haemothorax and understand its combined respiratory and circulatory consequences.
6. Recognise open pneumothorax and other major chest wall injuries.
7. Understand the significance of pulmonary contusion and evolving respiratory failure.
8. Coordinate oxygenation, ventilation, monitoring, imaging, and definitive management.
9. Reassess the patient after thoracic interventions.
10. Anticipate deterioration and communicate a clear escalation plan.

## Key Concepts

### Breathing Is Both a Respiratory and Circulatory Assessment

The breathing assessment should not be limited to oxygen saturation or respiratory rate.

Thoracic injury can produce both respiratory failure and obstructive or haemorrhagic circulatory compromise.

The Trauma Team Leader should therefore integrate:

- respiratory rate;
- work of breathing;
- oxygenation;
- chest movement;
- breath sounds;
- chest wall findings;
- haemodynamic status;
- mental status;
- and response to intervention.

The important question is:

> **"Is there a chest problem that is threatening oxygenation, ventilation, or circulation right now?"**

### Treat Immediately Life-Threatening Problems

A sufficiently recognised life-threatening thoracic injury should be treated without unnecessary delay.

The team should not allow completion of a diagnostic sequence to delay a time-critical intervention.

At the same time, treatment should remain integrated with ongoing assessment.

After an intervention, the leader should ask:

> **"Did the patient improve as expected?"**

Failure to improve should trigger reconsideration of the diagnosis, adequacy of treatment, and possibility of another concurrent problem.

### Clinical Findings Must Be Integrated

No single finding should be interpreted in isolation.

Reduced air entry may occur with several thoracic conditions.

Tachycardia and hypotension may reflect hypovolaemia, obstructive physiology, pain, or another injury.

Hypoxia may result from chest wall injury, pneumothorax, haemothorax, pulmonary contusion, airway problems, or other causes.

The leader should therefore combine:

**Mechanism → Examination → Physiology → Response → Trajectory**

## Immediate Breathing Assessment

### Observe Before Intervening

The leader should rapidly observe the patient's overall respiratory state.

Important observations include:

- respiratory rate;
- respiratory effort;
- ability to speak;
- use of accessory muscles;
- chest symmetry;
- visible chest wounds;
- cyanosis where present;
- agitation or reduced consciousness;
- and overall trajectory.

A patient who is becoming progressively exhausted may be deteriorating even if an individual numerical measurement has not yet changed dramatically.

### Inspect the Chest

The chest should be adequately exposed to identify important abnormalities.

The team should look for:

- penetrating wounds;
- open chest wounds;
- bruising;
- deformity;
- paradoxical movement;
- asymmetrical expansion;
- extensive subcutaneous emphysema;
- and other evidence of major chest trauma.

The absence of an obvious external injury does not exclude significant internal thoracic injury.

### Palpate and Assess Chest Wall Stability

Palpation may identify:

- tenderness;
- crepitus;
- rib fractures;
- chest wall instability;
- and subcutaneous emphysema.

A severely injured chest wall may impair ventilation through pain, mechanical instability, or associated pulmonary injury.

### Auscultation

Auscultation should be performed as part of the overall assessment.

The leader should consider:

- asymmetry of breath sounds;
- markedly reduced or absent air entry;
- abnormal additional sounds;
- and changes compared with previous examinations.

However, auscultation should not be treated as a definitive exclusion test for serious thoracic pathology.

### Percussion and Other Clinical Findings

Where clinically useful, percussion may contribute to the assessment.

The leader should integrate this finding with the rest of the examination rather than allowing one sign to determine the diagnosis in isolation.

## Tension Pneumothorax

### Why It Matters

Tension pneumothorax is a time-critical thoracic emergency.

Increasing intrathoracic pressure can impair ventilation and venous return, producing both respiratory and circulatory compromise.

The clinical picture may include:

- severe respiratory distress;
- hypoxia;
- tachypnoea;
- unilateral reduced or absent breath sounds;
- haemodynamic deterioration;
- distended neck veins in some patients;
- and progressive clinical deterioration.

The classical combination of findings may not be present in every patient.

### Do Not Wait for the Full Picture

The diagnosis should be considered clinically in a deteriorating trauma patient with compatible findings.

If tension physiology is sufficiently suspected, treatment should not be unnecessarily delayed for imaging.

The leader should ensure that the appropriate intervention is performed promptly by a suitably trained clinician using the locally approved technique and equipment.

### Reassess Immediately

Following decompression or other definitive intervention, the team should reassess:

- respiratory effort;
- oxygenation;
- chest movement;
- breath sounds;
- heart rate;
- blood pressure;
- and overall clinical response.

A lack of improvement should prompt immediate reconsideration.

Possible explanations include:

- incorrect diagnosis;
- inadequate intervention;
- recurrent or persistent pathology;
- another thoracic injury;
- or a non-thoracic cause of deterioration.

## Massive Haemothorax

### Recognise the Combined Threat

Massive haemothorax can cause both respiratory compromise and major blood loss.

The patient may present with:

- respiratory distress;
- reduced breath sounds;
- chest asymmetry;
- dullness where clinically appreciable;
- tachycardia;
- hypotension;
- and signs of haemorrhagic shock.

The leader should recognise that the respiratory findings may be only one part of the problem.

### Resuscitation and Definitive Haemorrhage Control

Management should occur within the broader haemorrhage-control strategy.

This may require:

- appropriate vascular or intraosseous access;
- blood product resuscitation;
- thoracic drainage by an appropriately trained clinician;
- monitoring of the response;
- and early involvement of the appropriate surgical or trauma team.

The key question is not simply whether blood has been drained.

It is:

> **"Is the patient's haemorrhage controlled, and what definitive treatment is required?"**

### Monitor the Response

The leader should monitor:

- haemodynamic response;
- respiratory status;
- ongoing blood loss;
- chest drain output where applicable;
- and overall trajectory.

Persistent instability despite initial management should trigger escalation and reassessment.

## Open Pneumothorax

### Recognise the Injury

An open chest wound may create a communication between the atmosphere and pleural space.

The patient may develop:

- respiratory distress;
- hypoxia;
- abnormal chest movement;
- and an obvious penetrating chest wound.

The wound should be identified and managed promptly according to local trauma protocols.

### Coordinate the Immediate Response

The leader should ensure that:

- the wound is addressed promptly;
- oxygenation and ventilation are supported;
- the patient is continuously reassessed;
- and definitive management is arranged.

The presence of an open chest wound should also prompt assessment for associated intrathoracic and vascular injury.

## Flail Chest and Chest Wall Injury

### Understand the Mechanism of Respiratory Failure

Severe chest wall injury may impair ventilation through:

- pain;
- mechanical instability;
- underlying pulmonary contusion;
- reduced respiratory effort;
- and fatigue.

The visible chest wall abnormality may therefore underestimate the severity of the underlying pulmonary injury.

### Analgesia Is Part of Respiratory Management

Effective analgesia can improve ventilation and reduce splinting.

The leader should ensure that pain management is incorporated into the overall resuscitation plan while continuing to monitor respiratory status and mental state.

Analgesia should not replace assessment for more serious thoracic pathology.

### Anticipate Deterioration

A patient with significant chest wall injury may initially maintain acceptable oxygenation and subsequently deteriorate.

The leader should therefore monitor trends rather than relying on the initial assessment alone.

## Pulmonary Contusion

### Early Findings May Underestimate Severity

Pulmonary contusion can evolve over time.

The patient may initially have relatively limited clinical findings before developing increasing respiratory distress and oxygenation impairment.

The leader should consider pulmonary contusion when respiratory function worsens following significant blunt chest trauma.

### Support and Reassess

Management is primarily supportive and depends on the patient's respiratory condition.

The team should monitor:

- oxygenation;
- respiratory effort;
- ventilation;
- work of breathing;
- and overall trajectory.

The leader should avoid unnecessary fluid loading when pulmonary injury and haemorrhage are both concerns, while ensuring appropriate resuscitation for the patient's actual physiological state.

## Airway and Chest Injury Interactions

### Thoracic Trauma May Complicate Airway Management

Chest injury should be considered when an injured patient deteriorates after airway intervention.

Positive-pressure ventilation can alter intrathoracic pressures and may worsen certain thoracic conditions.

The leader should therefore reassess the chest whenever respiratory or haemodynamic deterioration follows airway management.

### Deterioration After Intubation

A sudden deterioration after intubation should prompt a structured reassessment.

Potential causes include:

- tension pneumothorax;
- tube-related problems;
- inadequate ventilation;
- mainstem intubation;
- equipment failure;
- worsening pulmonary injury;
- or other causes of shock.

The leader should avoid assuming that deterioration is caused by a single mechanism without reassessment.

## Oxygenation and Ventilation

### Oxygenation Is Not the Whole Assessment

A satisfactory oxygen saturation does not necessarily mean that ventilation is adequate.

The leader should consider:

- respiratory rate;
- work of breathing;
- mental status;
- ventilation;
- oxygen requirement;
- and trajectory.

A patient who requires progressively increasing respiratory support may be deteriorating even before severe hypoxia develops.

### Avoid Delayed Recognition of Respiratory Failure

Increasing fatigue, worsening mental status, rising work of breathing, or inability to maintain adequate oxygenation should prompt escalation.

The leader should anticipate the need for advanced respiratory support rather than waiting until deterioration becomes catastrophic.

## Imaging During the Breathing Assessment

### Use Imaging to Answer a Clinical Question

Imaging can support the assessment of thoracic trauma, but it should not delay treatment of a sufficiently recognised life-threatening condition.

Depending on the patient's condition and local trauma pathway, imaging may include:

- chest radiography;
- focused ultrasound;
- or CT in an appropriately selected and sufficiently stable patient.

The important question is:

> **"What decision will this investigation change?"**

### Do Not Transfer an Unstable Patient Without a Clear Purpose

Moving a critically unstable patient for imaging carries risk.

Before transfer, the leader should consider:

- current physiological stability;
- airway and ventilation requirements;
- monitoring;
- available personnel;
- immediate treatment needs;
- and whether the imaging result will alter management.

The investigation should serve the resuscitation pathway rather than become the endpoint.

## Reassessment After Thoracic Intervention

### Reassessment Is Mandatory

Every significant thoracic intervention should be followed by reassessment.

The leader should ask:

- Did oxygenation improve?
- Did respiratory effort improve?
- Did breath sounds change?
- Did blood pressure improve?
- Did heart rate improve?
- Is the patient clinically better?
- Is there evidence of another problem?

### Failure to Improve Is Diagnostic Information

If the patient does not respond as expected, the team should not simply repeat the same intervention without reconsideration.

The leader should revisit:

**Diagnosis → Intervention → Technical adequacy → Response → Alternative pathology**

This approach reduces fixation on an incorrect initial diagnosis.

## Anticipating Deterioration

The Trauma Team Leader should continuously ask what is likely to happen next.

Examples include:

- increasing oxygen requirement;
- worsening respiratory fatigue;
- evolving pulmonary contusion;
- recurrent or persistent pneumothorax;
- ongoing intrathoracic bleeding;
- deterioration after positive-pressure ventilation;
- or progression towards respiratory failure.

Anticipation allows the team to prepare equipment, expertise, blood products, monitoring, and definitive support before the patient reaches a critical state.

## Coordinating the Team

### Assign Clear Ownership

Critical tasks should have identifiable owners.

For example:

> **"Ahmed, assess the right chest and tell me whether you find features suggesting a tension pneumothorax."**

Or:

> **"Sarah, prepare the equipment for thoracic decompression and tell me when it is ready."**

The leader should know:

- who is performing the task;
- what is expected;
- when an update is required;
- and what the next action will be.

### Maintain a Shared Mental Model

The leader should periodically summarise the situation.

For example:

> **"We have worsening respiratory distress with unilateral reduced air entry and evolving haemodynamic compromise. We are treating the suspected thoracic emergency now, reassessing the response, and preparing for definitive chest management."**

This helps the team understand both the current problem and the intended direction of management.

## When to Escalate

Escalation should occur early when:

- respiratory support requirements are increasing;
- the patient remains unstable despite intervention;
- major intrathoracic haemorrhage is suspected;
- definitive thoracic intervention may be required;
- advanced airway or ventilatory support is anticipated;
- or the diagnosis remains uncertain despite deterioration.

The leader should activate the appropriate trauma, surgical, anaesthetic, critical care, or other specialist resources according to local pathways.

## Definitive Management

The breathing assessment should lead towards definitive care.

Depending on the injury pattern, this may involve:

- definitive chest drainage;
- operative management;
- specialist thoracic intervention;
- critical care;
- advanced ventilatory support;
- or transfer to an appropriate trauma facility.

The leader should avoid allowing temporary stabilisation to become the endpoint.

The key question remains:

> **"What does this patient need next, and what needs to happen now to get them there safely?"**

## Practical Approach

### First 30 Seconds

Rapidly establish:

1. Is the patient maintaining adequate oxygenation?
2. Is ventilation adequate?
3. Is there severe respiratory distress?
4. Is there an obvious chest wound?
5. Are breath sounds markedly asymmetric?
6. Is there evidence of tension physiology?
7. Is there haemodynamic compromise?
8. Is the patient deteriorating?

### Immediate Priorities

The leader should then:

- identify immediately life-threatening thoracic problems;
- initiate appropriate treatment without unnecessary delay;
- support oxygenation and ventilation;
- assess for associated haemorrhage;
- assign clear task ownership;
- and establish the next reassessment point.

### Reassess

After intervention:

> **Recognise → Intervene → Reassess → Escalate → Definitive Care**

This cycle should continue throughout the resuscitation.

## Common Leadership Errors

### Treating Oxygen Saturation as the Entire Breathing Assessment

A single oxygen saturation value does not describe the patient's complete respiratory state.

The leader should assess the patient, not just the monitor.

### Waiting for Imaging Before Treating an Obvious Emergency

Imaging should support clinical decision-making.

It should not unnecessarily delay treatment of a sufficiently recognised life-threatening thoracic problem.

### Focusing on the Chest Without Considering Circulation

Major chest trauma can cause both respiratory and haemorrhagic shock.

The leader must consider both.

### Assuming an Intervention Has Worked

The intervention is not the endpoint.

The response must be assessed.

### Failing to Anticipate Deterioration

A patient who is currently compensating may deteriorate rapidly.

The team should prepare for the next likely problem rather than waiting for it to occur.

## Clinical Pearl

> **In chest trauma, the critical question is not simply "What do I hear?" It is "What is threatening oxygenation, ventilation, or circulation now, what can I treat immediately, and did the patient respond as expected?"**
