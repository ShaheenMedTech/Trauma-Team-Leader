# Chapter 16 — Pelvic Trauma, Genitourinary Injury and Perineal Assessment

## Clinical Scenario

A 29-year-old man is brought to the resuscitation room following a high-speed motorcycle collision.

The patient was reportedly thrown several metres from the motorcycle and landed heavily on his pelvis.

On arrival, he is conscious but distressed.

Initial observations are:

- respiratory rate 26/min;
- oxygen saturation 96% on supplemental oxygen;
- heart rate 128/min;
- systolic blood pressure 88 mmHg;
- Glasgow Coma Scale 14/15;
- cool peripheries.

There is no major external haemorrhage.

The patient reports severe pelvic pain and is unable to move his lower limbs comfortably because of pain.

The team identifies bruising across the lower abdomen and perineum.

The Trauma Team Leader recognises that the absence of obvious external bleeding does not exclude major pelvic haemorrhage.

The immediate priorities are therefore to assess the patient's physiology, minimise further disruption of a potentially unstable pelvic injury, identify associated genitourinary injury, and activate an appropriate pathway for haemorrhage control.

The leader must also avoid allowing examination or investigation to delay definitive treatment if the patient remains unstable.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Recognise pelvic trauma as a potential source of life-threatening haemorrhage.
2. Identify clinical features that should increase suspicion for significant pelvic injury.
3. Understand the role and limitations of pelvic examination during trauma assessment.
4. Recognise when pelvic stabilisation should be considered.
5. Identify clinical features suggesting urethral or bladder injury.
6. Perform an appropriate perineal and genital assessment.
7. Avoid unnecessary repeated manipulation of an unstable pelvis.
8. Integrate pelvic findings with the overall haemodynamic picture.
9. Recognise when definitive haemorrhage control should take priority over further investigation.
10. Coordinate multidisciplinary management of major pelvic trauma.

## Key Concepts

### Pelvic Trauma Is a Haemorrhage Problem Until Proven Otherwise

A major pelvic injury may be associated with substantial blood loss despite minimal external bleeding.

Potential sources include:

- pelvic arterial bleeding;
- pelvic venous plexus bleeding;
- cancellous bone surfaces;
- associated soft-tissue injury;
- and concurrent intra-abdominal or retroperitoneal bleeding.

The clinical challenge is that much of this blood loss may occur internally.

The patient may therefore deteriorate without an obvious external source.

The Trauma Team Leader should interpret pelvic injury in the context of the entire physiological picture rather than waiting for a single examination finding to establish its significance.

### Mechanism Matters

Mechanisms that should increase suspicion for significant pelvic injury include:

- high-energy road traffic collision;
- motorcycle or pedestrian impact;
- significant crush mechanism;
- fall from height;
- direct high-energy impact to the pelvis;
- and major deceleration.

The mechanism should not be used in isolation.

It should be integrated with:

**Mechanism → Injury pattern → Physiology → Examination → Response to treatment**

A patient with a relatively modest-looking pelvic injury but persistent shock requires the same degree of clinical attention as a patient with obvious pelvic disruption.

### Pelvic Injury May Coexist With Other Sources of Shock

A patient with pelvic trauma may also have:

- thoracic haemorrhage;
- abdominal injury;
- retroperitoneal bleeding;
- long-bone fractures;
- external haemorrhage;
- or traumatic brain injury.

The leader should therefore avoid anchoring on the pelvis as the only possible explanation for shock.

Persistent haemodynamic instability should trigger continued reassessment for all major sources of blood loss.

## Recognising Significant Pelvic Injury

### Clinical Features

Possible features include:

- severe pelvic pain;
- inability to mobilise because of pain;
- pelvic or lower abdominal bruising;
- perineal bruising;
- genital swelling;
- limb-position abnormalities;
- apparent limb shortening or rotation;
- haemodynamic instability;
- and associated lower abdominal or perineal injury.

No single finding reliably excludes significant pelvic trauma.

### Do Not Repeatedly "Spring" the Pelvis

Repeated manual compression or manipulation of the pelvis is generally unhelpful and may potentially disrupt clot formation or worsen bleeding.

The leader should avoid repeated attempts to determine pelvic stability through aggressive manual testing.

If a significant pelvic injury is suspected clinically, the team should proceed according to the local trauma protocol and use appropriate stabilisation rather than repeatedly manipulating the pelvis.

### Pelvic Binder Considerations

A pelvic binder may be used when an unstable pelvic injury is suspected, according to local trauma protocols.

The purpose is to reduce pelvic volume and stabilise the pelvic ring.

The binder should be:

- correctly positioned;
- applied according to the device and local protocol;
- checked after application;
- and not allowed to become a substitute for definitive haemorrhage control.

Incorrect positioning may reduce effectiveness.

The team should also remain alert to complications associated with prolonged or inappropriate application.

### Reassessment After Stabilisation

After pelvic stabilisation, the leader should reassess:

- haemodynamic status;
- pain;
- peripheral perfusion;
- neurological findings;
- limb position;
- and overall clinical trajectory.

The important question is not simply whether the binder has been applied.

The important question is:

> **"Has the patient's physiology improved, and what does the response tell us about the underlying problem?"**

Failure to improve should prompt continued investigation of other bleeding sources and consideration of definitive haemorrhage control.

## Pelvic Haemorrhage

### Why Pelvic Bleeding Is Difficult

Pelvic haemorrhage can be difficult to recognise because:

- bleeding may be concealed;
- the patient may have multiple injuries;
- hypotension may initially be attributed to another cause;
- physical examination may underestimate the severity of injury;
- and bleeding may be venous, bony, arterial, or mixed.

The leader should therefore use physiology and trajectory rather than relying solely on the initial examination.

### Clinical Indicators

Features that may increase concern include:

- persistent hypotension;
- tachycardia;
- cool peripheries;
- altered mental status;
- increasing transfusion requirements;
- poor response to initial resuscitation;
- and known or strongly suspected pelvic disruption.

The absence of hypotension does not necessarily exclude significant bleeding, particularly early after injury or when compensatory mechanisms remain effective.

### Major Haemorrhage Activation

When major bleeding is suspected, the team should activate the appropriate major haemorrhage pathway according to local protocol.

This may involve:

- blood products;
- haemorrhage-control specialists;
- trauma surgery;
- interventional radiology;
- orthopaedic surgery;
- anaesthesia;
- critical care;
- and transfer to an appropriate definitive treatment facility.

The leader should communicate the suspected source clearly.

A useful statement is:

> **"This patient has suspected major pelvic haemorrhage with ongoing physiological instability. We need definitive haemorrhage control while resuscitation continues."**

## Definitive Haemorrhage Control

### Resuscitation Is Not Definitive Treatment

Blood products and temporary stabilisation may support the patient, but they do not themselves control the source of haemorrhage.

The leader should continuously consider whether the patient requires:

- operative haemorrhage control;
- pelvic packing;
- angioembolisation;
- other interventional radiology procedures;
- mechanical stabilisation;
- or transfer to a centre capable of providing definitive treatment.

The exact pathway depends on:

- the source of bleeding;
- the patient's physiology;
- local expertise;
- imaging findings where appropriate;
- and institutional trauma protocols.

### Unstable Patients

A persistently unstable patient should not be sent for investigations simply because imaging is diagnostically attractive.

The leader must ask:

> **"Will this investigation change what we do next, and can the patient safely tolerate the journey?"**

If the patient requires immediate definitive haemorrhage control, diagnostic processes should not create avoidable delay.

### Stable or Responding Patients

A patient who demonstrates a sustained physiological response may be appropriate for further assessment and imaging.

However, stability should be reassessed continuously.

A patient who deteriorates during investigation should return immediately to active resuscitation and reassessment.

## Perineal Assessment

### Why Examine the Perineum?

The perineum can contain important clues to pelvic and genitourinary injury.

Assessment may identify:

- bruising;
- haematoma;
- swelling;
- laceration;
- blood at the urethral meatus;
- genital injury;
- and evidence of open pelvic trauma.

The examination should be performed sensitively but systematically.

### Perineal Bruising

Perineal bruising may indicate significant pelvic or soft-tissue trauma.

It should increase the team's suspicion for associated genitourinary injury.

However, absence of bruising does not exclude urethral or bladder injury.

### Open Pelvic Injury

An open wound communicating with the pelvic region represents a particularly serious injury pattern.

The team should assess:

- location;
- depth;
- contamination;
- active bleeding;
- associated rectal or perineal injury;
- and evidence of communication with the pelvic cavity.

Management should involve early specialist input and appropriate infection and haemorrhage-control strategies.

## Genitourinary Injury

### Why It Matters

Pelvic fractures may be associated with injuries to:

- urethra;
- bladder;
- kidneys;
- ureters;
- external genitalia;
- and surrounding soft tissues.

The presence of one injury should increase vigilance for associated injuries.

### Urethral Injury

Possible features include:

- blood at the urethral meatus;
- inability to pass urine;
- perineal bruising;
- scrotal or penile swelling;
- pelvic fracture;
- and a high-riding or abnormal prostate on examination where relevant.

The absence of these findings does not reliably exclude urethral injury.

### Catheterisation Considerations

The team should consider the possibility of urethral injury before attempting urinary catheterisation in a patient with concerning clinical features.

If urethral injury is suspected, appropriate specialist assessment should be obtained and the local trauma/urology pathway followed.

The key principle is:

> **Do not allow the need for urinary drainage to result in avoidable worsening of a suspected urethral injury.**

### Blood at the Meatus

Blood at the urethral meatus following pelvic trauma is an important warning sign.

The finding should prompt careful consideration of urethral injury and appropriate further evaluation before routine urethral catheterisation.

### Bladder Injury

Bladder injury may occur with pelvic trauma.

Possible clues include:

- haematuria;
- suprapubic pain;
- difficulty voiding;
- abdominal distension;
- pelvic fracture;
- and associated perineal injury.

The severity and pattern of injury determine the appropriate investigation and management pathway.

## Renal and Ureteric Injury

### Consider the Upper Urinary Tract

High-energy trauma can also injure the kidneys or ureters.

Features that may increase suspicion include:

- flank trauma;
- flank bruising;
- haematuria;
- abdominal pain;
- lower rib injuries;
- and mechanism suggesting significant deceleration.

The absence of visible haematuria does not necessarily exclude significant renal injury.

### Integrate With the Whole Patient

Renal injury should be considered alongside:

- haemodynamic status;
- abdominal findings;
- associated injuries;
- urine findings;
- and imaging requirements.

The team should avoid allowing an isolated urinary finding to distract from more immediately life-threatening pathology.

## Neurological and Limb Assessment

Pelvic trauma may coexist with:

- lumbosacral plexus injury;
- peripheral nerve injury;
- vascular injury;
- and lower-limb fractures.

The neurological assessment should therefore include:

- motor function;
- sensation;
- distal perfusion;
- and comparison between limbs.

Any new neurological deficit should be communicated clearly and documented.

A deteriorating neurological state should trigger immediate reassessment rather than being attributed automatically to the pelvic injury.

## Distal Vascular Assessment

The leader should ensure that distal circulation is assessed in injured limbs.

This includes consideration of:

- pulses;
- capillary refill;
- skin temperature;
- colour;
- and neurological function.

A pelvic injury may coexist with vascular injury or severe lower-limb trauma.

Changes should be reassessed after:

- pelvic stabilisation;
- repositioning;
- reduction;
- or other interventions.

## Associated Abdominal Injury

Pelvic trauma and abdominal trauma frequently coexist.

The leader should remain alert for:

- abdominal tenderness;
- distension;
- peritonism;
- abdominal wall bruising;
- haemodynamic deterioration;
- and other evidence of intra-abdominal bleeding.

A pelvic binder or pelvic diagnosis should never create diagnostic anchoring.

If the patient's physiology is not explained adequately, the team must continue searching for another source.

## Imaging Strategy

### Imaging Should Answer a Clinical Question

The choice of imaging depends on:

- physiological stability;
- suspected injuries;
- local trauma protocols;
- available expertise;
- and whether imaging will alter management.

The leader should ask:

> **"What decision are we trying to make?"**

Examples include:

- Is there an unstable pelvic injury?
- Is there active haemorrhage?
- Is there associated abdominal injury?
- Is there genitourinary injury?
- Does the patient require operative or interventional treatment?

### Imaging in the Unstable Patient

An unstable patient should not be transported away from the resuscitation environment simply to obtain additional information unless the investigation is necessary, appropriate, and immediately actionable within the local trauma pathway.

If the clinical picture indicates the need for definitive intervention, the team should avoid unnecessary diagnostic delay.

### Imaging in the Stable Patient

A patient who is haemodynamically stable or demonstrates a sustained response to resuscitation may proceed to appropriate imaging.

The results should then be integrated with:

- clinical examination;
- physiological trajectory;
- laboratory findings;
- and the suspected injury pattern.

## The Trauma Team Leader's Role

The leader should maintain control of the overall process.

Key responsibilities include:

- recognising possible pelvic haemorrhage;
- avoiding repeated pelvic manipulation;
- ensuring appropriate stabilisation;
- coordinating haemorrhage resuscitation;
- identifying associated genitourinary injury;
- deciding whether further examination is safe;
- coordinating imaging;
- communicating with specialist teams;
- and maintaining a clear pathway towards definitive treatment.

The leader should ensure that the team does not confuse completion of an examination with completion of management.

## Communication

### Closed-Loop Communication

Important findings should be communicated clearly.

For example:

> **"There is blood at the urethral meatus. Do not insert a urethral catheter until the urethral injury pathway has been reviewed."**

Or:

> **"The patient remains hypotensive despite haemorrhage resuscitation and pelvic stabilisation. We need definitive haemorrhage control."**

The receiving specialist should understand:

- mechanism;
- suspected injury;
- current physiology;
- interventions already performed;
- response to treatment;
- and the next required step.

## Common Leadership Errors

### Repeated Pelvic Manipulation

Repeatedly testing pelvic movement can cause unnecessary movement and does not provide a reliable basis for ongoing management.

**Better approach:**

Recognise the mechanism and clinical pattern, stabilise appropriately, and proceed with definitive assessment.

### Anchoring on the Pelvis

A pelvic fracture may be obvious but may not be the only cause of shock.

**Better approach:**

Continue searching systematically for thoracic, abdominal, external, and other sources of haemorrhage.

### Ignoring Genitourinary Clues

Blood at the urethral meatus, perineal bruising, or urinary difficulty may be dismissed as minor associated findings.

**Better approach:**

Treat these findings as potential indicators of significant associated injury.

### Routine Catheterisation Without Considering Urethral Injury

Urinary catheterisation is often routine during major trauma resuscitation.

However, concerning urethral findings should trigger appropriate evaluation before routine urethral catheterisation.

### Sending an Unstable Patient for Non-Essential Imaging

Diagnostic certainty is valuable, but it should not come at the cost of delaying definitive haemorrhage control.

**Better approach:**

Ask whether the patient is stable enough for the investigation and whether the result will change immediate management.

### Failure to Reassess

The patient's response after pelvic stabilisation or haemorrhage resuscitation provides important information.

**Better approach:**

Use:

**Intervention → Response → Reassessment → Escalation**

## Practical Trauma Team Checklist

### Suspected Major Pelvic Injury

- Consider the mechanism.
- Assess haemodynamic status.
- Look for associated external and perineal injury.
- Avoid repeated pelvic manipulation.
- Apply pelvic stabilisation when indicated by the clinical situation and local protocol.
- Activate major haemorrhage management when appropriate.
- Reassess after intervention.
- Search for other sources of bleeding.
- Consider definitive haemorrhage control early.

### Suspected Genitourinary Injury

- Examine the perineum.
- Look for blood at the urethral meatus.
- Assess for genital or perineal injury.
- Consider urinary retention or difficulty voiding.
- Look for haematuria.
- Consider urethral injury before catheterisation when concerning findings are present.
- Involve the appropriate specialist pathway.
- Do not allow urinary management to distract from haemorrhage control.

## Decision Framework

When confronted with major pelvic trauma, the leader should repeatedly ask:

### 1. Is the patient physiologically stable?

If no, prioritise resuscitation and definitive haemorrhage control.

### 2. Is major pelvic haemorrhage likely?

If yes, stabilise appropriately and activate the relevant haemorrhage pathway.

### 3. Is there another source of shock?

Continue assessment for chest, abdominal, external, long-bone, and other sources of haemorrhage.

### 4. Is there evidence of genitourinary injury?

If yes, adapt the examination and urinary management accordingly.

### 5. Is the patient stable enough for imaging?

If no, do not allow imaging to create avoidable delay.

### 6. What is the definitive treatment pathway?

The leader should identify early whether the patient requires:

- theatre;
- interventional radiology;
- specialist urological management;
- orthopaedic intervention;
- critical care;
- or transfer.

## Clinical Scenario — Revisited

The 29-year-old patient remains hypotensive despite initial blood product resuscitation.

A pelvic binder is applied appropriately.

The patient's blood pressure improves only transiently.

During examination, the team identifies perineal bruising and blood at the urethral meatus.

The Trauma Team Leader immediately recognises two important issues:

1. ongoing haemorrhage remains a major concern;
2. urethral injury must be considered before routine urethral catheterisation.

The leader communicates:

> **"This patient has suspected major pelvic haemorrhage with evidence of associated genitourinary injury. Continue haemorrhage resuscitation, maintain pelvic stabilisation, avoid unnecessary urethral instrumentation, and activate the definitive pelvic haemorrhage and urological pathways."**

The team then reassesses the patient and coordinates the next step according to local trauma protocols and available definitive treatment.

The important lesson is that the pelvic examination has not become an endpoint.

It has changed the team's understanding of the injury pattern and influenced the immediate management pathway.

## Clinical Pearl

> **Pelvic trauma is not simply an orthopaedic problem. In the unstable trauma patient, it may represent a major haemorrhage problem with associated genitourinary and multisystem injury.**

## Leadership Pearl

> **Do not repeatedly manipulate the pelvis, do not anchor on the pelvic fracture as the only source of shock, and do not allow diagnostic investigation to delay definitive haemorrhage control.**

## Final Take-Home Principles

1. Significant pelvic injury may cause life-threatening internal haemorrhage without obvious external bleeding.
2. Mechanism, physiology, examination, and trajectory must be interpreted together.
3. Avoid repeated manual manipulation of a potentially unstable pelvis.
4. Pelvic stabilisation may be an important early intervention when major pelvic injury is suspected.
5. Persistent instability requires continued assessment for ongoing haemorrhage and other sources of shock.
6. Perineal and genital examination may reveal important associated injuries.
7. Blood at the urethral meatus should raise concern for urethral injury.
8. Suspected urethral injury should influence the approach to urinary catheterisation.
9. Imaging should answer a clinical question and should not unnecessarily delay definitive treatment.
10. The Trauma Team Leader must continuously link temporary resuscitation with the pathway to definitive haemorrhage control.

## Summary

Pelvic trauma requires the Trauma Team Leader to think beyond the obvious fracture.

The central challenge is to recognise potentially catastrophic internal haemorrhage while simultaneously identifying associated injuries, particularly genitourinary and perineal trauma.

The leader should avoid repeated pelvic manipulation, ensure appropriate stabilisation, coordinate haemorrhage resuscitation, and reassess the patient's response.

Genitourinary findings should be actively sought because they may alter immediate management.

Above all, the team must maintain focus on physiology and definitive treatment.

The key sequence is:

**Recognise → Stabilise → Resuscitate → Reassess → Identify associated injury → Control haemorrhage definitively**
