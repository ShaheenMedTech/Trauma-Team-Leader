# 19. Managing the Team During Crisis

## Introduction

Trauma team leadership is most visible when the situation becomes difficult.

A well-prepared team can perform efficiently during a straightforward resuscitation. The true test of leadership, however, comes when the patient deteriorates unexpectedly, several critical problems appear simultaneously, information is incomplete, the team is becoming overloaded, and the margin for error is narrowing.

At this point, the trauma team leader must do more than make clinical decisions.

The leader must manage the **system**.

That system includes:

- the patient,
- the team,
- information,
- time,
- competing priorities,
- available resources,
- the environment,
- and the trajectory of the resuscitation.

Crisis management is therefore not simply about knowing what to do next.

It is about ensuring that the **right problem is recognised, the right intervention is prioritised, the right person performs it, the result is communicated, and the team remains coordinated while the situation evolves.**

The central principle is:

> **When the situation becomes more complex, leadership must become more structured.**

---

## 19.1 What Makes a Trauma Resuscitation a Crisis?

Not every difficult trauma is a crisis.

A crisis occurs when the normal capacity of the team or system is being exceeded, or is at imminent risk of being exceeded.

Typical features include:

- rapidly changing physiology,
- multiple simultaneous life-threatening problems,
- uncertainty about the diagnosis,
- insufficient information,
- limited time,
- competing priorities,
- resource limitations,
- unexpected deterioration,
- failure of an anticipated intervention,
- communication breakdown,
- disagreement within the team,
- or loss of situational awareness.

A crisis may be obvious.

For example:

> The patient suddenly becomes profoundly hypotensive and loses consciousness.

But it may also develop gradually.

For example:

> The patient has persistent tachycardia, increasing oxygen requirement, worsening metabolic disturbance, increasing blood loss, and progressively slower responses despite apparently adequate initial treatment.

The second situation can be more dangerous because the team may continue operating according to the original plan even though the clinical situation has changed.

The leader must therefore continuously ask:

> **"Has this become a different problem from the one we started with?"**

---

# 19.2 The First Leadership Response to a Crisis

When a crisis develops, the leader should avoid immediately becoming absorbed in the technical details.

The first task is to regain control of the situation.

A useful sequence is:

1. Recognise the change.
2. State it aloud.
3. Pause non-essential activity.
4. Reassess the patient.
5. Identify the immediate threat.
6. Establish priorities.
7. Allocate tasks.
8. Confirm critical actions.
9. Reassess the response.
10. Anticipate the next problem.

This does not necessarily require a prolonged pause.

In a genuine emergency, the sequence may take only a few seconds.

The important point is that the team understands that the situation has changed.

A simple statement may be enough:

> "Team, this is a deterioration. Stop and reassess."

Or:

> "We have a new problem. The patient is becoming unstable."

Or:

> "We are losing the patient. I need a structured reassessment."

The leader is deliberately creating a shared mental model.

---

# 19.3 The Crisis Reset

A useful technique during major deterioration is the **crisis reset**.

The purpose is not to restart the entire resuscitation.

The purpose is to reset the team's attention.

The leader briefly establishes:

### Where are we?

What is the patient's current physiological state?

### What has changed?

What new finding or deterioration triggered the crisis?

### What is immediately life-threatening?

Which problem will cause death first if untreated?

### What are we doing now?

Which interventions are currently underway?

### What are we waiting for?

Are investigations, blood products, procedures, theatre, imaging, specialist support, or transport delaying definitive treatment?

### What could happen next?

What is the most likely next deterioration?

A concise reset might sound like:

> "Current problem: worsening shock. We have ongoing haemorrhage until proven otherwise. Continue blood products, reassess response, and prepare for definitive haemorrhage control. Anaesthesia, update me on airway and ventilation. Surgery, I need your plan for control of bleeding. Nurse, confirm blood availability."

This is leadership through structure.

---

# 19.4 Stop the Cognitive Drift

During prolonged resuscitation, teams can become anchored to the original diagnosis.

This is particularly dangerous in trauma.

The initial working diagnosis may have been:

- blunt abdominal trauma,
- chest trauma,
- isolated head injury,
- pelvic injury,
- extremity haemorrhage,
- or a combination of injuries.

But the patient's physiology may later reveal that the dominant problem is different.

The leader must therefore distinguish between:

**What we thought was happening**

and

**What is happening now.**

A useful question is:

> "What evidence do we have that our current working diagnosis is still correct?"

If the answer is weak, the team should reconsider.

---

# 19.5 The Leader Must See the Whole System

During a crisis, individual team members naturally focus on their own tasks.

The airway clinician focuses on the airway.

The proceduralist focuses on the procedure.

The nurse focuses on medications, blood products, monitoring, and equipment.

The surgical team focuses on operative control.

The radiology team focuses on imaging.

The trauma team leader must focus on the **whole picture**.

This is one of the most important distinctions between leadership and technical performance.

The leader should continuously maintain awareness of:

- airway,
- breathing,
- circulation,
- neurological status,
- exposure,
- haemorrhage control,
- blood products,
- procedures,
- investigations,
- response to treatment,
- destination,
- available resources,
- and the team's performance.

The leader should not attempt to personally perform all of these tasks.

The leader must ensure that they are being performed.

---

# 19.6 Do Not Become the Most Useful Technician

One of the most common leadership failures during crisis is the leader becoming involved in a technical task.

This can happen because the leader is highly skilled.

The leader may think:

> "I can do this faster myself."

Sometimes that is true.

But the question is not simply whether the leader can perform the task faster.

The question is:

> **"Who is best placed to perform this task while maintaining overall control of the resuscitation?"**

If the leader becomes occupied with:

- inserting a line,
- performing a procedure,
- preparing equipment,
- obtaining a blood sample,
- reviewing every image personally,
- or managing a single technical problem,

the wider team may lose central coordination.

The leader should therefore remain cognitively above the immediate task whenever possible.

---

# 19.7 When the Leader Should Take Direct Action

There are situations in which the leader must become directly involved.

Examples include:

- an immediate life-threatening procedure with no competent alternative,
- a procedure that cannot safely be delegated,
- failure of the assigned operator,
- unexpected technical difficulty,
- imminent loss of airway or circulation,
- or a situation in which the leader's intervention is the fastest route to definitive treatment.

Even then, the leader should explicitly communicate the change.

For example:

> "I am taking over the airway. Anaesthesia, continue monitoring and prepare the next equipment."

This prevents uncertainty about who is responsible.

Once the immediate crisis is controlled, leadership should return to the wider team.

---

# 19.8 Crisis Communication

Communication during crisis should become:

- shorter,
- clearer,
- more specific,
- more structured,
- and more explicit.

Long explanations are rarely useful when the team is overloaded.

The leader should communicate:

**Who → What → Why → When**

For example:

> "Ahmed, give 1 gram tranexamic acid now because we have major haemorrhage."

Or:

> "Anaesthesia, prepare for definitive airway now."

Or:

> "Nurse, activate the massive transfusion protocol and confirm when the next cooler arrives."

Avoid vague instructions such as:

> "Someone get blood."

Instead:

> "Sarah, activate the massive transfusion protocol now and tell me when blood is ready."

Specificity reduces ambiguity.

---

# 19.9 Closed-Loop Communication During Crisis

Closed-loop communication becomes particularly important when cognitive load is high.

The sequence is:

1. Leader gives an instruction.
2. Recipient acknowledges it.
3. Recipient performs the task.
4. Recipient reports completion or the result.
5. Leader confirms that the information has been received.

Example:

> Leader: "Give 100 micrograms of phenylephrine."

> Nurse: "100 micrograms phenylephrine."

> Nurse: "Given."

> Leader: "Acknowledged."

The same principle applies to major clinical decisions.

> Leader: "Prepare the patient for theatre."

> Surgeon: "Preparing for theatre."

> Surgeon: "Theatre is ready."

> Leader: "Understood. We are proceeding."

This creates a shared information loop.

---

# 19.10 Escalation Must Be Explicit

During crisis, teams can lose valuable time because everyone assumes someone else has escalated the problem.

The leader should make escalation explicit.

Examples:

> "Call the consultant now."

> "I need anaesthesia consultant support."

> "Activate the massive transfusion protocol."

> "Notify theatre that this is an emergency."

> "Call vascular surgery."

> "We need senior radiology input now."

The instruction should identify:

- who,
- what,
- and the required urgency.

---

# 19.11 Resource Management

A crisis can quickly become a resource problem.

Resources include:

- people,
- equipment,
- blood products,
- operating theatre,
- interventional radiology,
- imaging,
- transport,
- specialist expertise,
- and time.

The leader must identify resource requirements early.

A useful question is:

> **"What resource will we need in the next ten minutes?"**

Not:

> "What do we need right now?"

Anticipatory resource management prevents delays.

Examples:

If the patient is becoming unstable:

> "Prepare theatre now."

If massive haemorrhage is likely:

> "Ensure blood products are continuously available."

If difficult airway management is anticipated:

> "Bring the difficult airway equipment before we need it."

If transfer is likely:

> "Contact the receiving centre early."

---

# 19.12 Managing Multiple Simultaneous Problems

Trauma crises rarely present as a single problem.

The team may simultaneously face:

- airway compromise,
- tension pneumothorax,
- massive haemorrhage,
- traumatic brain injury,
- hypothermia,
- coagulopathy,
- and logistical delay.

The leader must avoid trying to solve everything simultaneously.

Instead:

### Identify the dominant threat.

What will kill the patient first?

### Identify threats that can be delegated.

Which problems can another team member manage independently?

### Identify threats that require coordination.

Which problems require multiple specialties?

### Establish sequence.

Which intervention must happen before another?

### Reassess continuously.

Has the priority changed?

---

# 19.13 Parallel Processing

An effective trauma team should work in parallel whenever possible.

For example:

While one clinician manages the airway:

- another assesses breathing,
- another establishes vascular access,
- another prepares blood products,
- another obtains relevant history,
- another performs ultrasound,
- another contacts the appropriate specialty team.

The leader coordinates these activities.

The leader should avoid converting a parallel system into a sequential one.

A common failure is:

> "Let's finish the airway first, then we'll think about circulation."

In a severely injured patient, this may waste critical time.

The correct approach may be:

> "Airway is being managed. Continue haemorrhage control and prepare blood simultaneously."

---

# 19.14 Managing Team Overload

Crisis increases cognitive load.

As cognitive load increases:

- memory becomes less reliable,
- attention narrows,
- communication becomes less precise,
- errors become more likely,
- and people may lose awareness of the wider situation.

The leader must therefore reduce unnecessary cognitive demand.

Useful strategies include:

- using names,
- giving one clear instruction at a time,
- avoiding unnecessary conversation,
- repeating critical information,
- writing down important times or doses,
- using checklists,
- verbalising priorities,
- and periodically summarising the situation.

The leader should also avoid introducing unnecessary complexity.

---

# 19.15 Managing Noise

Trauma resuscitation areas can become extremely noisy.

Multiple people may speak simultaneously.

Equipment alarms may sound.

Telephone calls may occur.

Additional staff may enter.

Family members or non-essential personnel may create distractions.

The leader should actively control the environment.

For example:

> "Only essential communication now."

Or:

> "One person speaking at a time."

Or:

> "I need the room quiet for the next decision."

This is not authoritarian behaviour for its own sake.

It is a patient-safety intervention.

---

# 19.16 The Leader Should Control the Information Flow

Information should reach the person who needs it.

Important information should not remain buried in individual conversations.

If a critical finding is identified, it should be communicated to the team.

For example:

> "FAST is positive."

Not:

> "Doctor, can I speak to you for a moment?"

The leader should encourage important information to be verbalised.

A useful rule is:

> **If it changes management, say it aloud.**

---

# 19.17 Managing Conflicting Information

During trauma resuscitation, information may conflict.

One clinician may report:

> "The patient is improving."

Another may say:

> "The blood pressure is falling."

The monitor may show one thing while the clinical examination suggests another.

The leader should not immediately choose the information that fits the current hypothesis.

Instead:

1. Identify the conflict.
2. Verify the data.
3. Reassess the patient.
4. Determine which information is reliable.
5. Update the working diagnosis.

A useful phrase is:

> "We have conflicting information. Let's verify it before we act."

---

# 19.18 Managing Disagreement

Disagreement is not necessarily a leadership failure.

Constructive disagreement can prevent errors.

The leader should create an environment in which team members can challenge a decision.

Examples:

> "If anyone sees a problem with this plan, speak up now."

Or:

> "Does anyone have a different interpretation?"

However, disagreement should not become uncontrolled debate.

The leader must eventually decide.

A useful structure is:

> "I hear the concern. Our immediate priority is haemorrhage control. We will proceed with this plan and reassess after the next intervention."

This acknowledges the concern while maintaining direction.

---

# 19.19 The Two-Challenge Principle

A team member who believes a critical patient-safety problem is being missed should be encouraged to speak up.

If the concern is not acknowledged, it should be repeated clearly.

For example:

> "I am concerned that the patient remains hypotensive despite treatment."

If there is still no response:

> "I am seriously concerned that we are missing ongoing haemorrhage."

The leader should recognise this as a safety signal rather than insubordination.

A strong leader responds:

> "Thank you. Let's reassess the source of bleeding."

---

# 19.20 Psychological Safety During Crisis

Team members must feel able to report:

- deterioration,
- errors,
- uncertainty,
- equipment problems,
- failed interventions,
- and disagreement.

A culture in which people are afraid to speak creates dangerous silence.

The leader should model this behaviour.

Useful phrases include:

> "If you are worried, tell me."

> "I may miss something; speak up."

> "If this is not working, tell me immediately."

> "We need to know when something has failed."

This improves information flow.

---

# 19.21 Managing Error During Resuscitation

Errors can occur even in highly experienced teams.

The leader's response matters.

If an error occurs, the first priority is:

> **Protect the patient.**

Then:

1. Recognise the error.
2. Correct it.
3. Communicate the correction.
4. Assess the consequence.
5. Prevent recurrence.
6. Document appropriately.
7. Debrief later.

Avoid wasting critical time assigning blame during the resuscitation.

For example:

> "The wrong medication was given. Stop further doses, assess the patient, and prepare the appropriate treatment."

This is more useful than:

> "Who gave that?"

Accountability matters.

But during an active crisis, patient safety comes first.

---

# 19.22 Managing Failure of an Intervention

A common crisis pattern is:

> Intervention → no improvement → repeated same intervention.

The leader must recognise treatment failure.

Ask:

> "What response did we expect?"

> "Did we get it?"

> "If not, why not?"

> "Is the diagnosis wrong?"

> "Is the intervention inadequate?"

> "Is the problem ongoing?"

> "Do we need definitive treatment?"

Failure should trigger reassessment, not automatic repetition.

---

# 19.23 The No-Response Signal

A useful mental trigger is:

> **No expected response = new information.**

If a hypotensive patient does not respond as expected:

Do not simply give more of the same treatment.

Reassess.

If an airway intervention does not improve oxygenation:

Do not simply continue repeating the same manoeuvre.

Reassess.

If ventilation remains inadequate:

Consider:

- equipment failure,
- airway position,
- obstruction,
- pneumothorax,
- inadequate ventilation,
- incorrect diagnosis,
- or another cause.

The absence of response is itself a clinical finding.

---

# 19.24 Managing the Deteriorating Patient

When the patient deteriorates, the leader should verbalise the change.

For example:

> "Blood pressure is falling despite resuscitation."

Then:

> "We need to identify the cause now."

The leader should rapidly reconsider:

- airway,
- breathing,
- circulation,
- haemorrhage,
- obstruction,
- cardiac causes,
- neurological deterioration,
- medication effects,
- equipment failure,
- and environmental factors.

The exact differential depends on the clinical context.

The leadership principle remains constant:

> **Deterioration requires reassessment, not assumption.**

---

# 19.25 The Human Factors of Crisis

Human factors become increasingly important as the situation deteriorates.

Common cognitive traps include:

### Anchoring

Fixating on the initial diagnosis.

### Confirmation bias

Looking only for evidence supporting the existing diagnosis.

### Premature closure

Stopping the diagnostic search too early.

### Action bias

Doing something simply because action feels preferable to uncertainty.

### Omission bias

Failing to act because intervention feels risky.

### Authority gradient

Failing to challenge a senior clinician.

### Normalisation of deviance

Accepting abnormal findings because they have been present for some time.

### Fixation error

Becoming excessively focused on one task.

The leader should actively counter these biases.

---

# 19.26 The Leader's Cognitive Position

During crisis, the leader should maintain three simultaneous levels of attention.

### Level 1: The patient

What is happening physiologically?

### Level 2: The team

Who is doing what?

Is anyone overloaded?

Is communication functioning?

### Level 3: The system

What resources are available?

What will happen next?

Where is the patient going?

What delays could threaten the plan?

Strong leadership requires movement between these levels.

---

# 19.27 The 30-Second Team Summary

During prolonged or complex resuscitation, a brief summary can restore shared awareness.

The leader might say:

> "Thirty-second summary."

Then state:

1. mechanism,
2. major injuries,
3. current physiological status,
4. interventions completed,
5. response,
6. unresolved threats,
7. current priority,
8. next destination.

For example:

> "High-speed collision with suspected chest and abdominal trauma. Airway secured. Oxygenation currently adequate. Persistent shock despite blood products. Positive abdominal ultrasound. Pelvic binder in place. Ongoing haemorrhage remains the major concern. Surgical control is required. We are preparing for theatre."

This takes seconds but can realign the entire team.

---

# 19.28 The Crisis Briefing

When the situation becomes particularly complex, the leader can give a short crisis briefing.

A useful structure is:

### Situation

What is happening?

### Assessment

What do we believe is causing it?

### Priority

What must happen first?

### Plan

What are we doing?

### Roles

Who is responsible?

### Contingency

What will we do if this fails?

### Destination

Where is the patient going?

Example:

> "We have persistent haemorrhagic shock despite ongoing transfusion. We believe uncontrolled intra-abdominal bleeding is the leading problem. The priority is definitive haemorrhage control. Surgery will prepare for theatre. Anaesthesia will maintain the airway and resuscitation. Nursing will continue blood products and monitoring. If the patient deteriorates further, activate the next escalation pathway. We are going directly to theatre."

---

# 19.29 Managing the Team's Tempo

The leader must control the tempo of the resuscitation.

A team can be:

- too slow,
- too fast,
- or appropriately paced.

Too slow may result in:

- delayed intervention,
- delayed escalation,
- delayed transfer,
- and loss of physiological reserve.

Too fast may result in:

- poor communication,
- duplicated tasks,
- medication errors,
- missed findings,
- and inappropriate decisions.

The leader should aim for:

> **Fast enough to protect the patient, slow enough to remain controlled.**

---

# 19.30 When to Slow Down

The leader should deliberately slow the team when:

- the diagnosis is unclear,
- the team is acting on conflicting assumptions,
- a major decision is being made,
- multiple people are speaking simultaneously,
- an intervention has unexpectedly failed,
- or there is disagreement about the next step.

A short pause can prevent a major error.

For example:

> "Stop for a moment. We need to agree on the next priority."

---

# 19.31 When to Accelerate

The leader should increase urgency when:

- a time-critical intervention is delayed,
- haemorrhage is ongoing,
- airway or ventilation is failing,
- the patient is deteriorating,
- definitive treatment is available but not activated,
- or the current environment cannot safely manage the patient.

Use explicit language:

> "This is time critical."

> "We need definitive control now."

> "Do not delay transfer for non-essential investigations."

---

# 19.32 Managing the Transition to Definitive Care

The trauma team leader must recognise that resuscitation is not the destination.

The ultimate objective may be:

- operating theatre,
- interventional radiology,
- CT,
- intensive care,
- specialist centre,
- or another definitive treatment environment.

A common failure is continuing resuscitation in the emergency department when definitive treatment is already indicated.

The leader should repeatedly ask:

> **"What does this patient need that we cannot provide here?"**

The answer often determines the next step.

---

# 19.33 Avoiding the Emergency Department Trap

The trauma bay can become psychologically comfortable.

The team has:

- familiar equipment,
- familiar staff,
- monitoring,
- access to procedures,
- and a sense of control.

But the patient may require treatment elsewhere.

The leader must distinguish:

> **Stabilisation**

from

> **Definitive treatment.**

A patient does not always need to be completely normal before leaving the trauma bay.

The goal is often:

> **The safest achievable physiological state for the next definitive intervention.**

---

# 19.34 The Handover as Part of Crisis Management

A crisis does not end when the patient leaves the trauma bay.

The leader must ensure continuity of information.

A critical handover should include:

- mechanism,
- major injuries,
- current physiological state,
- airway status,
- ventilation,
- circulation,
- blood products,
- medications,
- procedures,
- response,
- outstanding concerns,
- and planned next steps.

The leader should identify the person receiving the handover.

Avoid:

> "This is the trauma patient."

Instead:

> "This is a high-energy blunt trauma patient with ongoing haemorrhagic shock requiring definitive operative control."

---

# 19.35 Confirm the Receiving Team Understands the Situation

A handover should not be considered complete merely because information was spoken.

The receiving team should understand:

- the current problem,
- the immediate priority,
- and the anticipated next step.

A useful closing question is:

> "Is there anything you need clarified before we proceed?"

Or:

> "Our major concern is ongoing haemorrhage. Are you ready to receive the patient?"

---

# 19.36 Managing the Final Minutes of Resuscitation

The final minutes before transfer are high-risk.

The team may become distracted by logistics.

The leader should maintain clinical focus.

Before transfer, confirm:

- airway secure,
- oxygen and ventilation available,
- monitoring attached,
- vascular access functioning,
- blood products arranged if required,
- medications available,
- critical documentation complete,
- imaging available,
- receiving team aware,
- transport personnel ready,
- and destination confirmed.

The leader should not assume that another person has checked these issues.

---

# 19.37 Crisis Management Checklist

A concise crisis checklist can be remembered as:

## R — Recognise

What has changed?

## S — State

Say the deterioration aloud.

## A — Assess

What is happening now?

## P — Prioritise

What will kill the patient first?

## D — Delegate

Who will manage each problem?

## C — Communicate

Use clear, closed-loop instructions.

## E — Escalate

Call for additional expertise or resources.

## R — Reassess

Did the intervention work?

## A — Anticipate

What will happen next?

## T — Transfer

What definitive treatment does the patient need?

This creates the sequence:

> **Recognise → State → Assess → Prioritise → Delegate → Communicate → Escalate → Reassess → Anticipate → Transfer**

---

# 19.38 The Leader Should Reassess the Team, Not Only the Patient

A subtle but important leadership principle is that the team itself is a clinical resource.

The leader should watch for:

- confusion,
- repeated questions,
- missed instructions,
- duplicated tasks,
- silence,
- excessive noise,
- delayed responses,
- interpersonal tension,
- fatigue,
- and fixation.

These may indicate that the team's cognitive capacity is becoming overloaded.

The appropriate response may be to:

- simplify the plan,
- redistribute tasks,
- call for additional personnel,
- pause non-essential activity,
- or explicitly reset the team.

---

# 19.39 Recognising Team Saturation

A team is approaching saturation when:

- every member appears occupied,
- tasks are accumulating,
- important information is not being communicated,
- new requests are not being acknowledged,
- and no one appears to be monitoring the overall situation.

At this point:

> **Adding another task is unlikely to solve the problem.**

The leader may need to add another person.

This is why escalation should occur before complete overload.

---

# 19.40 Do Not Wait for Failure Before Escalating

Escalation should be proactive.

Do not wait until:

- the airway is completely lost,
- blood products are exhausted,
- the patient arrests,
- theatre is unavailable,
- or the team is overwhelmed.

Instead:

> "This patient is deteriorating and may require massive transfusion and operative control. I am escalating now."

Early escalation preserves options.

Late escalation removes them.

---

# 19.41 Managing External Pressure

During major trauma, the team may experience pressure from:

- relatives,
- administrators,
- police,
- other clinical teams,
- bed management,
- imaging availability,
- theatre capacity,
- transfer arrangements,
- or time constraints.

The leader must protect the clinical priorities of the resuscitation.

A useful principle is:

> **Urgency outside the trauma bay must not replace clinical prioritisation inside it.**

Communication with external teams should be delegated where possible.

For example:

> "Please update the family while we continue resuscitation."

Or:

> "Please coordinate the transfer and report back when transport is confirmed."

---

# 19.42 Managing Family Presence

Families may be distressed and may seek information during the resuscitation.

The team should have a defined approach.

Where appropriate, a designated staff member can:

- communicate with the family,
- explain what is happening,
- obtain relevant information,
- and provide updates.

The trauma team leader should generally avoid being diverted from clinical leadership unless circumstances require it.

---

# 19.43 Leadership Under Uncertainty

Trauma leadership often occurs without complete information.

The leader may not know:

- the exact injuries,
- the total blood loss,
- the full medical history,
- the final diagnosis,
- or the response to the next intervention.

Waiting for certainty can be dangerous.

The leader should make decisions based on:

- available evidence,
- physiology,
- mechanism,
- clinical trajectory,
- probability,
- and risk.

The key is to remain willing to revise the decision.

A good leader can say:

> "This is our current working diagnosis. We will reassess as new information arrives."

---

# 19.44 Decisions Are Time-Bounded Hypotheses

A useful mental model is:

> **A decision is a hypothesis that must be tested against the patient's response.**

For example:

> "We believe haemorrhage is causing the shock."

Intervention follows.

Then:

> "Did the patient respond?"

If yes, continue and reassess.

If no:

> "Our hypothesis may be incomplete or incorrect."

This prevents attachment to previous decisions.

---

# 19.45 Avoiding Leadership Lock-In

Once a leader announces a plan publicly, there can be psychological pressure to defend it.

This is dangerous.

The leader should be comfortable changing direction.

For example:

> "Our initial plan was CT, but the patient is now unstable. We are changing the plan and proceeding to definitive haemorrhage control."

Changing a plan in response to new evidence is not weakness.

It is adaptive leadership.

---

# 19.46 The Importance of Speaking the Change Aloud

When the plan changes, say so.

Otherwise different members of the team may continue following different plans.

Use:

> "Change of plan."

Then explain:

1. what changed,
2. why,
3. what happens now.

Example:

> "Change of plan. The patient is becoming unstable despite resuscitation. We are no longer proceeding to CT. We are going directly to theatre."

This is a powerful coordination tool.

---

# 19.47 Managing Unexpected Findings

Unexpected findings are common.

Examples include:

- a positive ultrasound when bleeding was not initially suspected,
- a deteriorating airway,
- an unexpected neurological deficit,
- a previously unrecognised pelvic injury,
- a medication history that changes management,
- or a new physiological abnormality.

The leader should avoid immediately treating the finding in isolation.

Ask:

> "How does this finding change the overall picture?"

Then:

> "Does it change our priority?"

---

# 19.48 Managing the "One More Test" Problem

Additional investigations can be useful.

But in an unstable patient, the leader must ask whether the result will actually change management.

Before ordering another test:

> "If this result is positive, what will we do?"

> "If it is negative, what will we do?"

If the answer is:

> "The same thing either way,"

the investigation may not be appropriate at that moment.

This is particularly important when the test delays definitive treatment.

---

# 19.49 The Leader's Responsibility for Time

Time is a clinical resource.

The leader should know:

- when the patient arrived,
- when major deterioration occurred,
- when key interventions were performed,
- when blood products were started,
- when specialists were called,
- when definitive treatment was activated,
- and when transfer occurred.

The exact requirements depend on local policy.

The leadership principle is:

> **Know where the critical minutes are going.**

---

# 19.50 Time Traps

Common time traps include:

- repeated unsuccessful attempts,
- prolonged discussion,
- unnecessary investigations,
- waiting for a specialist who is not responding,
- repeated requests for the same equipment,
- delayed activation of protocols,
- and continuing an ineffective treatment.

The leader should periodically ask:

> "What are we waiting for?"

If the answer is unclear, investigate the delay.

---

# 19.51 When a Plan Is Not Working

A useful rule is:

> **If the patient is not improving, the plan must be questioned.**

The leader should ask:

1. What was the intended effect?
2. Did it occur?
3. If not, what are the possible explanations?
4. What is the next best intervention?
5. Who can perform it?
6. What resources are required?
7. How quickly can it happen?

This creates a deliberate response to treatment failure.

---

# 19.52 The Leader's Voice

During crisis, the leader's voice becomes a stabilising element.

The goal is not to sound emotionless.

The goal is to sound:

- clear,
- calm,
- confident,
- decisive,
- and appropriately urgent.

Avoid:

> "Oh no, the pressure is dropping!"

Prefer:

> "The blood pressure is falling. We have worsening shock. Prepare the next intervention now."

The second statement communicates urgency without creating panic.

---

# 19.53 Emotional Regulation

The leader may experience:

- fear,
- uncertainty,
- frustration,
- anger,
- or pressure.

These emotions are normal.

The leader must prevent them from disrupting team function.

Useful strategies include:

- deliberate breathing,
- slowing speech,
- using structured language,
- returning to priorities,
- and relying on established protocols.

The objective is not to eliminate emotion.

It is to prevent emotion from controlling behaviour.

---

# 19.54 Managing Anger and Frustration

Frustration is common when:

- equipment is unavailable,
- a procedure fails,
- a team member does not respond,
- blood is delayed,
- or the patient deteriorates unexpectedly.

The leader should redirect frustration into action.

Instead of:

> "Why is the blood still not here?"

Use:

> "What is the current blood delivery status, and what is the fastest alternative?"

Instead of:

> "Why hasn't theatre been called?"

Use:

> "Confirm theatre activation now."

This keeps the team solution-focused.

---

# 19.55 The Crisis Leader Does Not Need to Know Everything

The leader may not know every answer.

Strong leadership does not require pretending otherwise.

The leader can say:

> "I don't know. Let's find out."

Or:

> "I need specialist input on this."

Or:

> "What am I missing?"

This demonstrates controlled uncertainty.

It also encourages the team to contribute information.

---

# 19.56 Asking for Help Is a Leadership Skill

Escalation is not an admission of failure.

It is a resource-management decision.

The leader should escalate when:

- the case exceeds local expertise,
- the physiological situation is worsening,
- the required intervention is unavailable,
- multiple competing problems exceed team capacity,
- or definitive treatment requires another service.

The best leaders recognise their limits early.

---

# 19.57 Managing the Team After the Crisis

Once the immediate crisis has been controlled, the leader should deliberately transition from crisis mode.

Say:

> "The patient is currently stabilised. Let's reset."

Then:

- reassess the patient,
- update the diagnosis,
- clarify the plan,
- redistribute tasks,
- review pending investigations,
- prepare for definitive care,
- and communicate the next objective.

This prevents the team from remaining in a high-intensity state unnecessarily.

---

# 19.58 The Post-Crisis Reset

A post-crisis reset can be brief.

Ask:

### What happened?

What triggered the deterioration?

### What did we do?

Which interventions were performed?

### Did they work?

What was the physiological response?

### What remains unresolved?

Which problems are still active?

### What happens next?

What is the definitive plan?

This restores structure.

---

# 19.59 Debriefing After a Major Crisis

A formal or informal debrief should be considered after significant events.

The purpose is not primarily to assign blame.

The purpose is to:

- understand what happened,
- identify what worked,
- identify what failed,
- identify system problems,
- support the team,
- and improve future performance.

A useful debrief structure is:

1. What went well?
2. What was difficult?
3. What surprised us?
4. What could we improve?
5. Is anyone concerned about the patient or the team?
6. Are there any equipment or system issues requiring follow-up?

---

# 19.60 The Leader Should Model Learning

If the leader makes an error, acknowledging it can strengthen the team.

For example:

> "I initially prioritised CT, but the patient's deterioration made that inappropriate. We changed course. That's something we should discuss afterwards."

This creates a culture in which learning is more important than protecting hierarchy.

---

# 19.61 Crisis Management: Common Failure Modes

## Failure Mode 1: Everyone Does Something

Without clear leadership, multiple tasks may be started while essential tasks remain incomplete.

### Correction

Assign named responsibilities.

---

## Failure Mode 2: Nobody Knows the Priority

The team is busy but not coordinated.

### Correction

State the immediate life threat explicitly.

---

## Failure Mode 3: The Leader Becomes a Technician

The leader becomes occupied with one procedure.

### Correction

Delegate and return to system-level oversight.

---

## Failure Mode 4: Instructions Are Vague

People assume someone else will act.

### Correction

Use names and explicit tasks.

---

## Failure Mode 5: Treatment Failure Is Ignored

The same intervention continues despite no response.

### Correction

Ask whether the expected physiological response occurred.

---

## Failure Mode 6: The Team Is Too Noisy

Important information is lost.

### Correction

Control the communication environment.

---

## Failure Mode 7: Escalation Is Delayed

The team waits until resources are exhausted.

### Correction

Escalate early when deterioration is anticipated.

---

## Failure Mode 8: The Original Plan Becomes Fixed

The team continues following a plan despite changing physiology.

### Correction

State and reassess the working diagnosis.

---

## Failure Mode 9: Disagreement Becomes Personal

Clinical disagreement becomes interpersonal conflict.

### Correction

Return discussion to patient priorities and evidence.

---

## Failure Mode 10: The Patient Is Stabilised but Not Definitively Treated

The team continues resuscitation without progressing to definitive care.

### Correction

Ask what the patient needs next and where it can be delivered.

---

# 19.62 Crisis Leadership Language

The following phrases are useful during difficult resuscitations.

### To reset the team

> "Stop for a moment. Let's reassess."

### To identify deterioration

> "This is a significant change."

### To establish priority

> "Our immediate priority is..."

### To delegate

> "Ahmed, you are responsible for..."

### To confirm an instruction

> "Please repeat that back to me."

### To escalate

> "We need senior support now."

### To challenge the plan

> "What evidence supports our current diagnosis?"

### To recognise failure

> "That intervention has not produced the expected response."

### To change direction

> "Change of plan."

### To control noise

> "One person speaking at a time."

### To encourage challenge

> "If you are concerned, say it now."

### To anticipate

> "What is most likely to happen next?"

### To transition

> "The immediate crisis is controlled. Let's reset and plan the next phase."

---

# 19.63 The Crisis Leadership Algorithm

A practical mental model is:

## 1. Detect

Recognise that the situation has changed.

## 2. Declare

Tell the team that the situation has changed.

## 3. Stabilise

Address immediate threats.

## 4. Structure

Re-establish priorities and roles.

## 5. Communicate

Use clear, closed-loop communication.

## 6. Escalate

Mobilise additional expertise and resources.

## 7. Reassess

Evaluate the response.

## 8. Reframe

Change the working diagnosis or plan if necessary.

## 9. Anticipate

Prepare for the next deterioration.

## 10. Transition

Move the patient toward definitive care.

---

# 19.64 The Crisis Leadership Checklist

Before moving on from a major crisis, the leader should be able to answer:

### Patient

- What is the current physiological state?
- What is the immediate threat?
- What has changed?

### Diagnosis

- What is our working diagnosis?
- What evidence supports it?
- What evidence contradicts it?

### Treatment

- What has been done?
- Did it work?
- What remains necessary?

### Team

- Who is doing what?
- Is anyone overloaded?
- Does anyone need help?

### Communication

- Have critical instructions been confirmed?
- Has important information been verbalised?
- Does everyone understand the current plan?

### Resources

- Do we have the people and equipment required?
- What resource will we need next?

### Escalation

- Has senior help been activated?
- Has the definitive team been contacted?

### Destination

- Where does the patient need to go?
- Is that destination ready?

### Contingency

- What will we do if the patient deteriorates further?

---

# 19.65 The Most Important Crisis Leadership Questions

When the situation becomes difficult, the leader can repeatedly return to a small set of questions:

> **What has changed?**

> **What is killing the patient now?**

> **What are we doing about it?**

> **Is it working?**

> **Who is responsible?**

> **What do we need next?**

> **What could happen next?**

> **Where does this patient need to go?**

These questions keep the leader oriented toward the patient rather than the noise surrounding the patient.

---

# 19.66 The Principle of Controlled Urgency

Trauma leadership requires urgency without chaos.

The leader should create:

**Urgency without panic.**

**Structure without rigidity.**

**Authority without intimidation.**

**Confidence without overconfidence.**

**Delegation without abandonment.**

**Reassessment without indecision.**

**Speed without loss of control.**

This balance is one of the defining characteristics of effective trauma leadership.

---

# 19.67 Final Principles

A trauma crisis is not managed by one heroic intervention.

It is managed through coordinated action.

The trauma team leader should:

1. Recognise deterioration early.
2. State the change clearly.
3. Re-establish the team's shared mental model.
4. Identify the immediate threat.
5. Prioritise interventions.
6. Delegate clearly.
7. Use closed-loop communication.
8. Control unnecessary noise.
9. Encourage appropriate challenge.
10. Escalate early.
11. Recognise treatment failure.
12. Reconsider the diagnosis when necessary.
13. Maintain awareness of the patient, team, and system.
14. Anticipate the next problem.
15. Protect definitive treatment pathways.
16. Transition deliberately from crisis mode.
17. Debrief after major events.
18. Learn from errors and near misses.

The leader's role during crisis is not to be the person who does everything.

It is to ensure that **everything necessary is being done, in the correct order, by the right people, with the right information, at the right time.**

---

# 19.68 The Core Message

> **When the trauma resuscitation becomes chaotic, the leader's first responsibility is to create structure.**

The leader sees the deterioration.

The leader names the problem.

The leader establishes the priority.

The leader allocates responsibility.

The team acts.

The leader evaluates the response.

The plan changes when the patient changes.

And the entire system remains focused on one objective:

> **Delivering the right patient to the right definitive treatment at the right time.**
