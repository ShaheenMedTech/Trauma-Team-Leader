# 29. Leading the Trauma Team in Mass-Casualty and Disaster Incidents

## Introduction

Mass-casualty and disaster incidents fundamentally change the problem faced by the trauma team leader.

In a conventional trauma resuscitation, the leader is primarily concerned with delivering the best possible care to one critically injured patient.

In a mass-casualty incident, the leader must think differently.

The central question becomes:

> **How can the team achieve the greatest overall benefit for the greatest number of patients, using the resources currently available?**

This does not mean that individual patients become less important.

It means that the leader must simultaneously manage:

- multiple injured patients
- competing clinical priorities
- limited personnel
- limited equipment
- constrained blood products
- restricted operating-theatre capacity
- competing requests for imaging
- overwhelmed emergency-department capacity
- incomplete information
- rapidly changing circumstances
- staff fatigue
- communication overload
- environmental hazards
- secondary casualties
- and the possibility that the incident will continue to generate new patients.

The trauma team leader therefore becomes not only a clinical leader but also a **systems leader**.

The leader must maintain a broad operational picture while ensuring that individual patients continue to receive appropriate assessment and treatment.

This chapter focuses on the leadership behaviours required when trauma care moves from a single-patient problem to a multi-patient, resource-constrained emergency.

---

# 29.1 The Fundamental Change: From Individual Care to Population-Level Prioritisation

The most important cognitive shift is understanding that mass-casualty leadership is not simply "normal trauma care with more patients."

It is a different operating environment.

In a single-patient trauma:

> One patient → one team → one priority sequence.

In a mass-casualty incident:

> Multiple patients → multiple teams → competing priorities → finite resources → continuously changing demand.

The leader must therefore answer several questions simultaneously:

1. How many patients are there?
2. How severely injured are they?
3. Who needs immediate intervention?
4. Who can safely wait?
5. Which resources are currently available?
6. Which resources are becoming limiting?
7. What will happen if another wave of patients arrives?
8. Which patients require transfer?
9. Which patients require definitive surgery?
10. Which patients can be managed locally?
11. Which patients may deteriorate while waiting?
12. What must the team do now to preserve future capacity?

This is why mass-casualty leadership requires a **system-level mental model**.

The leader must avoid becoming trapped inside one resuscitation bay while the wider incident deteriorates.

---

# 29.2 The First Principle: Establish Command Early

When a mass-casualty incident is recognised, leadership should become explicit.

The team should know:

- who is leading
- who is coordinating clinical operations
- who is responsible for triage
- who is coordinating resuscitation areas
- who is communicating with the wider hospital
- who is coordinating transfers
- who is managing blood products
- who is coordinating imaging
- and who is communicating with external agencies when required.

Ambiguous leadership creates duplicated decisions and competing priorities.

The trauma team leader should therefore establish:

> **"I am leading the clinical response. Please direct major clinical concerns and resource issues to me or the designated clinical coordinator."**

This does not mean that the leader personally controls every activity.

It means that the command structure is clear.

---

# 29.3 The Leader Must Think Beyond the Resuscitation Room

One of the most dangerous errors in a mass-casualty incident is allowing the resuscitation room to become the entire world.

The leader must deliberately maintain awareness of:

- the triage area
- the resuscitation area
- the imaging department
- the operating theatre
- the intensive care unit
- inpatient capacity
- blood bank availability
- transfer capacity
- ambulance access
- staff availability
- and the expected number of additional patients.

A patient may be clinically stable but operationally dangerous if that patient occupies the only available critical-care bed for several hours.

Similarly, a patient with major haemorrhage may require immediate access to an operating theatre even if another patient currently appears more dramatic.

The leader must therefore balance:

> **clinical urgency + resource requirements + future capacity.**

---

# 29.4 Activate the Major Incident Structure Early

If the incident meets local major-incident criteria, the appropriate institutional response should be activated promptly.

The trauma leader should not attempt to personally manage the entire response.

Instead, the leader should work within the established command structure.

Depending on the institution, this may include:

- hospital incident command
- emergency department leadership
- surgical leadership
- anaesthesia
- intensive care
- operating theatre coordination
- radiology
- blood bank
- laboratory services
- nursing leadership
- security
- portering
- communications
- facilities
- and external emergency services.

The exact structure varies between institutions.

The important leadership principle is universal:

> **Use the system that exists rather than creating an improvised parallel system.**

---

# 29.5 The Initial Situation Report

Before committing resources, the leader should obtain the clearest possible initial picture.

A useful situation report includes:

### Incident

- What happened?
- Where did it happen?
- Is the scene safe?
- Is the incident ongoing?
- Is there a possibility of secondary casualties?

### Patients

- How many patients are expected?
- How many have arrived?
- How many are critically injured?
- Are there children?
- Are there pregnant patients?
- Are there burns?
- Are there crush injuries?
- Are there penetrating injuries?
- Are there chemical, biological, radiological, or nuclear concerns?

### Resources

- How many clinicians are available?
- How many resuscitation bays are available?
- How much blood is available?
- How many operating theatres are available?
- What is the ICU capacity?
- Is imaging available?
- What transfer capacity exists?

### Time

- When are further patients expected?
- How quickly are ambulances arriving?
- How long will definitive treatment take?
- How long until resources become exhausted?

The leader does not need perfect information.

The leader needs **sufficient information to make the next safe decision**.

---

# 29.6 Triage Is a Leadership Function

Triage is not merely an administrative activity.

It is a dynamic clinical prioritisation process.

The leader must ensure that triage is:

- rapid
- systematic
- visible
- repeatedly reassessed
- linked to available resources
- and separated from emotional decision-making.

The purpose is not to identify the "sickest-looking" patient.

The purpose is to determine:

> **Who requires attention first, what intervention is required, and where that intervention should occur?**

---

# 29.7 Triage Is Dynamic

A patient initially classified as lower priority may deteriorate.

A patient initially classified as high priority may stabilise.

The leader must therefore expect triage categories to change.

A useful operational principle is:

> **Triage is a snapshot, not a permanent diagnosis.**

Reassessment should occur:

- after major interventions
- after changes in physiology
- when new information becomes available
- when resources change
- and whenever the clinical condition does not fit the existing priority.

---

# 29.8 Avoid Triage Drift

Triage drift occurs when a patient gradually receives more and more attention without a clear reassessment of whether that attention remains justified.

Examples include:

- repeatedly reassessing a stable patient while another patient deteriorates
- continuing extensive investigations in a lower-priority patient
- allowing one patient's family or social circumstances to disproportionately consume resources
- allowing a familiar patient to occupy a critical-care space indefinitely
- repeatedly discussing one case while new patients arrive.

The leader should periodically ask:

> "Is the current allocation of resources still appropriate?"

---

# 29.9 The Principle of the Greatest Overall Benefit

Mass-casualty care requires difficult prioritisation.

The leader should aim to maximise meaningful clinical benefit across the patient population.

This may require decisions such as:

- prioritising an immediately reversible life threat
- delaying a non-life-saving procedure
- moving a stable patient out of the resuscitation area
- transferring a patient to another hospital
- allocating a theatre to a patient with a time-critical intervention
- conserving limited blood products
- or postponing investigations that will not alter immediate management.

These decisions should be based on:

- clinical urgency
- reversibility
- time sensitivity
- resource requirements
- probability of meaningful benefit
- and the wider patient population.

They should not be based on:

- social status
- wealth
- nationality
- occupation
- familiarity
- pressure from relatives
- or personal preference.

---

# 29.10 Do Not Confuse Dramatic Appearance With Priority

Mass-casualty incidents generate emotionally powerful presentations.

Examples include:

- extensive external bleeding
- severe facial trauma
- open fractures
- dramatic burns
- crying children
- distressed relatives
- visible deformity
- or large wounds.

The most visually dramatic patient is not necessarily the highest priority.

The leader should continually return to physiology and time-critical pathology.

Ask:

> "What will kill this patient if we do not intervene now?"

and:

> "Which intervention will change the outcome?"

---

# 29.11 The Leader's Cognitive Position

The trauma leader should deliberately move between three levels of thinking.

### Level 1 — Patient

What is happening to this patient?

### Level 2 — Team

What is the team doing for this patient?

### Level 3 — System

What is happening to the entire incident?

A strong leader continuously cycles through all three.

A weak leader remains at Level 1.

---

# 29.12 The Danger of Becoming the Best Clinician in the Room

During a major incident, the most experienced clinician may naturally become involved in the sickest patient.

This can be useful.

It can also become dangerous.

If the leader personally performs every procedure, makes every decision, reviews every scan, and communicates every update, the wider system loses its coordinator.

The leader should therefore ask:

> "Am I adding more value by doing this procedure, or by coordinating the people who can do it?"

Delegation becomes more important as the number of patients increases.

---

# 29.13 Build Functional Teams

The leader should organise clinicians into functional groups.

Depending on the incident, these may include:

### Triage team

Responsible for rapid assessment and prioritisation.

### Resuscitation teams

Responsible for immediate life-saving interventions.

### Procedure team

Responsible for time-critical procedures where appropriate.

### Surgical coordination

Responsible for identifying patients requiring operative intervention.

### Imaging coordination

Responsible for prioritising imaging according to clinical need.

### Transfer coordination

Responsible for identifying patients who need movement to other facilities.

### Family and information coordination

Responsible for preventing relatives and information requests from overwhelming clinical teams.

The exact structure should be adapted to local resources.

---

# 29.14 One Leader Does Not Mean One Person Does Everything

The leader should distribute leadership.

A useful command principle is:

> **Centralise priorities, decentralise execution.**

The leader decides:

- what matters most
- what must happen now
- what can wait
- and where resources should go.

Individual teams decide:

- how to perform their assigned task
- which technical steps are required
- and when they need additional support.

This creates controlled autonomy.

---

# 29.15 Assign Tasks by Name

Mass-casualty environments generate noise.

Generic instructions are easily missed.

Instead of:

> "Someone get blood."

Say:

> "Ahmed, contact blood bank and bring the emergency blood pack to Resus 1."

Instead of:

> "Someone organise transfer."

Say:

> "Sara, coordinate transfer options for the unstable pelvic-trauma patient."

Then require confirmation:

> "Ahmed?"

> "Yes."

> "What are you doing?"

> "I'm contacting blood bank and bringing the emergency blood pack to Resus 1."

This is closed-loop communication applied to disaster medicine.

---

# 29.16 Establish a Shared Operational Picture

The leader should ensure that the team knows:

- how many patients are present
- where the critical patients are
- which resources are limited
- what interventions are underway
- what is expected next
- and what the current priorities are.

A simple verbal update may be:

> "We have twelve patients currently in the department. Three require immediate resuscitation. One requires urgent theatre. Blood is currently our limiting resource. Five additional patients are expected within the next twenty minutes. Our immediate priority is haemorrhage control and preserving resuscitation capacity."

This creates a shared mental model.

---

# 29.17 Use Regular Team Updates

Long periods without updates increase uncertainty.

Short, structured updates are preferable.

A useful format is:

### Situation

What is happening?

### Priorities

What matters most now?

### Resources

What is becoming limited?

### Risks

What could deteriorate next?

### Plan

What are we doing about it?

### Next update

When will we reassess?

For example:

> "Current situation: twelve patients, three critical. Priorities are haemorrhage control and airway management. Blood availability is becoming limited. Five further patients are expected. We are opening an additional resuscitation area and prioritising theatre access. Reassess in ten minutes."

---

# 29.18 Anticipate the Next Wave

One of the defining skills of disaster leadership is anticipating what has not happened yet.

Ask:

> "What happens if ten more patients arrive in five minutes?"

Then ask:

- Do we have space?
- Do we have staff?
- Do we have blood?
- Do we have airway equipment?
- Do we have monitoring?
- Do we have imaging capacity?
- Do we have theatre capacity?
- Do we have ICU capacity?
- Do we have transfer options?

The leader should act before the resource becomes exhausted.

---

# 29.19 Protect Capacity

Capacity is a clinical resource.

The leader must protect:

- resuscitation bays
- operating theatres
- ICU beds
- blood products
- imaging access
- staff
- equipment
- transport
- and communication channels.

A common error is consuming all available capacity on the first group of patients.

This leaves no reserve for patients who arrive later.

The leader should therefore deliberately maintain a reserve whenever possible.

---

# 29.20 The Reserve Principle

A useful question is:

> **"What capacity must I keep available for the next patient?"**

For example:

- Keep one resuscitation bay available.
- Keep airway equipment accessible.
- Maintain a blood-product reserve.
- Preserve a route for ambulance access.
- Maintain an available clinician who can respond to a sudden deterioration.

The exact reserve depends on the incident.

The principle remains:

> **Do not consume the entire system's capacity simply because it is available now.**

---

# 29.21 Resource Scarcity Changes Decision-Making

When resources are unlimited, many decisions are straightforward.

When resources become limited, the leader must prioritise.

Examples include:

- one available operating theatre
- one CT scanner
- limited blood products
- limited ventilators
- limited anaesthesia personnel
- limited ICU beds
- limited transport capacity.

The leader should identify the **bottleneck**.

Ask:

> "What resource is currently limiting the number of patients we can safely treat?"

Then concentrate leadership effort on that bottleneck.

---

# 29.22 Bottleneck Management

If the bottleneck is:

### Blood

Focus on:

- haemorrhage control
- blood conservation
- rapid surgical control
- appropriate transfusion
- communication with blood bank
- transfer planning.

### Theatre

Focus on:

- prioritising time-critical operations
- moving patients out of theatre
- coordinating surgical teams
- avoiding unnecessary theatre utilisation.

### CT

Focus on:

- scanning patients whose management will change as a result
- avoiding unnecessary imaging
- coordinating transport and return.

### ICU

Focus on:

- identifying patients who truly require ICU-level care
- step-down options
- transfer
- definitive treatment
- and early escalation.

### Staff

Focus on:

- delegation
- role clarity
- rest and rotation
- avoiding duplication
- and requesting additional personnel early.

---

# 29.23 Avoid Resource Hoarding

Resource scarcity can create defensive behaviour.

Teams may attempt to retain:

- equipment
- blood
- staff
- beds
- theatre time
- or imaging capacity.

The leader must ensure that resources are allocated according to patient need and overall incident priorities.

The principle is:

> **Resources belong to the response, not to individual teams.**

---

# 29.24 Major Haemorrhage in Mass-Casualty Incidents

Haemorrhage remains one of the most time-critical causes of preventable trauma death.

The leader must coordinate:

- recognition
- haemorrhage control
- major haemorrhage activation where appropriate
- blood-product availability
- surgical intervention
- interventional radiology where available
- warming
- calcium and coagulation management according to local protocol
- and definitive control.

However, in a mass-casualty incident, blood availability may itself become a limiting resource.

This reinforces the importance of:

> **rapid haemorrhage control rather than prolonged resuscitation without definitive control.**

---

# 29.25 Avoid Endless Resuscitation Without a Plan

A patient receiving blood, fluids, airway support, and repeated procedures may appear to be receiving intensive care.

But the leader must continually ask:

> "What is the definitive intervention?"

If the patient requires:

- surgery
- embolisation
- thoracotomy
- laparotomy
- vascular control
- or transfer,

the team should not lose sight of that objective.

Resuscitation is often a bridge to definitive control.

---

# 29.26 Operating Theatre Prioritisation

In a mass-casualty incident, theatre capacity may become one of the most important constraints.

Patients should be prioritised according to:

- immediacy of threat
- time sensitivity
- reversibility
- expected benefit
- resource requirements
- and available alternatives.

The leader should avoid allowing theatre decisions to become isolated from the wider incident.

A patient requiring a long operation may consume a resource that could potentially treat several other patients.

This does not mean that prolonged operations are inappropriate.

It means that their operational consequences must be recognised.

---

# 29.27 Imaging During Mass-Casualty Incidents

Imaging should support decision-making rather than become a bottleneck.

The leader should ask:

> "Will this investigation change what we do now?"

If the answer is no, the investigation may not deserve immediate priority.

This is particularly important when:

- CT access is limited
- patients are unstable
- transport is difficult
- multiple patients require imaging
- or definitive treatment is already indicated.

---

# 29.28 Transfer as a Clinical Intervention

Transfer is not merely an administrative activity.

Moving a patient to another facility may be the most appropriate clinical decision.

Transfer may be required because of:

- lack of theatre capacity
- lack of ICU capacity
- specialist expertise
- paediatric capability
- burns capability
- vascular surgery
- neurosurgery
- interventional radiology
- or overwhelming local demand.

The leader should consider transfer early rather than waiting until local capacity is exhausted.

---

# 29.29 Avoid the "Last Bed" Problem

A common operational failure is waiting until every resource is exhausted before initiating transfer.

By that point:

- transport may be unavailable
- receiving hospitals may be overwhelmed
- staff may be exhausted
- and the patient may be deteriorating.

Early transfer planning creates options.

The leader should ask:

> "If we continue at this rate, when will we exceed capacity?"

Then act before that point.

---

# 29.30 Communication With the Wider Hospital

The trauma team cannot function independently during a major incident.

Communication may be required with:

- emergency department leadership
- surgical teams
- anaesthesia
- ICU
- theatre
- radiology
- blood bank
- laboratory
- bed management
- infection prevention
- security
- portering
- ambulance services
- and hospital command.

Information should be:

- concise
- structured
- current
- actionable
- and directed to the appropriate recipient.

---

# 29.31 Communication Under Information Overload

Disaster incidents produce excessive information.

The leader should distinguish between:

### Information

Something that is known.

### Intelligence

Information that has been interpreted and has operational meaning.

### Action

The decision made because of that intelligence.

For example:

> "Ten ambulances are approaching."

is information.

> "We are likely to receive another ten patients within fifteen minutes."

is interpreted operational intelligence.

> "Open the additional treatment area and prepare five additional resuscitation spaces."

is action.

The leader's job is to convert information into useful action.

---

# 29.32 Avoid Communication Saturation

Too many messages can become as dangerous as too few.

The leader should identify:

- who needs to know
- what they need to know
- when they need to know it
- and what action is expected.

Not every team member needs every piece of information.

---

# 29.33 Use Structured Communication

Useful structures include:

### SBAR

- Situation
- Background
- Assessment
- Recommendation

### ATMIST

- Age
- Time
- Mechanism
- Injuries
- Signs
- Treatment

### MIST

- Mechanism
- Injuries
- Signs
- Treatment

The exact structure should follow local policy.

The important principle is:

> **Use a predictable structure when information must be transferred rapidly.**

---

# 29.34 Manage External Information

Major incidents attract:

- senior managers
- media
- police
- family members
- administrators
- and other stakeholders.

The clinical leader should not become the main communication channel for every external request.

Designate appropriate people to manage:

- media
- family information
- operational reporting
- hospital command
- and external agencies.

Protect the clinical team from unnecessary interruption.

---

# 29.35 Psychological Safety During Disaster Response

Mass-casualty incidents increase cognitive and emotional load.

Team members may:

- fear making mistakes
- hesitate to challenge senior clinicians
- become overwhelmed
- experience tunnel vision
- or become emotionally attached to individual patients.

The leader should explicitly create permission to speak.

For example:

> "If you see a safety problem, say it immediately."

or:

> "Anyone can stop the process if they believe there is an immediate threat to patient safety."

This is a core Human Factors intervention.

---

# 29.36 Encourage Challenge-Response

A team member may say:

> "I am concerned that this patient is deteriorating."

The leader should respond:

> "What are you seeing?"

Then:

> "What do you think we should do?"

This converts concern into useful information.

The leader should never punish appropriate escalation.

---

# 29.37 Avoid Authority Gradient Problems

In a crisis, junior staff may identify problems before senior staff do.

The leader should therefore deliberately flatten unnecessary hierarchy.

Useful phrases include:

> "If you see something I have missed, tell me."

> "I want challenge early, not after the event."

> "Speak up if the plan does not make sense."

This improves situational awareness.

---

# 29.38 Cognitive Bias During Mass-Casualty Events

The leader should remain alert to common cognitive errors.

### Anchoring

Becoming fixed on the initial diagnosis.

### Confirmation bias

Looking only for evidence supporting the current plan.

### Availability bias

Overestimating a diagnosis because it is memorable.

### Premature closure

Stopping assessment too early.

### Action bias

Doing something simply because action feels preferable to waiting.

### Omission bias

Failing to act because the intervention feels risky.

### Framing effect

Allowing the way information is presented to influence prioritisation.

### Normalcy bias

Assuming the situation is less serious than it actually is.

### Authority bias

Accepting a senior person's conclusion without adequate challenge.

The leader should periodically ask:

> "What else could explain this?"

and:

> "What information would change our current plan?"

---

# 29.39 Use Forced Reassessment

At defined intervals, deliberately pause.

Ask:

1. What has changed?
2. Which patient is now the highest priority?
3. What resource is becoming limiting?
4. What have we missed?
5. What is the next predictable problem?
6. What do we need to prepare now?

A short pause can prevent major cognitive drift.

---

# 29.40 The Ten-Second Leadership Scan

In a chaotic environment, the leader can rapidly scan:

### Patients

Who is dying?

### People

Who is doing what?

### Priorities

What matters most?

### Resources

What is running out?

### Risks

What could happen next?

### Plan

What needs to happen now?

This can be repeated every few minutes.

---

# 29.41 Managing the Physical Environment

The physical environment itself can become hazardous.

Potential problems include:

- overcrowding
- blocked corridors
- poor access to equipment
- oxygen limitations
- electrical hazards
- contamination
- inadequate lighting
- noise
- poor patient identification
- and uncontrolled movement of people.

The leader should ensure that clinical areas remain functional.

---

# 29.42 Zoning

Where possible, establish clearly defined zones.

Examples include:

- triage
- immediate resuscitation
- urgent treatment
- delayed treatment
- observation
- discharge
- transfer
- deceased patients
- and family information.

The exact terminology depends on the local major-incident plan.

The purpose is to prevent mixing of priorities.

---

# 29.43 Patient Identification

Large numbers of patients increase the risk of:

- wrong-patient treatment
- duplicate investigations
- lost documentation
- incorrect medication
- incorrect blood products
- and inappropriate transfer.

Use the institutional patient-identification system.

If formal identification is initially unavailable, follow the local major-incident identification process and ensure that temporary identifiers are reconciled as soon as possible.

---

# 29.44 Documentation Under Pressure

Documentation remains important.

However, documentation should not prevent life-saving care.

The leader should ensure that the team records, as far as practical:

- identity
- injuries
- observations
- interventions
- medications
- blood products
- procedures
- imaging
- destination
- and major decisions.

A concise record is better than an elaborate record that delays treatment.

---

# 29.45 Managing the Deceased

Mass-casualty incidents may result in multiple fatalities.

The handling of deceased patients should follow local:

- forensic procedures
- legal requirements
- identification processes
- mortuary arrangements
- and family-support systems.

The trauma leader should ensure that the management of deceased patients does not obstruct care of living patients.

---

# 29.46 Children in Mass-Casualty Incidents

Paediatric casualties create additional challenges.

The leader should anticipate:

- age-specific equipment
- weight-based medication
- paediatric airway expertise
- family separation
- safeguarding issues
- and the possibility that parents and children may arrive together.

A child may initially appear stable but deteriorate rapidly.

Appropriate paediatric expertise should therefore be requested early.

---

# 29.47 Pregnant Patients

Pregnancy creates additional clinical and logistical considerations.

The leader should ensure appropriate involvement of:

- obstetrics
- neonatal services where relevant
- anaesthesia
- and other specialists required by the patient's injuries.

The presence of pregnancy should not delay treatment of life-threatening maternal trauma.

---

# 29.48 Older and Frail Patients

Older patients may have:

- anticoagulant use
- significant comorbidity
- frailty
- limited physiological reserve
- and atypical presentations.

The leader should avoid assuming that apparently modest injury is clinically insignificant.

At the same time, treatment decisions should remain based on clinical need and expected benefit rather than age alone.

---

# 29.49 Patients With Disability or Communication Difficulties

Patients with:

- hearing impairment
- cognitive impairment
- language barriers
- communication disorders
- or physical disability

may require additional communication support.

The leader should ensure that communication barriers do not become clinical neglect.

Use interpreters and appropriate support mechanisms where available.

---

# 29.50 Chemical, Biological, Radiological, and Nuclear Incidents

Some mass-casualty incidents involve hazards that can create secondary casualties.

The trauma leader must recognise that:

> **The treatment environment itself may be hazardous.**

In such incidents:

- personal safety comes first
- appropriate PPE is required
- contamination control is essential
- decontamination may precede definitive treatment where appropriate
- exposure pathways must be considered
- and specialist teams should be involved early.

Do not allow contaminated patients to enter clean clinical areas without following the established emergency protocol.

---

# 29.51 The Secondary-Casualty Principle

The leader should continually ask:

> "Could our response create another casualty?"

Examples include:

- entering a hazardous environment
- handling contaminated clothing
- crowding around an unstable structure
- moving patients without appropriate protection
- ignoring fire or electrical hazards
- or allowing unprotected staff exposure.

A successful disaster response protects both patients and responders.

---

# 29.52 Security and Violence

Mass-casualty incidents may involve:

- violence
- crowding
- distressed relatives
- weapons
- hostile individuals
- or security threats.

The trauma leader should not attempt to personally manage security.

Security concerns should be escalated through the appropriate command structure.

The leader's responsibility is to ensure that the clinical team can continue functioning safely.

---

# 29.53 Staff Fatigue

Large incidents may continue for many hours.

Fatigue progressively reduces:

- attention
- working memory
- communication
- judgement
- procedural reliability
- and situational awareness.

The leader should therefore plan staff rotation before performance deteriorates.

---

# 29.54 The Leader Must Protect the Team

The leader should ensure access to:

- hydration
- food where appropriate
- rest
- rotation
- psychological support
- and relief from prolonged high-intensity tasks.

A tired team is a safety risk.

---

# 29.55 Avoid Hero Culture

Disaster medicine can reward the person who appears willing to work indefinitely.

This is dangerous.

A clinician who is exhausted may become:

- slower
- less accurate
- more irritable
- less communicative
- and more likely to make errors.

The correct leadership message is:

> **Sustainable performance is part of patient safety.**

---

# 29.56 Leadership Under Emotional Pressure

Mass-casualty incidents may involve:

- children
- multiple deaths
- severe disfigurement
- trapped patients
- colleagues among the casualties
- or emotionally distressed families.

The leader must acknowledge the emotional impact while maintaining operational function.

Useful internal discipline is:

> **Recognise emotion. Do not allow emotion to replace prioritisation.**

---

# 29.57 The Leader's Language

During crisis, language should become:

- short
- specific
- calm
- respectful
- and decisive.

Avoid:

> "Maybe someone could perhaps have a look at this patient."

Prefer:

> "Ahmed, assess Resus 3 now and report the airway and haemodynamic status."

Avoid:

> "We might need to think about theatre."

Prefer:

> "Theatre is now the priority. I want the surgical team and anaesthesia contacted immediately."

Clarity reduces cognitive load.

---

# 29.58 Managing Disagreement

Disagreement is inevitable.

The leader should encourage disagreement about:

- clinical assessment
- priorities
- resource allocation
- safety
- and operational assumptions.

Disagreement should be converted into structured discussion.

Useful sequence:

1. State the concern.
2. State the evidence.
3. State the potential consequence.
4. State the recommended action.
5. Decide.
6. Communicate the decision.

Example:

> "I am concerned that we are keeping this patient in Resus 1 despite stable physiology. Resus capacity is becoming limited and another critical patient is arriving. I recommend moving this patient to monitored treatment. Agreed?"

---

# 29.59 When to Change the Plan

A plan should change when:

- new patients arrive
- physiology changes
- new diagnostic information becomes available
- resources become constrained
- another hospital becomes available
- transport becomes available
- or the incident itself changes.

Changing the plan is not failure.

Refusing to change the plan despite new information is failure.

---

# 29.60 The Escalation Threshold

The leader should define what requires escalation.

Examples include:

- uncontrolled haemorrhage
- unexpected deterioration
- airway failure
- inability to access theatre
- blood-product shortage
- ICU capacity failure
- equipment failure
- security concerns
- contamination
- staff shortage
- or an expected second wave of casualties.

Early escalation is usually safer than delayed escalation.

---

# 29.61 "What Are We Missing?"

One of the most powerful questions in disaster leadership is:

> **"What are we missing?"**

Ask the team.

Do not answer it yourself immediately.

A junior clinician may identify:

- an overlooked patient
- a missing blood product
- an unrecognised deterioration
- an unsafe transfer
- or a resource bottleneck.

This simple question improves collective situational awareness.

---

# 29.62 The 360-Degree Review

At intervals, deliberately look beyond the current patient.

### Patients

Who is deteriorating?

### People

Who is overloaded?

### Process

Where is the system slowing?

### Resources

What is running out?

### Environment

What has become unsafe?

### Future

What is likely to happen next?

This creates a 360-degree operational picture.

---

# 29.63 The Transition From Response to Recovery

The incident will eventually move from immediate response toward recovery.

The leader should anticipate this transition.

Tasks may include:

- transferring patients
- reopening normal clinical pathways
- reconciling patient identities
- completing documentation
- returning equipment
- replenishing blood products
- restocking medication
- releasing staff
- and preparing for debriefing.

The end of the emergency phase is not the end of the clinical responsibility.

---

# 29.64 Reconciliation

After a major incident, the team should reconcile:

- patients
- investigations
- procedures
- medications
- blood products
- transfers
- admissions
- deaths
- and outstanding clinical tasks.

A patient should not disappear operationally simply because the immediate crisis has ended.

---

# 29.65 Debriefing After a Mass-Casualty Incident

Debriefing should distinguish between:

### Hot debrief

A brief discussion immediately after the event.

### Structured debrief

A more detailed review after the team has had time to recover and information has been gathered.

The purpose is not primarily to assign blame.

It is to identify:

- what happened
- what worked
- what failed
- what was difficult
- what should change
- and what needs follow-up.

---

# 29.66 Separate Performance Review From Blame

A system may fail even when individuals acted reasonably.

The leader should ask:

> "What conditions made this error possible?"

rather than:

> "Who is responsible?"

Individual accountability remains important where appropriate.

But improvement requires understanding:

- workload
- staffing
- equipment
- communication
- protocols
- training
- environmental factors
- and system design.

---

# 29.67 Lessons From the Incident

A useful post-incident review should identify:

### What should we continue?

What worked well?

### What should we stop?

What created unnecessary risk?

### What should we start?

What was missing?

### What should we change?

What requires system redesign?

### Who owns the action?

Every important improvement should have an identified owner.

---

# 29.68 Disaster Leadership Checklist

## Before the incident

Know:

- the major-incident plan
- the command structure
- the triage system
- the evacuation plan
- the decontamination pathway
- the blood-product pathway
- transfer arrangements
- paediatric resources
- specialist referral pathways
- and emergency contact systems.

---

## At recognition

Ask:

- Is this a major incident?
- Is the environment safe?
- How many patients are expected?
- What is the likely mechanism?
- Are hazardous materials involved?
- What resources will become limiting?

Activate the appropriate response early.

---

## During the first wave

Establish:

- command
- triage
- treatment zones
- resuscitation teams
- communication channels
- resource tracking
- and transfer planning.

---

## During ongoing response

Continuously reassess:

- patient priorities
- team workload
- resource availability
- capacity
- safety
- incoming patients
- and the next predictable problem.

---

## Before resources are exhausted

Ask:

> "What will become the bottleneck next?"

Then act.

---

## During prolonged operations

Protect:

- staff
- communication
- blood supply
- theatre capacity
- critical-care capacity
- and reserve treatment space.

---

## During recovery

Ensure:

- patient reconciliation
- transfer completion
- documentation
- equipment replenishment
- blood-stock recovery
- staff relief
- and structured debriefing.

# 29.69 Practical Leadership Phrases

### Establishing command

> "I am leading the clinical response. Please direct major clinical concerns and resource issues to me."

### Establishing priorities

> "Our immediate priorities are haemorrhage control, airway management, and preserving resuscitation capacity."

### Assigning a task

> "Ahmed, contact blood bank and report current availability."

### Closing the loop

> "Ahmed?"

> "Contacting blood bank and reporting availability."

> "Good."

### Reassessing

> "Stop for ten seconds. What has changed?"

### Identifying the bottleneck

> "What resource is currently limiting us?"

### Anticipating

> "We are expecting another wave. What do we need before they arrive?"

### Encouraging challenge

> "If you think the plan is unsafe, say so now."

### Managing disagreement

> "State your concern, your evidence, and what you recommend."

### Protecting capacity

> "We need to keep one resuscitation space available for the next critical patient."

### Escalating

> "Our current capacity will not be sufficient. We need additional support now."

### Transitioning

> "The immediate response is stabilising. We are now moving toward transfer, reconciliation, and recovery."

# 29.70 Common Leadership Failures

## Failure 1 — Treating every patient as a single-patient trauma

### Why it fails

The system becomes overwhelmed.

### Better approach

Think simultaneously at patient, team, and system level.

---

## Failure 2 — Becoming trapped in one resuscitation

### Why it fails

The wider incident loses coordination.

### Better approach

Delegate technical tasks and maintain operational awareness.

---

## Failure 3 — Waiting too long to activate the major-incident response

### Why it fails

Resources are mobilised after capacity is already exceeded.

### Better approach

Escalate early when the incident exceeds normal operating capacity.

---

## Failure 4 — Using resources without considering future demand

### Why it fails

The next wave arrives to an exhausted system.

### Better approach

Protect reserve capacity.

---

## Failure 5 — Allowing one patient to consume disproportionate resources

### Why it fails

Population-level benefit is reduced.

### Better approach

Regularly reassess resource allocation.

---

## Failure 6 — Poor role allocation

### Why it fails

Tasks are duplicated or missed.

### Better approach

Use explicit roles and named task allocation.

---

## Failure 7 — Communication overload

### Why it fails

Critical information is lost in noise.

### Better approach

Use short, structured, actionable communication.

---

## Failure 8 — Failure to anticipate

### Why it fails

The system reacts after resources are exhausted.

### Better approach

Predict the next bottleneck and act early.

---

## Failure 9 — Ignoring staff fatigue

### Why it fails

Error rates increase.

### Better approach

Plan rotation and relief.

---

## Failure 10 — Treating disaster leadership as heroism

### Why it fails

Individual effort cannot compensate for system failure.

### Better approach

Build resilient teams and systems.

---

# 29.71 The Trauma Leader's Mental Model in a Mass-Casualty Incident

A useful mental model is:

> **See → Sort → Stabilise → Allocate → Anticipate → Reassess → Adapt**

### See

Understand the incident.

### Sort

Prioritise patients.

### Stabilise

Perform immediate life-saving interventions.

### Allocate

Direct resources where they produce the greatest benefit.

### Anticipate

Predict the next problem.

### Reassess

Look for changes.

### Adapt

Change the plan when circumstances change.

This cycle should continue throughout the incident.

---

# 29.72 The Five Questions That Keep the Leader Out of the Tunnel

At regular intervals ask:

1. **Who is dying?**
2. **What must happen now?**
3. **What resource is limiting us?**
4. **What is likely to happen next?**
5. **What am I missing?**

These questions are simple enough to use under extreme pressure.

---

# 29.73 The Final Leadership Principle

Mass-casualty trauma leadership is not about controlling every detail.

It is about creating a system in which the right things happen in the right order.

The leader must:

- establish command
- create clarity
- prioritise patients
- allocate resources
- protect capacity
- anticipate deterioration
- coordinate teams
- communicate clearly
- encourage challenge
- recognise limitations
- escalate early
- protect staff
- and continuously reassess.

The leader should remain calm enough to think while everyone else is busy acting.

The goal is not to make every decision personally.

The goal is to ensure that the team makes the **right decisions at the right time**.

---

# 29.74 Final Take-Home Messages

> **A mass-casualty incident is not simply a large trauma patient. It is a different clinical and operational problem.**

> **The leader must move continuously between the individual patient, the team, and the whole system.**

> **Triage is dynamic and must be repeatedly reassessed.**

> **The most dramatic patient is not necessarily the highest priority.**

> **Resources must be allocated according to clinical need and overall benefit.**

> **Protecting future capacity is part of current patient care.**

> **Identify the bottleneck before it becomes a crisis.**

> **Anticipate the next wave rather than waiting for it to arrive.**

> **Centralise priorities and decentralise execution.**

> **Use names, closed-loop communication, and structured updates.**

> **Encourage junior team members to challenge unsafe decisions.**

> **Protect staff from fatigue and psychological overload.**

> **Transfer is a clinical strategy when local capacity is insufficient.**

> **A safe response protects both patients and responders.**

> **The leader's job is not to do everything. The leader's job is to make sure the system does what matters most.**

# Conclusion

The defining skill of the trauma team leader during a mass-casualty or disaster incident is not speed alone.

It is **controlled prioritisation under uncertainty**.

The leader must resist tunnel vision, maintain a shared operational picture, allocate finite resources deliberately, anticipate the next problem, and continuously adapt the plan as the incident evolves.

The best disaster leaders do not simply manage the patients in front of them.

They manage the **system that will care for the patients who are already here, the patients who are deteriorating, and the patients who have not yet arrived.**

> **In a major incident, leadership means preserving the ability to keep caring for patients when the system is under maximum strain.**
