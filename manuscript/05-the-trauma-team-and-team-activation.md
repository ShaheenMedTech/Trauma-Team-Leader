# Chapter 5 — Trauma Team Activation

## Clinical Scenario

It is 02:15.

The emergency department receives a pre-alert:

> "Motorcycle collision. Male, approximately 30 years old. Thrown from the motorcycle. GCS 13. Respiratory rate 30. Systolic blood pressure 82 mmHg. Suspected pelvic injury. Ongoing external bleeding from the left leg. ETA six minutes."

The emergency department has a choice.

The team could wait until the patient arrives, perform a rapid assessment, and then call additional personnel.

Or the hospital can activate the trauma response immediately.

The difference is not simply administrative.

It is a decision about **time, risk, resources, and readiness**.

The trauma team leader should understand that activation is not the same as diagnosis.

The purpose of activation is to ensure that the appropriate people, equipment, blood products, and supporting services are ready **before critical interventions are required**.

A patient may ultimately have no major injury despite meeting activation criteria.

That does not necessarily mean the activation was inappropriate.

Conversely, a patient with a seemingly reassuring mechanism may deteriorate rapidly and require escalation after arrival.

The trauma team leader must therefore treat activation criteria as a **safety system**, not as a substitute for clinical judgment.

---

# Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain the purpose of trauma team activation.
2. Distinguish trauma-system triage from hospital trauma-team activation.
3. Recognise clinical features that should trigger an urgent trauma response.
4. Understand full, modified, and specialty-specific trauma activations.
5. Identify when additional services should be mobilised.
6. Use pre-arrival information to anticipate clinical needs.
7. Avoid under-triage and inappropriate delays in activation.
8. Recognise situations in which over-triage may occur.
9. Reassess the activation level after patient arrival.
10. Lead escalation and de-escalation safely.
11. Integrate blood bank, operating theatre, interventional radiology, anaesthesia, and specialist services into the activation process.
12. Apply activation principles to difficult and ambiguous cases.

# 1. The Trauma Team as a Clinical System

A trauma team is a multidisciplinary group of healthcare professionals assembled to assess and treat patients with major or potentially life-threatening trauma.

Major trauma frequently involves:

- multiple injured organ systems
- rapidly changing physiology
- simultaneous diagnostic and therapeutic requirements
- competing priorities
- time-critical interventions
- multiple specialist disciplines
- substantial information uncertainty

No single clinician can reliably perform all required functions simultaneously.

The trauma team therefore distributes work and enables parallel processing.

While one clinician manages the airway:

- another assesses circulation
- another establishes vascular access
- another obtains blood samples
- another prepares blood products
- another performs neurological assessment
- another documents events
- the leader integrates the information

The purpose of the team is not simply to increase the number of people in the room.

Its purpose is to increase the team's ability to:

> **recognise threats, intervene rapidly, reassess continuously and coordinate definitive care.**

A high-performing trauma team should therefore be understood as a structured clinical system rather than a collection of individuals.

---

# 2. Why Trauma Team Activation Exists

Major trauma is time-critical.

Some patients require interventions within minutes of arrival:

- haemorrhage control
- airway intervention
- assisted ventilation
- chest decompression
- pelvic stabilisation
- blood product administration
- emergency surgery
- interventional radiology
- neurosurgical intervention
- orthopaedic intervention
- paediatric expertise
- obstetric involvement
- burn management

The required personnel may not normally be physically present in the resuscitation room.

Activation creates a mechanism for mobilising them.

The objective is:

> **The right team, with the right resources, in the right place, before the patient arrives.**

NICE recommends that major trauma centres consider a tiered team response. This may include a standard multispecialty trauma team or a standard team with additional specialist involvement and mobilisation of supporting services such as transfusion, interventional radiology, and surgery. 

Therefore, trauma activation should not be viewed as a single binary event.

It is better understood as a **graded mobilisation system**.

---

# 3. Activation Is a System, Not a Telephone Call

A trauma activation pathway usually contains several linked processes:

1. Recognition of a potentially severely injured patient.
2. Transmission of a pre-alert.
3. Selection of the activation level.
4. Notification of the trauma team.
5. Preparation of the resuscitation environment.
6. Mobilisation of additional resources.
7. Assignment of leadership and roles.
8. Reception of the patient.
9. Immediate assessment and resuscitation.
10. Reassessment of the initial activation level.
11. Escalation or de-escalation.
12. Documentation and subsequent performance review.

A weak system may have excellent clinicians but still perform poorly because information is delayed.

A strong system reduces unnecessary delay before the patient reaches the resuscitation room.

---

# 4. Trauma Activation Versus Field Triage

These concepts are related but not identical.

## Field Triage

Field triage helps determine:

- whether the patient is likely to have serious injury
- the appropriate destination
- whether transport to a higher-level trauma centre is appropriate

The 2021 ACS National Guideline for the Field Triage of Injured Patients is specifically designed to support prehospital destination decisions. ACS explicitly states that these guidelines should not be used directly as hospital trauma-team activation criteria. 

## Hospital Trauma-Team Activation

Hospital activation answers a different question:

> **What level of hospital response is required for this patient right now?**

The hospital may receive:

- a patient already identified as major trauma
- an unstable patient without a dramatic mechanism
- a patient with isolated but time-critical injury
- a patient whose severity becomes apparent only after arrival

The two systems should communicate with each other, but they should not be treated as interchangeable.

---

# 5. The Principle of Anticipation

The trauma team leader should ask:

> "What might this patient need in the first five minutes?"

Then:

> "What might this patient need in the next thirty minutes?"

For example:

### Patient A

High-speed collision, hypotension, suspected pelvic injury.

Possible needs:

- major haemorrhage pathway
- blood products
- pelvic binder
- massive transfusion capability
- trauma surgeon
- anaesthesia
- operating theatre
- interventional radiology

### Patient B

Penetrating neck injury with airway bleeding.

Possible needs:

- anaesthesia
- airway equipment
- surgical airway equipment
- ENT or trauma surgery
- operating theatre
- blood products

### Patient C

Severe head injury with GCS 6.

Possible needs:

- airway team
- anaesthesia
- neurosurgery
- CT
- blood products if indicated
- critical care

The activation decision should therefore be based on **anticipated clinical needs**, not simply on the mechanism of injury.

---

# 6. Activation Criteria

Every trauma system should have locally agreed activation criteria.

These criteria should be:

- clearly defined
- accessible to staff
- regularly reviewed
- based on evidence and local resources
- linked to a defined response
- understandable to prehospital and hospital clinicians

The exact criteria vary between trauma systems.

There is no single universal list that should simply be copied into every hospital.

However, common triggers include:

## Physiological compromise

Examples include:

- hypotension
- significant tachycardia
- abnormal respiratory rate
- respiratory distress
- hypoxaemia
- altered mental status
- reduced GCS
- signs of shock
- peri-arrest physiology
- requirement for advanced airway or ventilatory support

Physiological deterioration should generally carry greater weight than mechanism alone.

---

# 7. High-Risk Injury Patterns

Certain injury patterns should prompt a high level of concern.

Examples include:

- penetrating injury to the head, neck, torso, or proximal extremity
- suspected major vascular injury
- suspected pelvic fracture with haemodynamic instability
- flail chest
- major chest injury
- suspected traumatic brain injury with reduced consciousness
- spinal injury with neurological deficit
- mangled or pulseless extremity
- traumatic amputation
- uncontrolled external haemorrhage
- major burns associated with trauma
- multiple proximal long-bone fractures

These examples should be interpreted according to local activation policy.

The presence of one feature should not automatically replace clinical judgment.

---

# 8. Mechanism of Injury

Mechanism remains useful.

However:

> **Mechanism is a risk marker, not a diagnosis.**

Examples of concerning mechanisms include:

- high-speed road traffic collision
- occupant ejection
- significant vehicle intrusion
- death in the same passenger compartment
- pedestrian struck by a vehicle
- motorcycle collision with significant impact
- significant fall
- crush injury
- high-energy penetrating trauma
- blast injury

Mechanism should raise suspicion, but it should not be used in isolation to determine severity.

A patient with a dramatic mechanism may have limited injury.

A patient with a seemingly minor mechanism may have catastrophic injury.

---

# 9. Special Populations

Some patients require a lower threshold for escalation because their physiology or injury pattern may be difficult to interpret.

Examples include:

- children
- older adults
- pregnant patients
- patients taking anticoagulants
- patients with significant comorbidity
- patients with communication difficulties
- patients with cognitive impairment
- patients with intoxication
- patients with burns
- patients with major psychiatric or behavioural barriers
- patients with limited physiological reserve

The trauma team leader should ask:

> "Could this patient's apparent stability be misleading?"

---

# 10. Tiered Trauma Activation

A useful trauma system may employ different levels of activation.

For example:

## Level 1 — Full Trauma Team Activation

Used when there is a high probability of immediate life-threatening injury or need for multiple simultaneous interventions.

Potential team members may include:

- trauma team leader
- emergency physician
- trauma surgeon
- anaesthetist
- airway clinician
- emergency nurses
- radiographer
- healthcare assistants
- blood bank/transfusion support
- operating theatre team
- other specialists according to injury pattern

The exact composition should follow local policy.

---

## Level 2 — Modified Trauma Activation

Used when serious injury is suspected but a full multidisciplinary response may not initially be necessary.

Possible examples:

- stable penetrating injury
- isolated serious limb injury
- moderate traumatic brain injury
- suspected spinal injury without physiological instability
- significant burns
- isolated chest injury

The team can then escalate rapidly if the patient deteriorates.

---

## Specialty-Specific Activation

Some situations require immediate mobilisation of a particular service.

Examples include:

- neurosurgical activation
- vascular surgery
- cardiothoracic surgery
- orthopaedics
- obstetrics
- paediatrics
- burns service
- interventional radiology

The principle is:

> **Activate the resources required by the patient, not simply the resources traditionally associated with the trauma team.**

---

# 11. The Trauma Team Leader's Role Before Arrival

Once activated, the trauma team leader should not simply wait for the patient.

The leader should establish:

### 1. What is coming?

- age
- sex
- mechanism
- number of patients
- estimated time of arrival

### 2. How sick is the patient?

- airway
- breathing
- circulation
- neurological status
- haemorrhage
- interventions already performed

### 3. What is likely to kill the patient first?

Think:

> **What must we be ready to treat immediately?**

### 4. What resources might be required?

- blood
- airway equipment
- chest drains
- pelvic binder
- ultrasound
- radiography
- CT
- operating theatre
- interventional radiology
- specialist teams

### 5. What is the contingency plan?

Ask:

> "If the patient arrives worse than described, what will we do?"

---

# 12. The Pre-Alert

A useful pre-alert should be concise and structured.

One practical format is:

### ATMIST

**A — Age**

**T — Time**

**M — Mechanism**

**I — Injuries suspected**

**S — Signs**

**T — Treatment given**

The receiving team should listen specifically for:

- physiological instability
- airway compromise
- active haemorrhage
- altered consciousness
- interventions already performed
- estimated arrival time

---

# 13. Do Not Let the Pre-Alert Become Noise

Pre-alert information can become ineffective when too many details are transmitted without prioritisation.

The trauma team leader should identify the critical information.

For example:

> "Thirty-year-old male, high-speed motorcycle collision, approximately six minutes away. GCS 13, systolic pressure 82, active leg haemorrhage and suspected pelvic injury. Tourniquet applied, two large-bore IVs, no blood yet."

This immediately suggests:

- major trauma activation
- haemorrhage preparation
- blood availability
- pelvic injury management
- anaesthesia readiness
- surgical involvement

The leader does not need the entire history before activating the team.

---

# 14. The Five-Minute Preparation

During the final minutes before arrival, the team should prepare the room.

## Airway

Check:

- suction
- oxygen
- bag-mask equipment
- airway devices
- intubation equipment
- difficult-airway equipment
- capnography
- surgical airway equipment

## Breathing

Prepare:

- oxygen delivery
- monitoring
- chest decompression equipment
- chest drains
- ultrasound if available

## Circulation

Prepare:

- vascular access equipment
- rapid infuser
- blood warmers
- haemorrhage control equipment
- pelvic binder
- tourniquets
- haemostatic dressings
- blood products according to local protocol

## Disability

Prepare:

- neurological assessment tools
- glucose measurement
- appropriate sedation and airway medications
- temperature monitoring

## Exposure

Prepare:

- trauma shears
- warming equipment
- blankets
- fluid warming
- temperature control

---

# 15. Blood Bank Activation

If major haemorrhage is suspected, the trauma team leader should ensure that the appropriate haemorrhage pathway is activated early.

The exact process is institution-specific.

Depending on local protocol, this may involve:

- notifying the blood bank
- activating a massive haemorrhage protocol
- preparing emergency blood
- arranging rapid blood product delivery
- notifying transfusion laboratory staff
- preparing a blood warmer
- arranging laboratory monitoring

The key principle is:

> **Do not wait for profound physiological collapse before preparing for major haemorrhage when the clinical picture already indicates a high risk.**

NICE recommends that major trauma systems provide immediate access to haemorrhage-control pathways and supports a tiered trauma response with mobilisation of transfusion and other specialist services when required. 

---

# 16. Operating Theatre and Interventional Radiology

Activation may need to extend beyond the emergency department.

A patient with suspected uncontrolled haemorrhage may require:

- emergency surgery
- interventional radiology
- hybrid theatre
- damage-control surgery

The trauma team leader should therefore ask early:

> "If resuscitation fails, where is this patient going?"

The answer should not be discovered after twenty minutes of unsuccessful resuscitation.

It should be anticipated.

---

# 17. Activation Does Not Mean Every Resource Must Arrive

A common error is to interpret activation as a command for every available specialist to attend every patient.

This can create:

- overcrowding
- confusion
- role duplication
- communication failures
- unnecessary resource use
- alarm fatigue

The better principle is:

> **Mobilise what the patient may need, and escalate rapidly when new information changes the risk.**

This is one reason tiered activation systems are useful.

---

# 18. Under-Triage

Under-triage occurs when a patient who requires a higher level of trauma response does not receive it.

Potential consequences include:

- delayed airway management
- delayed haemorrhage control
- delayed blood products
- delayed specialist intervention
- delayed theatre access
- delayed transfer to a trauma centre
- increased risk of preventable deterioration

The trauma team leader should be particularly cautious when:

- physiology is abnormal
- the patient is deteriorating
- there is uncontrolled haemorrhage
- the injury pattern is unclear
- the patient cannot provide reliable history
- multiple body regions may be injured

---

# 19. Over-Triage

Over-triage occurs when the trauma response is activated for a patient who ultimately does not have severe injury.

Some degree of over-triage is expected in trauma systems.

The alternative—attempting to eliminate all unnecessary activations—may increase under-triage.

The goal is therefore not:

> "Never activate unnecessarily."

The goal is:

> **Maintain an acceptable balance between under-triage and over-triage.**

ACS emphasises that trauma activation criteria should be evidence-based and regularly reassessed for effectiveness. It also recognises that some patients who meet activation criteria will ultimately not have serious injuries. 

---

# 20. The Activation Decision Should Be Reversible

Activation is not a one-way decision.

The team leader may:

1. activate the team,
2. receive new information,
3. reassess the risk,
4. escalate the response,
5. maintain the response,
6. or safely de-escalate.

This flexibility is important.

For example:

> "The patient was initially reported as hypotensive with suspected pelvic injury. On arrival, blood pressure is 125/80, examination suggests an isolated forearm fracture, and there is no evidence of major haemorrhage."

The leader may reduce the response after appropriate assessment.

Conversely:

> "The patient was reported as stable. On arrival, they are pale, hypotensive, confused, and have active abdominal bleeding."

The activation level must immediately escalate.

---

# 21. Escalation After Arrival

The trauma team leader should have a low threshold for escalating the response when new information appears.

Triggers may include:

- worsening haemodynamics
- increasing oxygen requirement
- failed airway
- worsening neurological status
- uncontrolled bleeding
- unexpected penetrating injury
- suspected major vascular injury
- positive FAST with instability
- severe pelvic injury
- evidence of major chest injury
- unexpected burns or inhalation injury
- deterioration during transfer to CT

A useful command is:

> "This is now a full trauma response. I need anaesthesia, surgery, blood bank, and theatre activated."

The escalation should be explicit.

---

# 22. De-Escalation

De-escalation should also be explicit.

For example:

> "The patient is haemodynamically stable with no evidence of major injury. We are standing down the full trauma activation. Continue standard emergency assessment."

This prevents:

- unnecessary staff remaining in the room
- unclear leadership
- duplication of work
- inappropriate resource utilisation

However:

> **De-escalation should never occur simply because the room is busy or because the activation is inconvenient.**

Clinical risk determines the response.

---

# 23. Multiple Casualties

A major trauma activation system must also function when there is more than one patient.

The leader should ask:

- How many patients are coming?
- How many are critically injured?
- What are their estimated arrival times?
- Are additional resuscitation rooms required?
- Is another trauma team available?
- Is the hospital entering a major incident response?
- Are blood products sufficient?
- Is theatre capacity available?
- Does the regional trauma system need notification?

A standard trauma activation pathway may be insufficient during a mass-casualty incident.

Mass-casualty triage requires a separate system.

The ACS field triage guidelines explicitly state that they are not designed for mass-casualty incidents. 

---

# 24. Paediatric Trauma Activation

Children may require:

- paediatric expertise
- age-appropriate equipment
- weight-based medication
- paediatric airway expertise
- paediatric blood products or protocols
- specialist imaging considerations
- safeguarding assessment

NICE recommends that major trauma centres have a paediatric trauma team available immediately for children with major trauma. 

The activation pathway should therefore identify children early.

A paediatric patient should not arrive before the team discovers that the equipment and personnel are inappropriate.

---

# 25. Trauma in Pregnancy

Pregnancy introduces additional considerations.

The activation process may require:

- obstetric involvement
- neonatal support depending on gestation
- awareness of altered maternal physiology
- consideration of fetal assessment
- preparation for emergency delivery when clinically indicated

The maternal trauma response remains the priority.

The activation system should ensure that obstetric expertise is mobilised when required rather than waiting for deterioration.

---

# 26. Penetrating Trauma

Penetrating trauma may require a different activation pattern from blunt trauma.

Potential concerns include:

- airway injury
- major vascular injury
- cardiac injury
- thoracic injury
- abdominal injury
- pelvic injury
- occult bleeding

The activation should reflect the anatomical region and likely intervention.

For example:

> Penetrating neck injury + expanding haematoma + airway symptoms

should trigger a different specialist response from:

> isolated superficial extremity stab wound in a stable patient.

The word "penetrating" alone does not determine the exact activation level.

---

# 27. The Trauma Team Leader Should Not Be the Sole Source of Information

Activation should be supported by multiple information channels.

Useful sources include:

- EMS pre-alert
- ambulance control
- referring hospital
- police or fire service
- nursing triage
- imaging
- laboratory results
- electronic records
- bedside assessment

If information conflicts, the leader should clarify rather than assume.

For example:

> "EMS reports systolic BP 80, but triage says 120."

The appropriate response is not to choose the reassuring number.

Ask:

> "What was the patient's blood pressure immediately before arrival, and what interventions occurred?"

---

# 28. Activation and Closed-Loop Communication

Activation commands should be closed-loop.

Instead of:

> "Someone call blood bank."

Use:

> "Sarah, call the blood bank and activate the major haemorrhage pathway."

Then:

> "Blood bank activated."

Then:

> "Confirmed. Thank you."

This creates accountability.

The same principle applies to:

- theatre
- anaesthesia
- radiology
- interventional radiology
- specialist teams
- transfer coordination

---

Before the patient arrives, ask:

### Physiology

- Heart rate?
- Respiratory status?
- GCS?
- Oxygenation?
- Evidence of shock?

### Injury

- Airway?
- Chest?
- Abdomen?
- Pelvis?
- Head?
- Spine?
- Limbs?
- Major haemorrhage?

### Resources

- Full or modified activation?
- Anaesthesia?
- Surgery?
- Blood bank?
- Theatre?
- Interventional radiology?
- Neurosurgery?

### Environment

- Equipment checked?
- Blood available?
- Warming measures ready?
- Imaging pathway ready?

### Contingency

- What if the patient arrives worse?
- Where will the patient go after resuscitation?
- Who will make the next decision?

# 30. Case-Based Discussion

## Case 1 — Obvious Full Activation

A 24-year-old male is involved in a high-speed collision.

Pre-alert:

- GCS 7
- systolic BP 70 mmHg
- respiratory distress
- suspected pelvic fracture
- ongoing external haemorrhage

### Questions

1. Should the trauma team be activated?
2. What additional services should be considered?
3. What equipment should be prepared?
4. Should blood products be anticipated?
5. Where might the patient go after initial resuscitation?

### Leadership approach

This patient requires a high-level trauma response.

The leader should anticipate:

- airway intervention
- haemorrhage control
- blood products
- pelvic management
- operative or interventional haemorrhage control
- critical care

The leader should not wait for the patient's arrival before mobilising critical resources.

---

# 31. Case 2 — Dramatic Mechanism, Stable Patient

A 28-year-old patient is involved in a high-speed collision.

The patient is:

- alert
- speaking normally
- BP 130/80
- HR 88
- RR 18
- SpO₂ 99%
- no obvious major injury

### Question

Should this automatically result in full trauma activation?

Not necessarily.

Mechanism is important, but activation should incorporate:

- physiology
- injury pattern
- clinical examination
- prehospital assessment
- local activation criteria

The patient may require a trauma response, but the level should reflect the actual risk.

---

# 32. Case 3 — Apparently Minor Mechanism

An 82-year-old patient falls from standing height.

The patient takes anticoagulant medication.

On arrival:

- GCS 14
- BP 105/70
- HR 104
- confused
- scalp swelling

The mechanism may appear minor.

The patient may nevertheless have significant injury.

The leader should avoid anchoring on the phrase:

> "Only a fall from standing."

Age, medication, physiological reserve, and neurological findings may substantially change the risk.

---

# 33. Case 4 — Stable Penetrating Injury

A patient presents with a single stab wound to the forearm.

The patient is:

- haemodynamically stable
- alert
- no active major bleeding
- distal perfusion intact

A full trauma activation may not be necessary.

However, the clinician must still assess:

- vascular injury
- tendon injury
- nerve injury
- compartment syndrome
- retained foreign body
- associated injuries

Activation should match the anticipated clinical need.

---

# 34. Case 5 — The Activation That Must Escalate

A patient arrives under a modified activation.

Initially:

- BP 118/75
- HR 98
- GCS 15

During assessment:

- BP falls to 82/50
- HR increases to 130
- abdomen becomes distended
- FAST is positive

The leader should immediately escalate.

A useful command is:

> "This patient is now haemodynamically unstable with suspected intra-abdominal haemorrhage. Activate the major haemorrhage pathway and notify surgery and theatre."

The initial activation level does not constrain the subsequent response.

---

# 35. Common Pitfalls

## Pitfall 1 — Waiting for the Patient to Arrive

This wastes the pre-arrival period.

### Better approach

Use the pre-alert to prepare the team and resources.

---

## Pitfall 2 — Using Mechanism Alone

A dramatic mechanism can create unnecessary activation.

A seemingly minor mechanism can create dangerous reassurance.

### Better approach

Integrate:

- mechanism
- physiology
- injury pattern
- patient factors
- clinical judgment

---

## Pitfall 3 — Delaying Activation Until Diagnosis Is Certain

Activation exists because diagnosis is often uncertain.

### Better approach

Activate based on anticipated risk.

---

## Pitfall 4 — Treating Activation as Binary

Full activation versus no activation is often an oversimplification.

### Better approach

Use tiered activation where supported by the local system.

---

## Pitfall 5 — Failing to Escalate

A patient may deteriorate after arrival.

### Better approach

Continuously reassess the activation level.

---

## Pitfall 6 — Failing to De-Escalate

An unnecessarily large team may create confusion.

### Better approach

Stand down clearly when clinically appropriate.

---

## Pitfall 7 — Forgetting Supporting Services

The emergency department may be ready while:

- blood bank
- theatre
- radiology
- interventional radiology
- intensive care

are unaware.

### Better approach

Think beyond the resuscitation room.

---

## Pitfall 8 — Unclear Responsibility

Everyone assumes someone else has made the call.

### Better approach

Assign named tasks and use closed-loop communication.

---

# 36. Performance Improvement

Trauma activation should be reviewed as part of the trauma service's performance-improvement process.

Useful questions include:

### Under-Triage

- Which patients were not activated but subsequently required major intervention?
- Were there delays?
- Were activation criteria followed?
- Was the pre-alert adequate?

### Over-Triage

- Which activations resulted in no significant injury?
- Was the activation appropriate based on information available at the time?
- Could the activation criteria be improved?

### Process

- Did the team arrive on time?
- Was the trauma leader present?
- Was blood available when required?
- Were theatre and specialist services notified appropriately?
- Were roles clear?
- Were communication failures identified?

ACS trauma standards emphasise structured trauma-system readiness and performance improvement, while trauma activation criteria should be reviewed regularly for effectiveness. 

---

# 37. The Activation System Should Be Locally Owned

A textbook cannot provide a universal activation protocol for every hospital.

The final activation criteria should reflect:

- trauma centre designation
- available specialists
- operating theatre access
- interventional radiology availability
- blood bank capability
- paediatric resources
- transfer pathways
- regional trauma network
- staffing model
- local governance

The practical rule is:

> **Use evidence to design the system, but use local governance to define the operational pathway.**

---

# 38. Role of the Trauma Team Leader

The trauma team leader should:

1. Recognise the need for activation.
2. Select the appropriate response level.
3. Communicate the activation clearly.
4. Ensure the resuscitation room is prepared.
5. Anticipate likely interventions.
6. Mobilise additional resources.
7. Confirm that critical tasks have been completed.
8. Reassess the patient continuously.
9. Escalate when required.
10. De-escalate when safe.
11. Coordinate disposition planning.
12. Participate in review of activation performance.

The leader therefore manages **risk before diagnosis is complete**.

---

# 40. Key Takeaways

- Trauma activation is a **readiness system**, not merely a notification.
- The purpose is to have the appropriate team and resources available before critical interventions are required.
- Field triage and hospital trauma-team activation are related but distinct processes.
- Activation criteria should be evidence-based and locally defined.
- Physiological compromise and high-risk injury patterns should strongly influence activation.
- Mechanism is useful but should not be used in isolation.
- Tiered activation can allow resources to match clinical risk.
- The trauma team leader should anticipate likely interventions before patient arrival.
- Blood bank, theatre, interventional radiology, and specialist services may need early mobilisation.
- Activation must be continuously reassessed.
- Escalation should be explicit.
- De-escalation should also be explicit.
- Some degree of over-triage is preferable to unsafe under-triage.
- Activation performance should be reviewed through trauma-system quality improvement.
- The trauma team leader's role is to make sure that the system is ready **before the patient needs it**.
